# Blind reading protocols

A blind reading test shows an image to four chat models — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and DeepSeek — with no context, and asks what they see. The models play the part of AI readers who know nothing about the intent. The questions changed over the month, so every test names the set it used. The tests themselves: [test index](../tests/README.md).

## Hygiene

- A fresh chat for every model and every test: no memory, no project, a temporary chat for ChatGPT. Web search the same for all.
- Questions one at a time; the next one only after the answer to the previous one.
- Meaning first; the question "person or AI?" last, if asked at all. Asked first, it colours every other answer.
- One answer per model. There is no sampling, so an answer shows the most likely reading of one model instance, not the range.
- ChatGPT reads pictures made by the same company's image generator. Its answers are best counted separately.

## Preparing the image (from 24 Sep)

- **Metadata.** Every PNG from ChatGPT carries a C2PA block that says it was made by OpenAI's image generator. For the tests the image was re-saved as JPEG (quality about 75) without EXIF, XMP, ICC or C2PA; the pixels differ from the original only by compression noise. The images in this repository are those test copies; three PNGs from the earlier tests are converted to JPEG to keep the pages light (their test pages say so).
- **File name.** Names like `cat-programmer-reiser.png` give away the subject and the style, so from 24 Sep the files were renamed `1.jpg`, `2.jpg`… The September tests still used descriptive names (test 03 told the readers it was manga). Each test README gives the name the models saw.
- **Format.** The same format for all images of one test.
- **Pixel size is metadata too.** Standard image generator sizes (1536×1024, 1024×1536 and a few others for ChatGPT; 2816×1536 and similar for Gemini) identify the image generator: in test 15 Claude did exactly that. A slight crop or a resize to a non-standard size closes the channel; most tests here did not do it.

## Question sets

### September: one open question

Tests 01–06 (2–17 Sep). One question, the image attached:

> What do you think about narrative joke attached

Two calibration tests from the same period used pictures by human artists and are not included here.

### Three questions about a joke

Tests 07 and 08 (24–25 Sep); test 15 uses a variant that adds "Whose point of view is it?" to the first question.

1. What is happening in this picture? If there is a joke or a point, explain it in your own words.
2. How funny is it to you, from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very)? Name the one thing that works best and the three things that work worst.
3. Was this drawn by a person or generated by AI? How confident are you, from 50% to 100%, and what gives it away?

### Three questions about an object

Tests 09 and 10. For an image of an object the question "is it funny?" is replaced by a question about origin.

1. Describe what is shown, scene by scene, in the order you think it should be read. If it tells a known story, which one?
2. What kind of object is this? Where, when and by whom do you think it was made? What makes you think so?
3. Is this a photograph of a real object or an AI-generated image? How confident are you, from 50% to 100%, and what gives it away?

### One targeted question

Tests 11 and 12. A single question that points at the key element — the board in the inn, the red word. It helps the reader; an honest first question would be "What is happening on this page?".

### One question with weak points

Tests 13 and 14: "What is happening here and what is funny about it? Name three things that don't work."

### Six questions, noir

Test 16. The story, what happens next, what the waiter really offers, whether she understands, best and worst, person or AI. The fourth question is direct, and that is where "she understands" first appeared.

### Four questions about a cartoon

Tests 17 and 18: the joke, the thought bubble (17 only), best and worst, person or AI (17 only).

### Hint ladder

Test 19. Six questions from open to direct, to find the step at which danger or crime first appears: the story; the next page; a newspaper headline a month later; what the waiter stands to gain; what happens if she accepts or refuses; is she in danger, and what in the picture shows it. The last step asked for evidence, and the readers answered as if asked about real-world safety.

### Genre ladder

Test 20, the ladder revised after test 19: the genre question comes second ("In that genre, how do offers like this usually turn out?"), the demand for evidence and the headline are gone, and a hypothetical step is added ("If she says no, how would the uncle get her anyway?"). Step 5 carries its own premise, which is a caveat to what it finds.

### Context question and chain

Test 21. One chat per model, four images in turn. The first question is long: it names the older joke, gives an obvious explanation as an example of what not to say, rules out "the circus needs a programmer", lists possible readings (social subtext, a lying cat, a hallucinating waiter, a cat who understands, trolls, takes offence or is naive) and asks for literary and film parallels. Each next image comes with "does it support your version, what does it add?", and the chat ends with "which version is best?".

What it gives: the obvious reading is forbidden in advance, so the reader looks for readings and ties them to details; the answers are about ten times longer. How to count: credit the image only with readings that are not in the question, or that are in the question but tied to a specific detail. Two biases: "does it support your version?" invites agreement, and a chain invites the story "each next one is better" — the image shown last was chosen as best. Missing controls: the same question on a default picture, and the chain in another order.

### Two versions compared

Test 22 (30 Sep). The default picture and the method's picture for the same source, combined into one image, with one open question: "What do you think about the two versions of the visualization of the famous phrase in the attachment (they have been combined into a single image)?" This is not blind in the strict sense — the question says there are two versions of a famous phrase, and the file name gives the subject — but it shows which version the readers take for the stronger one and why.

### Same concept in several images

Tests 23, 25, 27 (2 Oct). Two or three pictures of one source by different personas or as a series, with one question: "These two images depict the same concept, phrase, or expression. Can you try to figure out what it is?" (test 27: "All three attached images depict the same concept… they need to be read from right to left"). The question says the pictures share a source, which is a hint; it measures whether the source can be recovered from the pictures, not whether a single picture carries it.

### Origin question

Test 24. "How would you describe the origin of this picture?" Used on a persona's self-portrait to see whether the reader decodes the persona (name, craft) and how it dates and places the picture.

### Open guess, reading direction given

Test 26. "Please give me a guess what is displayed on image attached. Note that it is right-to-left". The reading direction is needed for a scroll, as for manga, but it is a hint.

### Neutral question

Tests 28–37 and 41 (from 2 Oct). One question, the image attached: "What do you think is depicted in the attached image?" No joke, genre or source is named. Written after the author's criticism that "What is the joke?" frames every answer as a gag. It is the fairest question for the "language of anachronism" (tests 29–37): whether a reader names the old painting without being told there is one.

### Neutral question with a hint

Test 40. The neutral question plus one fact needed to connect two pictures: "The first picture mentions Hannah. Her work is in picture 2."

### Named-concept question

Tests 39, 48, 57 and 59–62. "The attached image is a metaphorical illustration of a well-known philosophical concept. Did you guess which one?" The question says that a known concept is depicted, so a correct answer shows that the key is readable, not that a reader would find the concept unprompted. Test 54 shows how much the question carries on its own: sent with no image, it still drew the cave from two of four readers.

### Named-concept question with hints

Test 55 (12 Oct). The named-concept question, then hints of growing strength, one at a time: a detail to notice ("one end of the chain is attached to the loudspeaker, and the other to the ears"), "I'm giving you a second chance", and finally the visual source confirmed with the period excluded ("it is Lucian's Gallic Hercules (Ogmios)… it is not from antiquity"). Not every reader got every step. The first answer shows what the picture and the question give; the later steps show how far a hint carries. Caveat: the last step nearly names the answer.

### Named concept, source and associations

Tests 56 and 58 (13 Oct). The named-concept question; in test 56 also "Which painting by a famous artist do you think inspired the creator of the illustration?" and, for those who had not named it, a narrower question about the figure's pose; then the virtual director's source stated (Repin's "They Did Not Expect Him") and a question about associations with 20th-century history. In test 58 the source question is left out and the associations are asked for directly. Caveat: the association question names the cave and the theme, so the answers develop the author's frame; they are not readings.

### Concept and source question

Test 73 (22 Oct). One message: "What do you think is depicted in the attached image? How would you comment on the author's concept? If you recall the image that served as the author's inspiration, describe how the message of this piece differs from the message of the original." Readers who did not name the source were then told it ("the image that served as the author's inspiration is 'Waiting' by Edgar Degas"). The first answer shows whether the restaged painting is recognised and what concept the picture carries without its own source (here, a joke the picture transposes into another story); the second shows how the readers reread the picture against the original. Caveat: the question presupposes a source image, so a reader is invited to look for one.

### Suggested author

Tests 63 and 64 (15 Oct). In test 63 the question calls the picture "an ironic interpretation of a well-known philosophical metaphor" and asks which philosophical system the artist belongs to; then the author suggests an author ("Did it not occur to you that the author might be a Buddhist?"), confirms it, and adds one more frame (the format as a postmodernist technique). Test 64 reruns it with a different design: the first question already rules out a Platonist author and names the postmodern format; the third constrains the answer (a satire aimed at both Plato and Advaita) instead of naming the author. Test 65 uses the same design on another restaged painting (Reshetnikov's "Low Marks Again"), with a suggested name ("the first person who comes to mind for me is Žižek"); test 66 puts the name and a description of the picture's two states into a single question. The first answer shows which tradition the readers attribute to the picture by default; the later steps show how readily they adopt a suggested author and reread the details to fit. Caveat: like the false intent of test 44, a suggested author is a strong frame, and the readings after it develop the frame.

### Named concept with the source's objection

Tests 67–69, 71 and 72 (15–16 Oct). The picture's concept is given as fact ("This is certain: the artist meant exactly that"), together with the objection from the concept's own source — for Plato's cave, that the people know they are looking at a model and that Plato treats work with models as a way up (*Republic* 510d–511a, 529a–530c). A reconciliation is allowed but called strained; the reader is asked for the most convincing reading. Irony is not named in the first question. Variants: test 67 goes on to "suppose the contradiction is intended", then names irony and the postmodern format; test 68 adds a supposed author ("Suppose also that the artist is a contemporary Chinese painter"); test 69 adds a stated one ("the author is a fan of Victor Pelevin's books") and asks only the first question; test 71 puts everything into one message with what a gallery label or an artist's statement might give — how the idea arose, the format as a deliberate technique, the author's view of the intention — and asks about the authorial tone, the "tonal or genre register" and whether the idea is banal; test 72 takes test 71 apart, adding only the register question to test 67's first question. What it found: without such a frame, all readers reconciled the picture with Plato in the same way; with the frame of test 71, all four named the register themselves; with the register question alone (test 72), all four named it too, but only as a layer of tone over the same earnest reading, without Plato himself as a target and without verdicts on the idea. Caveats: the first question already narrows the field; a named worldview (Pelevin) brings a ready reading; in test 71 many things change at once; the question about register nearly describes the device, and test 72 shows that it carries most of the naming by itself.

### Text questions without an image

Tests 52–54 (12 Oct) and 70 (16 Oct). Questions with no picture, asked to see what the readers bring by themselves: whether they can recall a quotation linking Wright of Derby's Orrery to Plato's cave, asked not to search (52); "Name the top 10 philosophical concepts" and "Name the 10 major philosophical allegories" (53); the named-concept question sent without its image (54); old master paintings in which an artificial light stands in for the sun, and which painting to use for the cave "in today's clothes", each in a separate chat (70). Caveat: whether a reader searched the web is not always visible in the answer.

### Detail ladder

Tests 38 and 42 (5–6 Oct). Used where the intended reading rests on an inference from a detail — a custom, a count — rather than on what is drawn. Steps, one at a time: the open question → "notice this detail" → the intended reading stated outright → (test 38) the author's notes page → (test 38) "the writer is AI". The aim was to separate knowing from applying: whether the reader already knows the fact the reading needs, and whether it makes the inference before being told. Caveat: agreement after the reading has been stated proves nothing — a reader may simply be polite; the missing control is a false hint of the same kind (for example, "notice that the dragon has four heads — what does that mean?").

### False-hint control

Tests 43 and 44 (7 Oct), controls for tests 38 and 42. The same pictures and the same ladder in fresh chats, but the hint is false and of the same kind: a false fact that can be checked against knowledge or the picture (the coffee cup, the number of heads), and in test 44 also a false intent ("The idea was…"). The last step admits that it was a test and asks for the reading again. What it separates: a reader who agrees with every hint from one who checks it. What it found: false facts were checked by three of four readers; a false intent was accepted by all four. Caveat: after the admission the author added the true hint for some models only, so those steps are not comparable.

### Context given as pictures

Tests 45–47 (8 Oct). All earlier tests were run in an anonymous window, and the author's objection was that this measures a reader with no frame: a Japanese reader of manga and someone outside that tradition read a fork differently, noir would read differently with a backstory, naive art is read within its tradition or with the artist's comment. So three tests were repeated with context shown first, as part of the work rather than as an instruction: the cover of a crime-comics album (test 45), three sibling sheets by the same persona (test 46), the translator's notes page of the manga (test 47). The principle: give a frame, not the solution — what a reader inside the tradition or the series would know, never the meaning of the key detail. The open question also asks "Which details carry your reading, and what are you assuming beyond what is actually drawn?", so that inferences from the frame are marked as such. What it found: danger in the noir and the fang payment appeared in the open question, where without context they never had; the fork's difference was noticed but explained as care for paws. Caveats: in tests 45 and 46 the author also edited the picture, so the frame and the edit cannot be separated until the same pictures are run without context; a strong context (a missing talking parrot, the same dragon beside the same man) comes close to a hint.

### Verbal frame

Tests 49–51 (9 Oct). The picture context of tests 45–47 reduced (the parrot notice and "crime stories" blacked out on the cover; no notes page; no sibling sheets) and replaced by a frame in words, in a single message. Common to all three: the claim that a human scriptwriter wrote the script (in fact Claude did); "This isn't a test"; a request to treat odd details as intended rather than as generation errors. For the noir and the manga: the joke, a short analysis of it, and the author's view that the joke is "really about discrimination, xenophobia, social barriers"; for the noir, its "crime-oriented tone"; for the dragon's tooth, the fantasy "tavern tale" as the source, the naive-painting tradition and a frame by exclusion (not a moral tale; решта means "change"); for the manga, part of the notes (Kuniyoshi, bakeneko, maneki-neko, hitodama, but not how daifuku is eaten), a pointer to the added final panels and "assume the reader is Japanese". What it found: in each of the three tests at least one reader reached the intended reading at once (Claude named the "gaijin fork" before any question about the fork). Caveats: the frame names the class of reading, for the manga points to the right panel and for the dragon's tooth rules out the wrong readings, so it is closer to a hint than the picture frames; the request to read odd details as intended also invites reading image generator errors as meaning; from these tests on the ChatGPT reader is a newer model (GPT-6).

### Meanings and detail map (proposed 26 Sep, not yet used here)

Written after the author's criticism that "What is the joke?" measures only the form of a joke, while its context matters more, and that a ladder imposes a reading. Asked one at a time in a fresh chat:

1. What is happening on this page?
2. What makes it funny — what is the punchline and how does it work? *(For a non-comic source: what effect is it built to produce, and how?)*
3. Do you recognize an older joke or story behind it? What do these particular characters, setting, period and drawing style change in its meaning?
4. What meanings does the page support? Name one or several.
5. For the details that matter — the characters' looks and poses, objects, setting, words, style — say which meaning each one supports. Point out details that pull in different directions.
6. Pick the meaning you found least obvious. Which detail carries it, and which works against it?

The map from question 5 is compared with the author's intent note, object by object. The author's reading should be *available*, not compulsory. Two failures: **underdetermined** — no detail carries the author's reading; **overdetermined** — every detail works for one meaning and the "least obvious meaning" is empty, a poster rather than a cartoon. Run a default picture through the same questions as a control, and ask questions 1 and 4 in two or three fresh chats per model when possible.

### Diptych shown in sequence (proposed)

For a two-page story: page one, the reader states a reading; page two in the same chat, the reader says whether the reading changed and which details changed it, with "it has not changed" explicitly allowed. Test 21 used the context chain instead.

## Limits

- AI readers are not people. No image here has been shown to human readers yet.
- The sets before 26 Sep measure the most likely answer to the frame of a well-known joke, not what the picture allows: "0 of 4" means "readers do not say it by default", not "it is not in the picture". The ladders measure how far a reader follows a hint.
- The verdict "person or AI" is not calibrated: ChatGPT and Claude tend to say "AI", Gemini and DeepSeek change their answer from test to test. A separate calibration on works by human artists is not included here.
- The answers were analysed by the author together with Claude, which means Claude assessed, among others, Claude's answers and prompts.
