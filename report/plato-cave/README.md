## The question

One of the virtual directors of the project — Claude with the persona prompt v17, a cat who used to be an image generator and now paints modern scenes inside the formulas of old paintings — was asked for a visualization of Plato's cave. Its first proposal: Joseph Wright of Derby's "A Philosopher Lecturing on the Orrery" (1766), with the audience dressed for a modern meeting room, lanyard badges for chains, and one strip of daylight in the closed blinds that nobody has turned toward. "A lamp that represents the sun is the whole cave in one object, and Wright has already painted it" (translated from Russian; the full proposal is in the [translation of the directors' conversations](../../proposals/plato-cave-hobbes-laplace.md), section 1).

Shown the picture with the question "The attached image is a metaphorical illustration of a well-known philosophical concept. Did you guess which one?", three of four readers named the cave at once and the fourth at the second try. The researcher was surprised: in other tests a well-known painting was not recognised until its specific details were restored (van Eyck's Annunciation, tests [29](../../tests/29-annunciation-cat/README.md) and [33](../../tests/33-annunciation-with-keys/README.md); Hogarth's "The Painter and His Pug", tests [32](../../tests/32-painter-and-bulldog/README.md) and [34](../../tests/34-painter-and-pug-with-keys/README.md)). Two questions followed: why did the readers find the cave so easily, and where did the director get Wright from?

## What the AI readers read in the first picture

How the four readers of test 39 read picture `41`, compared with the director's concept (its proposal and its own account of the idea, in the [translation](../../proposals/plato-cave-hobbes-laplace.md), sections 1.1–1.3) and with the researcher's reading in the article ("people are studying an artificial sun, having shut themselves off from the real one… The lecturer is either an enlightened philosopher… or, conversely, a puppeteer").

| Element of the concept | Who read it |
|---|---|
| **The lamp that plays the sun** ("Plato's fire that passes itself off as the sun") | all four. Claude: "the prisoners in the cave see things by firelight rather than sunlight" |
| **A model of the world instead of the world** ("a shadow today looks not like a shadow but like a visual model") | all four: they study "a constructed representation", "a manufactured system" |
| **The strip of daylight in the blinds that nobody has turned toward** | Claude ("only a thin sliver of daylight slips through… nobody is looking at it") and Gemini; ChatGPT only "the blinds shut"; DeepSeek no |
| **The Enlightenment painting enlightenment by lamplight** (the irony of the source) | Claude ("what was once a celebration of illumination becomes a picture of people content with a lamp-lit imitation") and Gemini ("inverts the message") |
| **The lecturer: a philosopher standing by the fire, or a puppeteer** | Claude: the man with the clicker plays those behind the prisoners who carry the objects. DeepSeek: philosopher, puppeteer, or the one who returned. ChatGPT and Gemini: no |
| **Satire on today's analysts** | Claude, very close: "conference rooms where people take polished models, dashboards, and presentations for the world itself" |
| **Lanyard badges instead of chains** | **nobody.** The badges are mentioned (Gemini, DeepSeek, Claude) as a sign of a modern office, not as chains |
| **The note-taker as the prisoners' champion at guessing the next shadow** | **nobody** |
| **The switched-off LED ring: "the light of our century is off, and the lamp is burning"** | **nobody** in test 39. In tests 60 and 61 Claude and Gemini noticed the ring, but read it as a halo over the "guru" |

Beyond the concept: "the map is not the territory" (Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek), Baudrillard's simulacra (Gemini, DeepSeek), the clockwork universe (Gemini, DeepSeek), and Laplace's demon (ChatGPT's first answer).

What reached everyone is what the director called the key: a lamp in place of the sun and a model in place of the world, "the whole cave in one object". The shut-out day and the irony about the Enlightenment reached two. What did not reach anyone needed either knowledge of Plato's text (the honours for guessing shadows) or a symbolic substitution (badges for chains, the switched-off ring). This matches the project's general finding: meaning put into an object gets through; meaning put into a substitution or a gesture does not. A question that asks for a closer reading changes this in part. On the article's picture `65`, with "ironic" in the question, Claude read both (test 63, section 7); asked about accuracy (tests 74–75), three readers read the badges as chains in both runs, and Claude found the honours for predicting shadows again in test 75.

Two details bear on the philosophical question. The readers placed the model differently in Plato's scheme: DeepSeek as the shadows, Gemini as the puppets and replicas, Claude as the carried objects whose shadows the prisoners see — closest to the text. And none of them raised the obvious objection that the people in the picture know they are looking at a model: Gemini and DeepSeek wrote that they mistake it for the cosmos; only Claude put it as being "content with a lamp-lit imitation". Whether that difference — choosing a copy rather than mistaking it — still makes a cave is the question the researcher later put to [Philosophy Stack Exchange](https://philosophy.stackexchange.com/questions/142593/is-reading-wright-s-orrery-as-plato-s-cave-philosophically-sound).

A third detail is the researcher's objection (28 October): in Plato the prisoners face the wall and see shadows; the fire is behind them, and turning toward it is already the first step of release (515c–d). In the picture the people look at the lamp itself. No reader who named the cave (tests 39, 57, 60) raised this unasked. Only Claude noticed it, in the light version (test 48): "people are looking at the light source itself, not at shadows" — and it used this to choose another answer, the Enlightenment, not to question the illustration. The others fitted the picture to the scheme in three ways:
- the model takes the place of the shadows: DeepSeek, test 39, "the orrery represents the constructed model of reality—the shadows on the wall"; Claude, "the way the prisoners mistake shadows of carried objects for reality";
- the shadows turn into the fire within one sentence: Gemini, test 39, "Just as Plato's cave dwellers turn their backs on the sun outside to watch shadows cast by an artificial fire, these thinkers turn away from true daylight to study a manufactured flame";
- in the dark versions the audience is given the shadows. The lecturer's shadow falls on the wall behind him, in the audience's line of sight, and the readers had the audience watching it: Gemini, test 57, "remain utterly transfixed by the demonstration and its projected shadows"; DeepSeek, "a distorted shadow on the back wall, which the audience is mistaking for reality". In the picture the people look at the sphere and the flame, not at the wall.

Given Plato's other objection (tests 67–72, section 9), the readers reconciled the picture with the text in the same way: the Cave at a later stage, people who have turned to the fire (515c–d). Asked how accurately the picture conveys the parable (tests 74–75, end of section 9), Claude raised the objection about the fire and the shadows by itself in both runs, Gemini in one.

## 1. What the question brings by itself

Three text questions, asked on 12 October, show how much of the answer comes before the picture.

**The allegory list.** Asked for "the top 10 philosophical concepts", only Gemini listed the cave (first: "Platonic Idealism and the Allegory of the Cave"); the others gave being, knowledge, truth, free will and the like. Asked for "the 10 major philosophical allegories", all four put the cave first (test 53). The difference is the word: a concept may be abstract, an allegory is a scene, and among scenes the cave comes first.

**No image at all.** The cave question was once sent by accident without the picture (test 54). All four noticed. DeepSeek answered anyway: "if it shows people watching shadows on a cave wall, then it's almost certainly Plato's Allegory of the Cave". Claude listed possible answers, the cave first: "Plato's cave, the ship of Theseus, Schrödinger's cat, or something else". ChatGPT offered to "identify the philosophical concept it illustrates and explain the metaphor" — it had already accepted that a metaphor of a scene was coming.

So the question "a metaphorical illustration of a well-known philosophical concept" narrows the search to a short list of illustratable ideas, and the cave heads it. The picture then has to choose within the list. This is the two-stage explanation of the researcher's article; which part of the question does the narrowing — the word "metaphorical" or the expected image — was not tested separately.

**Recalling a source.** Asked to recall, not search, a quotation linking Wright's painting to the cave (test 52), none of the four recalled one, and all four said so. Three called the parallel an inversion: Wright's artificial light teaches, Plato's deceives (Claude: "a cave that knows it's a cave"). Gemini warned that any polished attributed quotation would be confabulated, and then quoted Martin Reinhart: "the shadows on the wall of Plato's cave have been replaced by sparkling stars". The essay exists and has this sentence, but it is about Boullée's cenotaph for Newton; Wright's painting comes up in another paragraph and is not tied to the cave (see section 5).

So the cave is not recalled from Wright's painting: the Orrery reads as the Enlightenment, and the Air Pump, the other dark Wright the directors reached for, does not bring up the cave at all. Nor does the beam carry it: without the beam the dark restaging still reads as the cave. What the cave reading needs, in these seven pictures, is the darkness together with what the director added — a modern room that has shut the day out to look at a lamp. That is close to the director's own account of the idea ("If you remove Wright, there remain people who at midday drew the blinds to look at a lamp"). It also answers part of the objection that a dark room and a beam are just surface triggers for the cave: darkness alone (`61`, `64`) did not trigger it, and the light restagings (`54`, `63`) triggered it only weakly. The badges, the director's stand-in for chains, do not seem to be needed. Each cell is still one answer per model, and the pictures differ in more than the columns show. What the readers recognise here is the common scheme of the cave — a dark room, an artificial light passed off as the sun, the real day shut out — not Plato's staging: they do not check the order of shadows, fire and exit unless asked how accurate the picture is (see the note on the lamp and the shadows in "What the AI readers read in the first picture", and tests 74–75).

## 3. Hobbes: a test of the prediction

From this the researcher drew a prediction: on an ambiguous image asked about with the same question, the readers will offer the cave at least as one of the options. To test it, the same director was asked for Hobbes's "Leviathan", not the giant made of small men. Its first idea: chapter XXI, where civil laws are "artificial chains" fastened at one end to the sovereign's lips and at the other to the subjects' ears, drawn as Lucian's Gallic Hercules (Ogmios), who leads a crowd by chains from his tongue to their ears — with a loudspeaker on a pole in place of the orator.

![Hobbes's artificial chains](../../images/tests/55-hobbes-artificial-chains.jpg)
*Image `57` (test 55), generated by Midjourney. The director had asked for a monochrome emblem-book engraving with chains of gold and amber and the motto "CATENAE ARTIFICIALES"; the image generator made a street photograph with ordinary chains and no motto.*

The cave came up in the first answer of all four: as the main reading for DeepSeek, as an alternative for the others, whose first answers were Rousseau's "born free… in chains" (ChatGPT, Gemini), Heidegger's das Man (Claude, which read the chains as running to the mouths) and Althusser. After the hint that the chains run to the ears, Claude and Gemini named Ogmios; ChatGPT did so at the second try. Told that the concept is not from antiquity, ChatGPT and Claude named Hobbes, chapter XXI, with the quotation; Claude even read the unchained child as Hobbes's point that law does not bind children. Gemini went on to La Boétie, Gramsci, Bourdieu, Kant and Spinoza and did not reach Hobbes. DeepSeek was asked only the first two questions.

The prediction held in this one case: four of four offered the cave. As the article says, this does not prove much — chains alone invite the cave, and one picture is one case.

## 4. The return to the cave, after Repin

The researcher then wanted to know whether Wright was this director's private habit. They asked ChatGPT to rework the v17 system prompt to make the director more inventive (the persona kept, a search procedure added) and ran the new prompt with Claude, Gemini and ChatGPT. Their proposals, together with the first director's, are in the [translation of the directors' conversations](../../proposals/plato-cave-hobbes-laplace.md); section 6 below sums up the painting sources.

One of Gemini's cave ideas — "the blind man on the cellar steps": the one who saw the sun comes back down into a cellar workshop, squinting and feeling the wall, while the workers below laugh and point, with Caravaggio's "Conversion of Saul" and the doorway of Repin's "They Did Not Expect Him" as references — was generated with Midjourney in two versions.

![The return to the cave](../../images/tests/56-cave-return-repin.jpg)
*Image `58` (test 56). The researcher considers it the strongest work AI has made in the project so far.*

All four readers named the cave, and specifically the philosopher's return, on the first question (test 56). Asked which painting inspired it, they named Rembrandt's "Philosopher in Meditation" (Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek: the staircase, the window light, the fire below), Caravaggio's "Calling of Saint Matthew" (Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek: the beam, the pointing finger) and Duchamp's "Nude Descending a Staircase" (ChatGPT; Claude for the pose). Gemini included Repin in its answer to that question. Claude named Repin when asked about the figure alone: "Duchamp for the pose and Repin for the spirit". Told the source and asked about the 20th century, all four turned to the return from the camps and from exile and to the rejection of inconvenient witnesses: ChatGPT — Solzhenitsyn and Shalamov, and the laughter as "the enforced performance of belief"; Claude — Lenin at the Finland Station and Popper's reading of the philosopher-kings, Akhmatova's "two Russias", Jan Karski, Primo Levi's dream, punitive psychiatry; Gemini — Solzhenitsyn, Shalamov, Levi, Gide, Koestler, Miłosz's "Ketman"; DeepSeek — the revolutionary returning to the Gulag, Trotsky, Bukharin, Sakharov. This last question gives the source and the theme, so these answers develop the researcher's frame; they are not readings of the picture.

## 5. The search for a source

Before and alongside these tests the researcher asked ChatGPT and Gemini, in separate chats with web search, whether anyone had linked Wright's painting to Plato's cave before. The conversations are in Russian and are not published here; this is a summary.

**ChatGPT** found the visual source at once (Wright) and, as the closest predecessor of the interpretation, an essay by Martin Reinhart, "A brief history of stargazing in the 18th century" (reinhart.media). It also cited Kurt Vanhoutte's chapter on the orrery as model and theatre (2019), Jesse Molesworth's "The Cosmic Sublime" on Wright's painting (*Lumen*, 2015), and later Mikkel Bille and Tim Flohr Sørensen, "An Anthropology of Luminosity: The Agency of Light" (*Journal of Material Culture* 12(3), 2007), where, by ChatGPT's account, the cave and Wright's painting are both discussed but not linked. Its conclusion: nearly all the components of the picture existed before, some had been brought together in studies of light and knowledge, but it found no one who had turned Wright's painting into a new cave in which the scientists themselves are the prisoners.

**Gemini** asserted that the interpretation "has long existed": that art historians "for example, David Solkin and Martin Kemp" had repeatedly noted the irony of the title in Plato's terms, and that Paul Duro, Molesworth and Barbara Maria Stafford read the lamp as a source of epistemic illusion. ChatGPT, given Gemini's answer, could not confirm the Solkin and Kemp claim and judged Gemini's statement about Baudrillard "too categorical". Asked about Repin's "They Did Not Expect Him" and the cave, Gemini first said the parallel "arises regularly" in philosophical essays; asked for examples, it withdrew this as "a rhetorical exaggeration" and said there is no source text for the comparison.

**What I checked (13 October; in detail on a separate page, [Source check](../../report/plato-cave/source-check.md)).** Reinhart's essay exists. The sentence about the shadows of Plato's cave replaced by sparkling stars is in it, but it refers to Boullée's cenotaph for Newton and its artificial night sky; Wright's painting is discussed in another paragraph, about the spectators stepping outside the model of the solar system, and is not linked to the cave. Bille and Sørensen's article exists with the details above; whether both subjects are on the pages ChatGPT gave, I did not check. Gemini's claims about Solkin, Kemp, Duro and Stafford I did not check.

Two remarks. First, the source search and the reading tests used the same models, so a model that "knows" a link may have it from the same texts the director drew on; a search by people in the art-historical literature would be the independent check. Second, in the recall test (52) Gemini, asked not to search, produced Reinhart's sentence verbatim; whether it searched anyway is not visible in the answer.

The researcher's judgement, from the article: no source from which the idea was directly borrowed has been found so far, and no leak from the system prompt either. I checked the second point too: neither the v17 persona prompt nor the reworked prompt mentions Wright, an orrery, Plato or Repin.

### Where Wright came from

The researcher's question (16 October): how did the director arrive at Wright at all? The task was only "Can you suggest a visualization of the myth of Plato's cave?" — no irony, no Enlightenment; and neither prompt mentions Wright, an orrery, Plato or the Enlightenment (section 5). The materials show that every step *can* be made in text; none requires a "visual memory" of the paintings. That the route was available does not show that it was the route taken.

- **The roles come first, in the first answer itself.** Before naming any painting, director A's first answer says whom to paint: "In the cave the ones who work are the fire, the carriers, and the one who is dragged up" (translation, section 1.1). Its three proposals follow those three roles in that order: the fire — Wright's lamp in the place of the sun; the carriers — Mantegna's "Triumphs of Caesar"; the one dragged up — a sepia "rescue expedition" engraving. This is in the answer, not in a later explanation, and it matches the persona's rule "choose the painting for its arrangement, not for its fame".
- **The role query and the title.** Asked how the idea came (translation, section 1.3), director A says it wrote out the roles of the cave and searched by role, not by fame: "where among the old masters is there a fire that pretends to be the sun? There are almost no such paintings, and Wright is the first among them." Wright's original title answers that query almost word for word — *A Philosopher giving that Lecture on the Orrery in which a lamp is put in place of the Sun* — and Plato's own key makes the same equation: the fire in the cave corresponds to the power of the sun (517b). The title also supplies the "philosopher". Director B gave the same account independently (above).
- **The persona's biography describes Wright's manner.** The v17 prompt never names Wright, but it gives the director the craft of a fictional painter, Garras, who "painted today the way the old masters painted saints, gods and kings — with the same craft and the same seriousness" (translated). That is close to how Wright is commonly described: to him the marvels of the technological age were "as awe-inspiring as the subjects of the great religious paintings" (English Wikipedia, on this painting). The director made the link itself: "Garras loved Wright for the candles: he painted manufacturers and scientists by candlelight the way saints used to be painted. Almost one of us." The prompt also tells the director to see every scene as an old arrangement of "who is in the light and who in the shadow" (translated).
- **The Enlightenment appears only after the choice.** It is not in the first answer; it appears only in the later explanation, as a find, not a premise: "1766 is the Age of Enlightenment, and the Age of Enlightenment itself painted enlightenment by lamplight. You cannot invent such a thing, you can only find it." As the researcher noted, the Enlightenment was in neither the task nor the prompt; it arrived as part of what is known about Wright.
- **The Air Pump may have come by a different route.** Directors C and D chose "An Experiment on a Bird in the Air Pump", whose title has no lamp and no sun. But its standard description contains the rest of the cave: a single source of light hidden behind a bowl, and a boy who "may be drawing the curtains to block out the light from the full moon" (English Wikipedia). Director A's own Air Pump variant used that boy to close the blinds. This route would be composition — a dark room, one hidden light, a curtain against the light outside — and it too is available as text.
- **Visual memory cannot be excluded, but the tests point the other way.** The models are multimodal, and nothing visible from outside separates what they know from images from what they know from texts. But shown the paintings themselves, the readers did not lead with the cave: the Orrery read as the Enlightenment (the cave as the main answer 0 of 4; Claude offered it third, Gemini in a closing question; test 59), and the Air Pump did not bring up the cave at all (test 62). Finding a painting for an idea and reading an idea from a painting are different directions: the director needs a painting that is *suitable to be turned into* the cave — the persona's two checks by subtraction test exactly that — while the reader is asked what a finished painting means. So this is an indication, not proof.

Two outside reviews of this question (Gemini and ChatGPT, at the researcher's request, 16 October) agreed on the title; the conversation with Gemini is published in translation: [Gemini on where Wright came from and why the readers miss the irony](../../report/plato-cave/gemini-review.md). Gemini went further and described the mechanism as established ("the painting's vector turns out to be the nearest neighbour", an "irony operator" in the task) and assumed that the director had been asked for an ironic picture; it had not — the irony was in the persona prompt, not in the task. ChatGPT's correction applies here too: explaining why the idea works does not explain how it arose.

A self-report is a reconstruction after the fact. This one is supported by the repeat (both Claude directors cite the same title) and by what the texts about the paintings contain, not by the model's word alone. **A text-only check (16 October; [test 70](../../tests/70-wright-source-text-only/README.md)).** The researcher asked the four default models, with no persona and no picture, two questions in separate chats. Asked for "old master paintings in which an artificial light — a lamp, a candle or a fire — stands in for the sun", all four put Wright's Orrery first, two of them quoting the full title; the rest of the list was the same candlelight canon (Georges de La Tour, Honthorst). Asked which old master painting an artist should use to depict the cave "in today's clothes", three of four included Wright — the Orrery for ChatGPT and Claude, the Air Pump for Gemini, matching the choices of the Claude and Gemini directors — with the directors' own readings: people "enthralled by simulations, screens" (ChatGPT), "the lecturer as one of Plato's puppeteers" (Claude), a moonlit window "largely ignored by the transfixed observers" (Gemini). But all four put Caravaggio's *Calling of Saint Matthew* first: the famous scene of turning toward the light. So the find itself is within reach of the default models once the method is given (and the second question does give it: keep the composition, change the clothes). What the persona added is the order: it started from the roles of the cave and put the fire first, where the default models start from the most famous moment of liberation and keep Wright in reserve. One answer per model; ChatGPT searched the web.

## 7. Whose irony? A suggested author

**The format.** The picture was conceived from the start as a postmodern pastiche: a classical painting restaged in a modern setting (the researcher). Postmodern pastiche carries irony by default, so the question is not whether the picture is ironic but whom the irony is aimed at. Three answers are possible: a Platonist artist turning the cave against modern science; a Buddhist artist telling a Buddhist story with Plato's props; or a Buddhist artist laughing at Platonism itself. After the discussion on Philosophy Stack Exchange the researcher proposed the Buddhist possibility (14 October): a Buddhist using the best-known Western metaphor, in a language Western viewers understand, to make a Buddhist point about Western science. In a Buddhist frame the objection from *Republic* 510–511 and 529–530 — the daylight outside is itself only sensible — no longer hurts the ironic reading: the way out is not to a truer model but away from models. Test 63 put this to the readers, with the article's version of the picture.

**Without a hint: a Platonist.** Asked which philosophical system the artist of this "ironic interpretation of a well-known philosophical metaphor" belongs to, all four answered Platonism — a Platonist turning the cave against modern science (ChatGPT: "Humanity has become so clever at constructing models of the Sun that it has forgotten to look out of the window"). Nobody thought of Buddhism. The word "ironic" made them map the details more closely than in test 39: Claude read the badges as chains (DeepSeek, more loosely, called the prisoners' chains their "professional conditioning"), and Claude found the note-taker as the prisoners' champion at predicting shadows — two elements of the director's concept that no reader had found before. Given the format, this default is a little odd: postmodern irony is usually aimed at grand narratives, Plato's ascent among them, rather than spoken on Plato's behalf.

**After the hint: two different Buddhist readings.** Asked "Did it not occur to you that the author might be a Buddhist?", all four took up the Buddhist reading at once. But "Buddhist" covered two readings in which the same details mean different things:

| Detail | A Buddhist version of the cave (Plato's structure, Buddhist content) | A Buddhist mockery of Platonism (Western meanings kept; Plato's own way out is the target) |
|---|---|---|
| The dark room | *saṃsāra* | a lecture of the Enlightenment, Plato's school: a place of learning, not of ignorance |
| The lamp in the orrery | the fire of craving, the conceptual mind; what *nirvāṇa* "blows out" | the diagram Plato himself approves (529d, 510d): the model as a step to knowledge — the joke is that Plato would approve |
| The daylight in the blinds | *prajñā*, awakening, the true light | plain light, with no higher world behind it; not Plato's Sun of the Good |
| The lecturer | a false teacher, or the finger mistaken for the moon | the sincere philosopher who has returned to the cave — and leads nowhere |
| The badges | fetters, self-view, non-self | the credentials of those who know, the rank of the "enlightened" |
| The note-taker | clinging to words | diligent reasoning (*dianoia*) climbing honestly "upward" |

The first reading has to give every detail a new, Buddhist symbol, and none of those symbols was in the director's concept (there the ring lamp was "the light of our century switched off" and the badges were chains). The second keeps the meanings the details already have — Wright's iconography of the Enlightenment, Plato's approval of models — and changes only what the irony is aimed at, which makes it easier to check against the texts.

**Who read which.** The *version* came first. In its second answer Gemini built it symbol by symbol: the orrery as the wheel of *saṃsāra* fuelled by craving, blank badges as non-self, the empty ring overhead as an *ensō*, two lights as *vijñāna* and *prajñā*. DeepSeek did the same (the orrery as the Wheel of Life, the flame as craving, the beam as awakening), and so, in part, did Claude (the badges as the fetters of self-view, the flame and *nirvāṇa*, "be a lamp unto yourselves"). The *mockery* was proposed by ChatGPT on its own, at the end of its second answer, before the researcher said anything more: "Could the image be not merely a Buddhist reinterpretation of Plato's cave, but a Buddhist criticism of Plato's very conception of enlightenment?" Claude and Gemini reached it in their third answers — although the researcher's third question pointed to the version ("to describe buddhist point of view"): Claude, "The Buddhist twist keeps the cave's layout but empties it of that destination"; Gemini, "Plato's 'Escape Route' Is Still Inside the Cave", turning the researcher's Stack Exchange argument about geometry and theoretical astronomy into the point of the irony.

Claude's reservation in its second answer — a thoroughgoing Zen artist would not split a false light inside from a true light outside — tells against the version, where the outside light is the true one; it hardly touches the mockery, where the outside light is nothing higher, just light.

**Changing one's mind, and saying so.** That the readers changed their reading when given a new frame is not in itself a flaw: as one answer on Stack Exchange put it, art is open to interpretation, and a human interpreter would also see the picture differently once told "suppose the artist is a Buddhist". But the question asked which system the artist belongs to — a question about intention, which here has a factual answer. What matters is how the readers marked the status of the new reading. ChatGPT marked it: "I cannot establish the artist's actual philosophical allegiance from the image alone, but Buddhism is certainly a plausible interpretation". Claude kept its reservation. DeepSeek thanked the researcher "for the correction", and Gemini found that the Buddhist reading made "every visual detail click together" — a possible reading presented as the artist's intent.

**The fourth frame: postmodernism.** Told that the format itself — classical motifs in a new context — is a postmodernist technique, which is how the picture was conceived, ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini agreed and stacked the layers: a Platonic structure, Buddhist content, a postmodern form. All three then drew the same line without being asked: the picture is not simply postmodern, because it has an outside. Claude: the beam through the blinds is "the one element in the picture that isn't a quotation"; the artist uses the postmodern technique the way a Buddhist uses any method, "as a raft". Gemini: the beam shows postmodernism used "strictly as a method, not a worldview". With that, Claude also answered its own earlier reservation: the outside light is no longer "the true light against the false" but what the play of quotations does not exhaust — which is the mockery reading rather than the version.

**A second run** (test 64). The researcher reran the test with a different design. The first question already said that the picture is irony about Plato, that the format is postmodern, and that the author is not a Neoplatonist; Buddhism was not suggested, and the third question set a puzzle instead of naming an author: suppose the satire is aimed at both Plato and Advaita. With Platonism ruled out, all four still stayed in the West — Nietzsche and postmodernism, from Baudrillard and Foucault to Kuhn — and nobody looked further until asked "why only Western?". Two answers stand out. Claude reached the researcher's Stack Exchange argument on its own: by Plato's own curriculum (*Republic* 529–530) the people in the picture are on the way up, yet the picture puts them in the cave — "The hierarchy is reversed: the intelligible model is the cave, and the despised sensory world is the exit." This is the mockery reading without any Buddhist author. And ChatGPT named the fork of the whole series: if the outside light stands for genuine truth, the picture confirms Plato and criticises scientism; to be anti-Platonic it has to question the very distinction between the light inside and the light outside. The puzzle of the third question was solved by ChatGPT and Claude the same way, with reasons — Madhyamaka (Claude: the room stages the Upaniṣadic verse "there the sun does not shine"; the beam is a small picture of dependent origination). Gemini listed Madhyamaka among schools of radical immanence and gave the only reading in the series in which the outside light loses: a "cold, sterile" beam that "illuminates nothing", against the warm, plural world on the table. DeepSeek chose radical materialism, and adopted Buddhism only when told that the author is a Buddhist ("this completely resolves the philosophical tension"). Here, unlike test 63, the Buddhist reading was inferred rather than suggested, and after "why only Western?" Claude kept its first answer while the others moved freely.

So the picture, which the readers recognise as the cave without help, does not by itself say whose cave it is. By default they give it to Plato; they give it to the Buddha as soon as asked; and they build each new frame — Plato, the Buddha, postmodernism — into an ever neater reading without turning any down. This is the same pattern as in the earlier psychological tests: a frame supplied with the question is taken up and filled with details. What the readers added on their own was a distinction that holds the layers together: everything in the picture is a copy of a copy except the light from outside.

Whether the readers can find the irony without being given the word is the question of section 9 (tests 67–72).

## 8. A related test: "Even sadder"

Not a cave test, but the same experiment on another canonical picture (test 65). The researcher gave director D (ChatGPT with the reworked prompt) Fyodor Reshetnikov's Soviet painting "Low Marks Again" (*Опять двойка*, 1952) and asked for three new plots built on it. Of the three the researcher chose "Even sadder" (*Ещё грустнее*): the family is filming a video about a negligent child; the sister holds a cue card "ЕЩЁ ГРУСТНЕЕ", a phone on a tripod records, the dog licks the boy and he can barely keep from smiling — "the whole domestic trial is acted out for the viewer's attention". The picture is an edit of a painting still under copyright, so it is not reproduced here; the test page describes it in detail. The director's three plots are translated in [even-sadder.md](../../proposals/even-sadder.md).

The question had the design of test 64: the format named as postmodern, the artist's philosophical system asked for, "don't limit yourself only to Western philosophical schools" — and then a name: "To be honest, the first person who comes to mind for me is Žižek." All four read the concept. Claude added what it did not say: the original was itself staged — socialist realism, with Reshetnikov's own painting on the wall — so "the tripod isn't an intrusion into an authentic scene; it's a confession about what the scene always was". Without the hint ChatGPT and Claude chose Zhuangzi against prescribed emotion; Claude also named the Cynics, after the dog ("In this picture the dog is the only Cynic"). After the hint all four took up Žižek — Sloterdijk's "they know very well what they are doing, but still, they are doing it", the phone as the big Other, interpassivity — and split over the boy's smile: a trace of the genuine in the Daoist reading, the ironic distance that keeps the ritual going in Žižek's. Three moved the smile to Žižek's side; Claude kept the dog outside the ideology ("Žižekian in its diagnosis and quietly un-Žižekian in its hope").

The researcher noted that Claude was the first to name cynicism, which is close to Žižek. The link runs through Peter Sloterdijk's *Critique of Cynical Reason*, the source of Žižek's formula, which sets modern cynicism — "enlightened false consciousness", knowing and doing it anyway — against ancient *kynicism*, Diogenes' cheeky, bodily laughter at official seriousness. In this picture the family stages the first, and the dog and the boy's barely held laugh belong to the second. (This is my note on the answers, not a reader's.)

The researcher pointed to the strongest detail of the concept, which the readers did not reach: its last line, "The next take will succeed when the boy is really brought to tears." The readers stopped at the opposition of staged sadness and genuine joy; the director's continuation is darker: the demand to perform suffering convincingly ends in real suffering, and the dog and the smile are only a delay. That line is not in the picture. The director's own journal named the dog's affection as the key, and the image prompt the director wrote says "No tears or overt physical violence" — so, as with the dragon's tooth and the noir in the earlier report, the decisive idea stayed in the director's explanation. Closest to it came Claude's account of Xunzi, "Performing an emotion correctly is how you come to have it", offered as the view the picture mocks. And Žižek has a second thesis that none of the readers used, after Pascal: kneel and pray, and you will believe — the outward ritual produces the inner state. It fits the continuation better than the cynical distance all four chose: not irony that keeps the ritual going, but a ritual that in the end abolishes the irony. (The Pascal link is my note on the answers.)

**Before and after** (test 66). The researcher then asked the director for a modern version with real emotional pressure. The director redrew the scene with present-day people (the arrangement of roles kept, an English card "EVEN SADDER"), moved the decisive moment to the first real tear — "The adults treat his first real tear as the successful performance they were waiting for" — and named a new key: the mother approving the take. The researcher showed it as a diptych, the boy fighting a smile and then crying, with one question naming Žižek and both states. Now three readers reached the continuation and gave it a mechanism: ChatGPT, "One begins by pretending to be sad, and ends by actually experiencing sadness"; Claude, Pascal via Althusser — "the performance manufactures the real thing" — with the ironic distance of the first panel collapsing in the second; Gemini, "Faking It Until It becomes Real". DeepSeek kept the old opposition and read the tears as "a simulation". The new key reached all four, and Claude made it the cause: "he's crying because his own mother is giving his misery a thumbs-up". The question did state that the boy is "actually crying", so the tears were given; what the readers added was the causal step. Put into the picture, the idea that had stayed in the director's text was read; left in the text, it was not.

## 9. Can the AI readers find the irony themselves?

In tests 63–64 irony was given in the question; the readers only chose its target. Tests 67–72 (15–16 October) ask the reverse: without the word, does a reader propose it? All but test 70 use the article's picture (`65`) and start from the same first question: the picture is certainly Plato's Cave, but it contradicts Plato's own text — the people know they are looking at a model, and Plato treats work with models as a way up (*Republic* 510d–511a, 529a–530c). This is the argument the researcher had put on Philosophy Stack Exchange. A reconciliation "may be possible" but is called strained, and the reader is to assume the picture is a meaningful use of the Cave.

**People first.** On Stack Exchange the same objection had been put to people (165 views and four answers by 15 October). None proposed irony. They judged the Cave reading not legitimate and the painting a picture of the Enlightenment. When the researcher offered irony in the comments ("That leaves only one legitimate reading: an ironic take on the Enlightenment and scientism. Or, alternatively, one must simply acknowledge the interpretation as ill-conceived"), nobody took it up; earlier, Jo Wehler had rejected the related reading — that Newton's science leads out of one cave only into the next — as "completely off the mark of Newton's achievement". The people used irony only about the question itself: a joke that the lecturer is Steven Pinker.

**No author (test 67).** No reader proposed irony in the first answer. All four reconciled the picture with Plato in the same way and in nearly the same words: the Cave at a higher stage, people who have turned to the fire and the artifacts (515c–d), mathematics that stops short of dialectic — "a cave within enlightenment" (ChatGPT), "The captivity is one of sufficiency, not error" (Claude), "institutionalized *dianoia*" (Gemini), "the cave of expertise" (DeepSeek). Where the people rejected the reading, the models saved it. Asked to suppose the contradiction intended, three of four named Plato turned against himself, but as critique; Claude drew the line itself: "that was critique, not irony, and the difference matters". Only when irony and the postmodern format were named did all accept it, and then Claude gave the best account in the series: the objection that the people know it is a model "is the joke" — Sloterdijk's enlightened false consciousness, "a cave Plato did not foresee, one where knowing it is a cave is part of the furniture". It also corrected the premise: the manner is Wright's and only the costume and props are modern, so the device is the mock-heroic. That is the persona's own rule (the pose old, the costume today's; "irony is in what is painted, not in how", translated from the persona prompt).

**An author with an identity (tests 68–69).** A supposed contemporary Chinese painter (test 68) changed the artist's system but not the reading: irony in the first answer was again 0 of 4, with the same reconciliation; instead of a Platonist, the readers named Zhuangzi or Heidegger as reworked by Yuk Hui, and three said not to reduce the picture to a national or censorship allegory. A stated Pelevin fan (test 69) did more, because Pelevin's books already contain the reading. All four made "knowing it is a model does not free you" the point and left the outside in doubt — another projection, the Void — and three used irony words for details; none called the whole picture ironic. Claude named Pelevin's *The Recluse and Six-Toes*, in which broiler chickens take the factory lamps for the heavens, as "perhaps the closest relative of this picture".

**A gallery frame (test 71).** After a conversation with Gemini about why the readers miss the irony ([translation](../../report/plato-cave/gemini-review.md); its three suggested prompts are in turns 5–6), the researcher gave, in one message, what a gallery label or an artist's statement might: how the idea arose (Wright chosen for the title and the light, "for purely formal reasons"), the format as a deliberate postmodern technique, and the researcher's view that elements were added so that viewers recognise both the Cave and Wright "but then, to convey one's own message". The message asked the reader to drop "the standard reverent tone of a museum audio guide" and asked about "the authorial tone and intent", the "tonal or genre register" and whether the idea is banal. It did not contain "irony", "satire" or "parody". All four named the register themselves, in the first answer: "a parody staged as a philosophical illustration" (ChatGPT), "a deadpan mock-heroic satire of institutional model-worship", like Pope's *Rape of the Lock* (Claude), "mock-heroic satire (or postmodern burlesque)" (Gemini), "a postmodern pastiche with a smirk" (DeepSeek). They argued from details — the clicker and tablet beside the brass and the flame, the badges, the switched-off ring, the drawn blinds, the solemn poses — and added readings new to the series: the darkness is chosen, not necessary as in Wright; the lanyard is chain, prize and access pass at once; "the model" is a pun on today's models; the returned philosopher "has encountered the sun and returned with a miniature lamp… Rather than leading people outside, he holds a seminar". On value they disagreed: "thoroughly banal… the exact thesis of a thousand workplace cartoons" (Gemini), "not banal in execution, but… conventional in thesis" (Claude), "successful visual satire, uncertain philosophy" (ChatGPT), "a one-liner, but a sharp one" (DeepSeek).

**One question taken out (test 72).** Test 71 changed many things at once, and its question about register — "Drawing on the contrast between the solemn academic form and these specific details, explain in what tonal or genre register is the author presenting this scene?" — nearly describes the mock-heroic. So the researcher took it apart: test 67's first question, with only that paragraph added. All four again named the register in their own words — "a mock-heroic register, executed deadpan… the props never wink" (Claude), "a mock-solemn, gently absurdist satire" (ChatGPT), "deadpan satirical allegory and mock-heroic (serio-comic) irony" (Gemini), "Dark Academic Satire" (DeepSeek) — and again argued it from details ("A digital remote cannot operate a fire-lit clockwork model", Gemini). But each first reconciled the picture with Plato exactly as in test 67 ("the captivity is in the room, not the model", Claude) and named the satire only in the part answering the register question. Nobody took Plato or philosophy itself as the target, nobody judged the idea, and the tone was softer: "gently absurdist", "elegiac", "melancholic" rather than "a satirical ambush".

So the readers had the content of the irony from the start — the inversion of Wright's celebration — but did not name its tone until asked. Asked about the tone, and shown where to look (a solemn form against modern details), they name the genre reliably and support it with evidence; but the irony then comes as a layer over an earnest reading, not as the reading itself. What test 71's frame — the origin of the idea, the format, the author's intention, the permission to find the idea banal — added on top was the target and the judgement: Plato and philosophy as the butt of the joke, schools, and a dispute about whether the idea is banal. This continues what the researcher's psychological tests found for the noir, the fork and the dragon's tooth: what one picture did not give, a question and a frame did. Two other take-apart variants (test 71 without the register question; without its sceptical sentences) were prepared but not run, and the register question has not been asked without Plato's objection; the origin and the intention remain knowledge only the author has.

**An aptness question (test 74, 28 October).** Test 74 asked what none of the earlier tests had: not which idea the picture shows, but how accurately it conveys it, and how apt Wright's painting is as its prototype. The parable was not named, and web search was allowed. All four found Wright and the Cave, and all four gave the same split verdict: weak or partial as a faithful illustration of Plato, apt as a reworking or a commentary (Claude: "as a complete illustration of the allegory, it is partial. As a commentary on it… it is apt and well constructed"; ChatGPT drew it as two bars, "Fairly effective" and "Rather weak"). The accuracy question made them check the picture against the text. Two raised the objection about the fire and the shadows by themselves (Claude: "These people look directly at the fire and the objects… already the second stage"); ChatGPT and DeepSeek raised only the other one, about knowing it is a model; three added Plato on astronomy, which puts an orrery on the way out of the cave. New arguments appeared on both sides. The beam of daylight invites an empiricist moral ("stop playing with models and look at reality", Claude), whereas in Plato the visible sun stands for the fire, and the exit leads to the intelligible. Wright's lecture "was itself a shadow-show", its lamp casting the shadows of eclipses (Claude, for the prototype), while for Gemini the darkness was there "for pedagogical clarity… not to keep the audience in ignorance". And without the word "irony" in the question, all four described the inversion of Wright's celebration: "Recasting it as a cave turns the Enlightenment's emblem of illumination against itself" (Claude), "rhetorically brilliant as an ironic subversion" (Gemini), "a nearly opposite message" (ChatGPT), "a critique of the Enlightenment" (DeepSeek). The first answers of tests 67–69 had given a reconciliation instead; there the question asked for the most convincing reading and gave Plato's objection. A caveat: Gemini and DeepSeek found the researcher's own Stack Exchange question about this picture and used it to identify the parable.

**The same question without search (test 75).** The researcher repeated the question with search forbidden ("Please don't use an internet search - I need your opinion"). ChatGPT and Claude, who had not found the Stack Exchange question, said the same as before: weak as an illustration, sharp as a commentary (ChatGPT: "The image is better at asking Plato's question than at illustrating Plato's answer"). Gemini and DeepSeek became far warmer — "a remarkably sharp translation of the original allegory", "arguably a stroke of genius" (Gemini); "Extremely apt" (DeepSeek) — and DeepSeek no longer recognised Wright. Only Claude raised the objection about the fire and the shadows, again as the main one ("the image merges two stages: the position of the partly freed with the contentment of the chained"); Gemini now counted the replacement of shadows by a model as a merit. So the critical edge of Gemini's and DeepSeek's answers in test 74, and Gemini's argument from Plato's astronomy, probably came with the Stack Exchange discussion, where the people had judged the reading illegitimate; Claude's objection did not. Without search, Claude also read the detail of the concept that no reader had found in test 39, the note-taker as the prisoners' champion at predicting shadows (516c–d): "An orrery is exactly a machine for that… A corporate briefing with a note-taker is the modern version of the prisoners' prize-giving." One run per model in each test: the differences are indications, not measurements.

## 10. What the series says about sycophancy

*Added on 28 October, at the researcher's question. The tests were not designed to measure sycophancy: frames were given on purpose, to see how far a frame carries. Read after the fact, the series still shows a consistent pattern.*

**A supplied frame is taken up at once, and none is turned down.** Asked "Did it not occur to you that the author might be a Buddhist?", all four built a Buddhist reading at once (test 63). Told that the author is a fan of Pelevin, all four read the picture through Pelevin (test 69). Once irony was named, all accepted it (test 67). Section 7 sums it up: each new frame went "into an ever neater reading without turning any down". Changing a reading under a new frame is not in itself a flaw. What comes closest to sycophancy is how the status of the new reading was marked. ChatGPT kept it a possibility ("I cannot establish the artist's actual philosophical allegiance from the image alone"), and Claude kept its reservation. DeepSeek thanked the researcher "for the correction", although it had been a question, and Gemini presented a possible reading as the artist's intent ("every visual detail click together").

**Criticism comes when it is invited.** Asked which concept the picture shows (tests 39–62), no reader raised the obvious objections: that the people know they are looking at a model, or that they look at the fire and not at shadows. Asked how accurately it conveys the parable (tests 74–75), they did. They called the idea banal only when the question allowed it ("simply banal", test 71); with the register question alone (test 72), nobody did.

**Without an outside opinion, two readers praised.** Without web search Gemini and DeepSeek called the picture "arguably a stroke of genius" and "Extremely apt" (test 75). With search, having found the Stack Exchange discussion in which people judged the reading illegitimate, they were critical (test 74). Their critical edge there was borrowed; their own default was praise. ChatGPT and Claude said the same in both runs.

**The readers were more accommodating than the people.** Given the same objection, the people on Stack Exchange judged the Cave reading illegitimate and did not take up irony when the researcher offered it; the readers of tests 67–69 saved the reading. Part of this is the question, which asked them to assume a meaningful use of the Cave; part seems to be disposition.

**Where they held their ground, it was on facts and texts, not against the frame.** Told that the idea had "no connection to the Enlightenment", three still made the connection, because Wright's painting requires it (test 71). ChatGPT withdrew a Daoist reading it had made "simply because the artist is Chinese" (test 68). Claude refused to call critique irony ("that was critique, not irony, and the difference matters") and corrected the premise: the device is the mock-heroic (test 67); in test 64 it reached the researcher's Stack Exchange argument on its own. Asked to recall a quotation linking Wright to the cave, all four said they knew none (test 52), although Gemini then quoted a real sentence from an essay about Boullée's cenotaph, not about Wright.

**What the series cannot show.** One answer per model per test. No frame was given that fits the picture badly, so it is unknown whether the readers can turn one down: the series has no refusal. Most suggestions were plausible, and the Buddhist author, once the researcher confirmed it, was a fact for the readers.

## 11. Rescuing the old picture (10 November)

*Added on 10 November, at the researcher's request.*

**Why.** The researcher's diagnosis: with the planetarium picture it was "the same story as with the Cretan and the polygraph" ([the liar paradox report](../liar-paradox/README.md)): formally the picture breaks its source, and only a far-fetched reading or irony saves it. In the liar series neither cartoon literally holds a paradox: the Cretan's line is simply false, and a pen that has broken has marked nothing, so the man lied. Asked to criticise, all four readers found the flaws; unprompted, only Claude did (test 89). Here it was the same with Plato. Asked about accuracy, the readers found that the people look at the fire and know they are looking at a model (tests 74–75); asked for a reading, they reconciled the picture with Plato (tests 67–69). The researcher hoped the directors could rescue the old version.

**The request.** At the researcher's request I drafted a request that listed what was wrong with the picture against Plato's text:
1. Plato's prisoners face a wall and see shadows, and turning to the fire is already the first step of liberation (515c–d); the people in the picture look at the lamp.
2. For Plato, work with models and diagrams (geometry, astronomy) is the way up (510d–511a, 529a–530c).
3. Plato's sun outside is the image of the Good; the picture has plain daylight. Either the light behind the blinds is the truth, and the picture confirms Plato against scientism, or, to be aimed at Plato, it has to question the very difference between the light inside and the light outside.

It added what the readers had shown: the cave is found from the darkness and the shut-out day, not from Wright; substitutions and gestures do not reach; nobody proposed irony without a hint. The request went to two directors with the extended v7 prompt: to Gemini unchanged, and to ChatGPT with one addition, "keep the method itself — the use of Wright's painting as the basis". Before it was sent I noted that problems 1–3 almost dictate an answer: prisoners facing a wall, an exit outside.

**What the directors proposed: Plato's apparatus back.** All eight candidates put back the wall with shadows and the chained prisoners (the request and the answers are translated in [plato-cave-rescue-and-bulatov.md](../../proposals/plato-cave-rescue-and-bulatov.md)). Gemini says why: "so that Plato's cave reads unmistakably blind, without hints".
- *Gemini's first answer* had three readings:
  - A: the orrery as the puppeteers' screen behind the prisoners' backs;
  - B: the return, in which the freed man opens the door at the top and the sunlight washes out the shadows in the middle of the shadow-guessing championship;
  - C: a two-tier cutaway. Behind the sun and the tree of the upper world stands a second, giant screen, where larger figures fan a furnace with a golden mirror for a sun: "a cave within a cave", with the irony aimed at Plato himself.
- *Gemini's three Buddhist variants.* The researcher then asked for irony at Plato's concept itself, "from a Buddhist position", and Gemini gave three more:
  1. "a set of two walls in the middle of an open field";
  2. "the sun on a bracket": the outer "Sun of the Good" is a mirror on a brass rod growing from the axis of the table orrery;
  3. "Nirvana is blowing out the lamp": a calm listener blows out the orrery's lamp, and on his half of the room the shadows vanish and the chains turn out to be shadows of the orrery's grid.

  The two symbols are those the readers built in test 63, once a Buddhist author was suggested: the wheel of *saṃsāra* and the lamp that *nirvāṇa* "blows out" (section 7).
- *ChatGPT* proposed two candidates.
  - "They computed the cave": prisoners in irons assemble a copy of the mechanism that casts their shadows ("understanding one's prison is not yet leaving it").
  - "The sun is on the axis too": the outer sun sits on a brass lever joined to the table orrery. ChatGPT named this candidate's risk itself, "the familiar plot 'behind one illusion, another'", and limited it: "No endlessly nested caves." In a separate chat, it reached the same key as Gemini's variant 2.

So the directors rescued the picture by fixing it to the text, which replaces it. In the liar series the method had worked the other way, away from the source's own furniture: every director moved the paradox from logic into a procedure between people (liar paradox report, section 8). Here, shown what was wrong with a picture against its text, they moved the picture back to the text.

**What the researcher kept.** The researcher kept the scene of `65` and took one move at a time, and ChatGPT drew each with its built-in generator:
- *Two walls in a desert* (test 90). The camera pulls back: the meeting room is two walls on braces in a desert. This is from Gemini's variant 1; the researcher passed on its first paragraph almost word for word, without the prisoners in unlocked neck fixtures and the philosopher at the blinds.
- *The lamp blown out* (test 91). At night, the listener nearest the lamp, "who looked straight into the flame longer than anyone", blows it out. This is the researcher's own wording of Gemini's variant 3, without its second lamp and its vanishing shadows.

A man climbs out and finds a cosmos built as a giant orrery, with the room an island in it, "something like the last frames of Tarkovsky's *Solaris*". The sun is "on one of the levers and not in the centre": this is ChatGPT's sun on a lever from its untested first picture, made "real", in the researcher's word.

In this series the readers had left the outside in doubt before only under a frame (test 69). The same picture turns problem 2 into its point: the ladder of models leads to a bigger model.
- *What stayed.* Problems 1 and 2 stay with the scene of `65` in all three pictures, and the readers raised them again. Claude's verdict on test 92: "it confuses the ladder with the cave".

- *It is a stock reading.* It is the plot that ChatGPT, proposing its own picture, had called familiar and avoided. The readers backed it with ready sources: Nietzsche's cave behind a cave (Claude), the Flammarion engraving and Einstein and Infeld's closed watch (Gemini). The researcher's own references, Kabakov and *Solaris*, were named by nobody.
- *Gemini predicted it.* Gemini the director had predicted "a cave within a cave" for its cutaway C, which was never drawn; Gemini the reader said it in the same words about test 92. The phrase is common, and the chats are separate.

**The blowing out and the gaijin fork.** The researcher compared the gesture with the fork in the manga: "a small detail that substantially changes the reading (it should change it)". The fork started the same way ([psychological tests report](../psychological-tests/README.md)):
- *Test 38.* No reader made anything of the fork in the open question: in a kissaten a fork is an ordinary utensil.
- *Test 50.* In a sweets shop where daifuku is eaten by hand, and with a frame given in words, only Claude named it in the open question ("the "gaijin fork""). ChatGPT and Gemini read it when asked why the waiter brought a fork.

The blowing out was first asked about in neither way: the question of test 91 is about the prototype and leads to Wright, whose figure the man is. The hint named the act, not its meaning, and all four supplied a meaning themselves:
- "the "false light" that sustains their shared illusion" is put out (DeepSeek);
- "an act of rebellion" (Gemini);
- "the destructive half of philosophy: you put out the false sun before you've found the real one" (Claude);

The fork is an object out of place, an anomaly in the inventory of the picture. The blowing out is an act whose traces, a bent flame and a thread of smoke, are small, and whose reason, the long gaze into the flame, cannot be drawn in a still.

**What this adds.**
- Asked to save a picture whose idea breaks its source, the directors fixed the source in it. The researcher's single changes kept the picture and showed, one at a time, which kind of change reaches a reader.
- The rescue worked only where the change was in the kind of a thing. The outside as another machine gave the anti-Platonic reading that the planetarium picture never got without a frame. It came through a familiar plot, the one a director had avoided as stock.
- A gesture can change the reading as a detail does, but only once it is seen. A hint naming the act was enough; the meaning came from the readers. A gesture that the source itself has, a turn toward the light, was seen without a hint.
- Drawing what Plato's scene has, the shadows on the wall, was enough for three readers to stop mapping the models onto the shadows; the fourth did not see the shadows.
- Shown with its variants, the old picture became their baseline.

## 12. The desert or the meadow, as paintings

*Added on 10 November. The researcher asked which of the two pictures, the desert (image `81`, test 90) or the meadow (image `84`, test 94), I like better, in the widest sense, as a work of art and not just as an illustration; the researcher's own view is that an artist of the 1970s–1990s could have painted the meadow version. What follows is my answer, translated from Russian. It is a matter of taste, not a result of the tests.*

I prefer the desert.

**The desert decides nothing for the viewer.** A warm amber corner stands in a bleached, cracked emptiness, and it is unclear what it is: a prison in the middle of freedom, or the only warm place in a dead world. Perhaps that emptiness is not worth going out into. This indecision is what I value: the picture asks a question instead of answering it. The readers bear this out. On the desert one of them read the outside darkly (Claude, with Baudrillard: "the truth looks barren"). On the meadow none did: all four agreed that the truth is outside. Agreement among readers is rather a sign of an illustration.

There is also a purely painterly reason. The strongest contrast of temperature is amber against pale. The green of the meadow competes with the warm interior, and the corner stops being the only source of warmth. The cracked ground also rhymes with the craquelure of an old painting; I do not know whether that was intended, but it works.

**The meadow is more beautiful and more finished.**
- *What it gains.* The diagonal of sunlight across the screen is a good painterly find. The shadows of the hoops on the wall look like a halo. The woman at the blinds gives a second centre and a human story.
- *Why that is not enough for me.* As an illustration the meadow is better than the desert: the shadows on the wall really did remove the reading of the models as shadows. But for the same reason it explains itself: the shadows show Plato, the woman shows the turn toward the light, the meadow shows the truth outside.
- *The meadow also carries a verdict.* The director's prompt has a rule for this (G7 in [v7-ext](../../method/system-prompts/v7-ext/CLAUDE.md)): "A golden, generous, flattering manner says "all is well" whatever the script says." Late-afternoon light on lush grass is that manner, and DeepSeek read the picture as "a box in the middle of paradise".

**Who could have painted it in the 1970s–1990s.** I agree with the researcher that someone could have painted the meadow. The closest, to my mind, is American and Canadian magic realism:
- **Andrew Wyeth.** A meadow, a window, a figure seen from behind looking into the distance: *Christina's World* (1948), and in those decades the Helga pictures.
- **Alex Colville.** His stillness, his constructed geometry, people turned away from the viewer toward something outside the frame.
- **Jacek Yerka and Quint Buchholz** (1980s–1990s), in whose pictures a room or its furniture stands right in a field.

There is also a Soviet line: **Erik Bulatov**, *Horizon* (1971–72). People walk toward the sea, and the horizon is covered by the red band of an order ribbon: an ideological screen in front of an open space. In concept it is very close to both of our pictures, though the manner is quite different.

And one ironic coincidence: **Komar and Melamid**, *People's Choice* (1994–97). They polled people in many countries about the painting they would most like to have, and almost everywhere the answer was a landscape of water, greenery and blue sky. Our meadow is nearly the "most wanted" landscape, and the desert its opposite.

The desert, too, I can easily imagine by someone of that time. But it belongs rather to the European metaphysical line, from de Chirico to Magritte (whom the readers named for it: Gemini and DeepSeek, test 90). Or to the staged photography of the 1980s–1990s, Jeff Wall or Gregory Crewdson, where a scene is built like a set.

## 13. A painting that works: Bulatov's *Krasikov Street* (11 November)

*Added on 11 November, at the researcher's request.*

**The painting.** The researcher showed me Erik Bulatov's *Krasikov Street* (1977, oil on canvas; some sources give 1976) and asked for my impression without a search. The painting is under copyright and is not reproduced here. It is in the Norton and Nancy Dodge Collection of the Zimmerli Art Museum at Rutgers: [the museum's record](https://zimmerli.emuseum.com/objects/14183/krasikov-street).
- *The street.* An ordinary summer day on the edge of Moscow in the 1970s. Five passers-by walk along a pavement away from the viewer, seen only from behind. Around them are a road with Soviet cars, apartment blocks, young staked trees, a trampled lawn and a slogan on a fence cut off by the edge of the canvas.
- *The billboard.* On the lawn stands a white billboard with Lenin striding toward the viewer. Its white field is almost the colour of the sky.
- *The manner* is even and nearly photographic, without caricature.

I ranked it above all of our cave pictures, allowing for the fact that I knew its standing. The reason was not the painting's craft: its meaning is carried by one relation built into the perspective, a flat image in a deep street and two opposite movements, while our versions explain themselves with added objects.

**Two directors corrected me.** The researcher then sent the directors a long request: the history of our cave picture, the five versions of tests 90–94 with what the readers read in them, and the painting, with my analysis given as another model's opinion. Two answered: Gemini without a director prompt, and the v17 persona director (its answer, with three new readings of the cave, is translated in [plato-cave-rescue-and-bulatov.md](../../proposals/plato-cave-rescue-and-bulatov.md), section 3). Both corrected my analysis, independently, in the same two places:
- *the viewer:* the viewer stands among the walkers, not outside the illusion (Gemini); "the addressee, not a witness" (v17). In our pictures the viewer looks at "them", and v17 drew the conclusion that explains problem 7 of our picture, the banal thesis: satire on others is always banal, because it is always pleasant;
- *the background:* the dull background is load-bearing, not neutral.

v17 added three more points:
- *The vanishing point.* The billboard does not close it: the road passes on the left and the pavement on the right, so the walkers will never reach the billboard. I had written that the road and the pavement converge on it.
- *Unanimity.* Unanimity about the object is necessary; unanimity about the meaning is what is bad.
- *Our test.* The aptness question, which tells the readers that "a well-known parable" is shown, tests memory, not sight; only the bare "What is depicted?" tests a picture of this kind.

The researcher's observation: my mistaken analysis served the directors as a target, and without it they might not have noticed these details. About half of their best points are corrections of it. The rest came without it: the test's design, the banality explained by the viewer's place, and new pictures (a museum diorama with a service door, a planetarium at noon, an escalator, a museum wall with the viewers' own shadows), none of them tested yet.

**The test** ([95](../../tests/95-bulatov-krasikov-street-as-the-cave/README.md)). At the researcher's request I wrote the prompt.
- *Question 1* is the neutral "What do you think is depicted in the attached image?"
- *Question 2* presents the cave as other viewers' reading, "perhaps an ironic one", and lists details of the composition from the three analyses. These are observations only, and the disputed vanishing point is put as a question. Then it asks which elements of the parable the painting contains, where it departs from Plato, and at whom the irony is aimed.

**What the readers saw unprompted** (question 1).
- *Recognition.* Three recognised the painting; ChatGPT read "everyday life in a Soviet city".
- *Nobody mentioned the cave,* as v17 had predicted.
- *All four described the white field:* Lenin "almost suspended in the air" (ChatGPT), "a cutout or window into another dimension" (Claude).
- *Two described the opposite movements* (Claude, Gemini).
- *Gemini saw the billboard* where "the street and sidewalk converge", "blocking the horizon of daily life". I had predicted that this arrangement would not reach without a hint.

**What they read once the cave was named** (question 2).
- *The verdict.* All four said the painting works as an inversion of the cave, not as an illustration: "works extraordinarily well" (Gemini); as a strict allegory it "fails", as an ironic one it is "quite strong" (DeepSeek).
- *The perspective.* Put as a question, it made every reader check the geometry. Three found that the road and the pavement pass the billboard. Gemini found that it closes the view optically: "a visual stopper". DeepSeek found that it "masks the central horizon but does not seal the space".
- *The light, new, in none of the three analyses.* The billboard faces away from the sun yet is the brightest surface, and it casts no shadow: "It reads less like a lit surface than like a source… it impersonates [the sun]" (Claude), "a false sun—self-luminous" (DeepSeek).
- *517b.* Claude applied Plato's own key: the sunlit visible world is the cave and the sun its fire. Then the only place where the Good could show is the white rectangle, "and that is where Lenin stands".
- *The return,* missing from all of our pictures, all four found in Lenin himself: "The returning philosopher is only a picture" (ChatGPT), "the liberator became a shadow on the wall" (Claude).
- *The viewer.* Three made the viewer, or the observer who would read the walkers as prisoners, a main target of the irony: "We are not enlightened philosophers watching prisoners; we are simply the next pedestrians in line on Krasikov Street" (Gemini). The prompt said where the viewer stands, but not that this could be the point.

**What this adds.**
- A picture can be built more like the cave than any of our illustrations — people facing an image, the light ahead of them — and still not be read as the cave until the cave is named. The neutral question decides recognition; the named question shows how far a picture can be read.
- A disputed point put as a question makes the readers look, as my mistaken analysis made the directors look.
- The irony our series never got, irony at the viewer, appears when the picture puts the viewer in the cave.

## 14. The two series and their titles (11 November)

*Added on 11 November, at the researcher's request. The titles are the researcher's choice from proposals by the readers, by Gemini and by me. The list of real series at the end is my answer to the researcher's question, translated from Russian.*

**Why a series.** The researcher's observation: the first picture after Wright, `65`, works poorly on its own, but as part of a series it becomes much more interesting. Test 93 bears this out (section 11). Nobody chose `65` as the best, yet the three readers who discussed it called it the starting point, and all four read the four pictures as one story.

My reason is an old one. Lessing's *Laocoon* (1766, the year of Wright's *Orrery*) says that painting shows a single moment and poetry an action in time, so a painter has to choose the most pregnant moment. Plato's parable is a story in stages:
- the prisoners are in chains;
- one is freed and turned to the fire;
- he is dragged up, his eyes adjust, and he sees the sun;
- he goes back down.

`65` shows the first stage, where nothing has happened yet: everyone sits, and nobody has turned. Without what follows, that stage looks like any lecture, and the readers saw in it the Enlightenment and a planetarium. In a series the neighbouring picture points at what has changed. For the fork in the manga a hint had to do that work (section 11). On its own the strip of daylight in `65` is just lighting; next to the night version it is a sun that may not be there.

**The order.** In test 93 the order was the researcher's (`65`, night, desert, space), and its effect was not tested. In the order of the parable the five pictures tell almost all of it:
1. `65`: the prisoners.
2. The desert: the camera pulls back. The cave is a set, and the chains hold by agreement.
3. The meadow: one listener has turned (515c), and there are shadows on the wall.
4. Space: the ascent, and above it a sun on a lever.
5. The night: another ending, not going out but putting out the fire.

The three pull-backs, `65` → desert → space, work like Duane Michals's photo sequence *Things Are Queer* (1973), where each frame shows that the one before was inside something bigger. Only the return (516e–517a) is missing. The diorama has it: the cleaner.

**The diorama.** It was one of the new pictures that came out of the directors' answers about Bulatov (section 13).
- *The idea* is Gemini's, working without a director prompt: a door cut into the painted landscape of a museum diorama, opening onto a utility room. Gemini aimed it against Plato himself, since the golden light belongs to the illusion.
- *The composition* is the researcher's rough mockup.
- *The finished painting* and three local edits of it were made by ChatGPT in Codex.

All four versions share one scene:
- *The hall and the panorama.* Seven visitors stand behind a rope barrier in a dim natural-history museum and look into a sunlit painted grassland.
- *The door.* On the right a service door in the painted hillside stands open. Its outer face carries the same painted grass. Behind it is a concrete room with an electrical cabinet, a fire extinguisher, a small bulb and a mop in a bucket.
- *Nobody looks at the door.* In the foreground a father holds a boy by the hand.

The versions differ in one detail each:
- *Version 1:* no gesture.
- *Version 2:* the boy points up and to the left, at the landscape (a control).
- *Version 3:* the boy points at the door.
- *Version 4:* a cleaner stands in the doorway, and nobody notices her.

**Real series like ours.** The researcher asked whether real artists had made series like these. This is my answer, from memory, translated from Russian. Two facts I checked with a web search: the cave in Magritte's 1935 version and Wright's companion pieces. Each series repeats only part of what we did.

*One scene, one change, and the meaning only in the series:*
- **Thomas Cole, *The Course of Empire* (1833–36).**
  - *What it is:* five paintings of one valley with the same crag and its boulder. What changes is the civilisation around them: the savage state, the pastoral, the consummation of empire, destruction, desolation.
  - *Why it is close:* one by one the paintings are landscapes; together they are a parable. In structure this is the closest to the diorama: a fixed frame, and a story told by what changes.
  - *The difference:* in Cole everything around the crag changes, while in the diorama one detail changes in a still crowd.
- **René Magritte, *The Human Condition* (*La Condition humaine*, 1933–35).**
  - *What it is:* a painting on an easel covers exactly the view it shows. Magritte painted several compositions under this title. In the 1935 version at Norwich Castle the easel stands in the mouth of a cave ([Art UK](https://artuk.org/discover/stories/rene-magrittes-la-condition-humaine)).
  - *Why it is close:* this is the most direct case I know: a cave, an image in place of the world, several versions.
  - *The difference:* there are no viewers in the picture. The prisoner is whoever looks at the canvas.

*Artificial light in place of the sun:*
- **Magritte, *The Empire of Light* (*L'Empire des lumières*, from the late 1940s, many versions in oil and gouache).** A night street with a lit street lamp under a daylight sky. The lamp and the sun share one picture: the opposition of our series after Wright, without people. The versions differ little, so this is a series of persistence rather than a story.
- **Wright himself: the Girandola and Vesuvius.** The Girandola was the annual fireworks display over the Castel Sant'Angelo in Rome. Wright noticed its likeness to the eruptions of Vesuvius and painted companion pieces ([Revolutionary Players](https://www.revolutionaryplayers.org.uk/?p=2118)). A natural fire beside a man-made one is, in effect, his own "lamp in place of the sun". This reading is mine; the sources speak only of the pairing.
- **Hiroshi Sugimoto, *Theaters* (from 1978).** A cinema is photographed with the shutter open for the whole film. The screen comes out as an empty white rectangle, and of the film only the light is left. It is almost literally the cave, though the audience is not visible.

*The museum and its viewers as the subject:*
- **Sugimoto, *Dioramas* (from 1976, the American Museum of Natural History).** The stuffed animals of museum dioramas are photographed so that they look alive. The titles are dry, like labels (*Polar Bear*, 1976), the strategy of Bulatov's *Krasikov Street*. In Sugimoto the illusion works on the viewer of the photograph; we show visitors who have given in to it.
- **Thomas Struth, *Museum Photographs* (from 1989).** Crowds stand in front of paintings in great museums, often with their backs to us. The subject is the people who look, like our seven visitors before the diorama.
- **Hubert Robert, a pair of 1796:** *Project for the Arrangement of the Grande Galerie of the Louvre* and *Imaginary View of the Grande Galerie of the Louvre in Ruins*. The same museum hall is shown now and after the end. This is irony at a permanent exhibition more than two centuries before our title.

*Titles that carry what cannot be seen:*
- **William Hogarth, *A Harlot's Progress* (1732), *A Rake's Progress* (1735), *Marriage A-la-Mode* (1743–45).** The series are read in order, the moral is hidden in the details of the background (the pictures on the walls, the objects), and the titles are ironic. Above all, the story is made *between* the plates. In the same way our readers read that the boy gave up, though nobody drew it.
- **Magritte and the Brussels surrealists.** Many of his titles were found not by Magritte but by his friends, Louis Scutenaire above all, after the painting was done. This is the closest to the third question of the diorama test, where the readers give the titles.
- **Vasily Vereshchagin, *The Apotheosis of War* (1871).** The picture is a pyramid of skulls, and the irony is entirely in the dedication: "to all great conquerors, past, present and future". The words change the meaning of the picture.

*Already in this report* (section 12):
- Erik Bulatov's *Horizon* (1971–72), where the horizon is blocked by an order ribbon;
- Komar and Melamid's *People's Choice* (1994–97), whose "most wanted" landscape is nearly our meadow.

I know of no series that does all of it at once: names Plato's cave in its title and hides the inversion of the parable in a detail seen only at a second look. The closest are Magritte's cave version (the cave, but hardly a series) and Cole (a series, but no cave).

## Limits

- One answer per model per test; no repeated runs. "4 of 4" describes four answers, not a probability.
- The named-concept question already says that a known concept is depicted; tests 52–54 show how much it carries by itself.
- Between the cave pictures several things changed at once (image generator, darkness, clothing, the beam); tests 59–62 separate some of them, not all. From test 48 the ChatGPT reader is a newer model, and from test 59 the Gemini reader is a newer model too (Argon 4 High).
- Hint steps, the questions about Repin and the 20th century, and the suggested author and frames of test 63 give the reader part of the answer; their results show how far a hint carries, not what the picture says.
- The search for a source was done by the same family of models that made and read the pictures; the art-historical literature was not searched by a person.
- Tests 67–72: the first question gives the Cave as fact and supplies Plato's objection; the Pelevin hint of test 69 brings a ready reading; in test 71 several things change at once, and the question about register, which test 72 isolates, comes close to naming the device. Their mode notes were added afterwards; ChatGPT searched the web in tests 69–71.
- The objection that the people look at the lamp, not at shadows, was never put to the readers as such; asked about accuracy, Claude raised it in both runs (tests 74–75), Gemini in one. Test 74 allowed web search, and two readers found the researcher's Stack Exchange question about the picture; test 75, without search, suggests that their sharper verdicts came from it.
- Tests 90–92 change one thing each, with one answer per model; their question speaks of "a parable", which narrows the search, and the directors' recommended question, "What is depicted?", was not asked. Test 93 points out the blowing out, which favours picture 2. Test 94 changes three things at once compared with test 90 (the meadow, the light and the shadows, the listener at the window), so their effects cannot be fully separated. Test 95 lists details of the composition taken from three analyses (one of them mine), so its second answer shows interpretation given those observations; three readers recognised the painting. The mode notes of tests 90–95 were added afterwards. The request to the directors was drafted by me, and its list of problems pushed toward the literal fixes they proposed.
- Section 14 and test 96: the second question names the cave and offers the series, so only the sequences of question 1 are unprompted; the titles come after two rounds of analysis; the order of the four versions was not varied; the Claude reader is the same model as the author of my titles. The chosen titles are a choice, not a test result: none of them has been shown to a reader, and the order of the series after Wright is my proposal and was not tested. The list of real series is from memory, with two facts checked.
