# **78 visualisations of the programmer-cat joke**

This joke has far more visual possibilities than simply “a cat talks to a waiter and then says something unexpected”. It can become an everyday comedy, an absurdist cartoon, a story about professional identity, a satire on the labour market, a fairy tale, or even a historical allegory.

I suggest treating it as a **construction kit of independent decisions**. First I will arrange possible interpretations along axes, including additional axes that substantially change the image. Then I will give **78 specific concepts — six in each of the 13 stipulated styles**.

These are not 78 slightly different drawings, but 78 independent stagings of the scene. Their elements can be combined to produce many more variants.

## **Part I. A complete system of axes**

### **Axis 1. Reading the joke**

The most important differences lie here: they determine *exactly why* viewers will find it funny.

**What the cat understands**

| Reading | How the visualisation changes |
|---|---|
| 1. The cat sees nothing unusual about herself | Her utterly calm behaviour contrasts with the shaken waiter |
| 2. The cat knows that talking cats are rare | She is accustomed to people's reactions and already irritated |
| 3. The cat genuinely loves the circus | She dreamily imagines acrobats and cotton candy but cannot see what work there would suit a programmer |
| 4. The cat considers herself a programmer first and foremost | Her profession matters more than her biological species |
| 5. The cat is a tired IT specialist | She came to take a break from work, only to receive another job offer |
| 6. The cat is professionally arrogant | She considers work as a circus animal beneath her qualifications |
| 7. The cat is pragmatic | She has nothing against a circus if it has interesting programming tasks |
| 8. The cat does not understand that they want to make her an attraction | To her, the invitation looks like an ordinary vacancy |
| 9. The cat understands the waiter's intentions perfectly | Her last line is a deliberately sarcastic refusal |
| 10. The cat has only recently learnt to program | She is still finding her professional identity |
| 11. The cat is an outstanding specialist | The circus job offer looks especially absurd against her achievements |
| 12. The cat lives in a world where animals commonly work | It is the waiter's stereotyped thinking, rather than the talking cat, that proves absurd |

**What the waiter wants**

| Reading | How the visualisation changes |
|---|---|
| 1. To help sincerely | He offers an opportunity that seems wonderful to him |
| 2. To make money | He sees the cat as a source of money, fame or commission |
| 3. To help his uncle | He tries to save his relative's loss-making circus |
| 4. To become famous | He already imagines newspaper headlines about his discovery |
| 5. To demonstrate resourcefulness | He considers himself a talented recruiter |
| 6. To get rid of an unusual customer | He hopes to send the talking cat far away |
| 7. To test the miracle | The job offer is a way to make the cat keep talking |
| 8. To exploit a connection | Above all, he wants to stress: “My uncle is the director!” |
| 9. To delight customers | He thinks of a circus as a place where the cat will be properly appreciated |
| 10. To find a performer | He acts on the director's direct instructions |
| 11. To change his own profession | He dreams of becoming a circus agent, starting with the cat |
| 12. To preserve his familiar worldview | Despite her profession, he cannot imagine a talking cat outside a circus |

**What the circus means**

| Reading | Meaning of the image |
|---|---|
| 1. An ordinary circus | A literal interpretation, as faithful to the text as possible |
| 2. A romantic circus from childhood dreams | The cat imagines a celebration, colourful tents, lights and sweets |
| 3. Circus as show business | Everyone sees the cat as a potential celebrity |
| 4. An old-fashioned circus | A clash between traditional entertainment and a modern profession |
| 5. A loss-making circus | An awkward attempt to find an incredible act that will save the business |
| 6. Circus as an office metaphor | The joke expands into a satire on corporate life |
| 7. Circus as a labour-market metaphor | Someone wants to use a talented specialist for the wrong purpose |
| 8. Circus as a technological organisation | Programmers may genuinely be needed there, but the waiter does not think of that |
| 9. Circus as a fantasy world | It exists only in the cat's imagination |
| 10. Circus as a huge absurd bureaucracy | It has more administrations and job titles than performers |
| 11. Circus as a competitor to the IT industry | The two professions are compared through absurdity |
| 12. Circus as a symbol of other people's expectations | The cat is offered what a cat is supposedly “meant” to do, rather than what she wants |

**Who the uncle is**

Possible choices: a completely unseen character; a director on a poster; a fat, good-natured man in a top hat; an impoverished entrepreneur; a harsh businessman; a caricature boss; a distant relative whom the waiter barely knows; a director personally present in the café; a man who mistakenly takes all animals for performers; a talking tomcat himself; a boss everyone fears; an invented relative the waiter uses to cover his own initiative.

A separate visual choice is **whether to show the uncle at all**. His absence keeps the joke compact, while his appearance opens up an additional comic thread.

**The waiter**

Young and enthusiastic, elderly and bewildered, pompous, phlegmatic, obliging, intrusive, cunning, tired, impeccably trained, or utterly incompetent.

He may be human or an anthropomorphic animal. In appearance: a classic apron, dinner jacket, diner uniform, period costume, or even a circus waistcoat if his connection with the uncle is to be stressed.

**Customers**

- The café is completely empty: an intimate comic scene.
- Customers notice nothing: the absurdity becomes even stronger.
- Everyone is stunned: the cat becomes the centre of attention.
- Children are delighted, adults frightened.
- Every customer has a laptop: a hint of an IT setting arises.
- Customers are unusual themselves: aliens, fairy-tale creatures, talking animals.
- The circus director sits among the customers, watching the conversation.
- Someone films the cat on a phone.
- Another customer also turns out to be a programmer.
- Only cats occupy the establishment, and the waiter is the sole human.

### **Axis 4. What happened before and what happens afterwards**

The joke can be shown not just at the moment of conversation but as a small story.

**Before the conversation**

1. The cat finished a hard working day.
2. She has just been dismissed from an IT company.
3. She has just been promoted.
4. That morning she successfully fixed a difficult bug.
5. She is coming from a professional conference.
6. For the first time, she has dared to order coffee in a human café.
7. The waiter has just received a call from his uncle.
8. The uncle has asked him to find an unusual performer.
9. Strange things have been happening in the café all day.
10. The cat is developing software for an entirely different circus.
11. She is on her way to a job interview.
12. She merely stepped out for a pastry, expecting no adventures.

**After the conversation**

1. The waiter brings the order after all.
2. The cat calmly returns to programming.
3. The waiter feels awkward.
4. He apologises and asks for her CV.
5. The cat agrees to write software for the circus.
6. The uncle offers her the job of technical director.
7. The cat arrives at the circus and discovers complete chaos.
8. The waiter offers her another unsuitable job.
9. Another talking cat appears, also a programmer.
10. The circus becomes an IT company.
11. The cat opens her own café.
12. Nothing happens: the conversation is instantly forgotten.

The continuation may appear in the comic's final panel, in a small epilogue, or in background details. But if retaining the joke's original impact matters, **the final programmer line should remain the climax**, rather than becoming the opening of a new story.

## **Part II. Catalogue of 78 variants**

## **1. Manga**

Variants 1–6. Emotional contrast, expressive faces, dynamic storyboarding, mental images.

**1. Classic four-panel joke.** Four vertical panels: order, open-mouthed waiter, excited uncle explanation, composed profession statement. His exaggerated emotions and her nearly unchanged face make contrast the entire joke.

**2. Epic response to an ordinary cat.** In a large city café he treats speech as an incredible discovery: dramatic angles, speed lines, eye close-ups, grandiose imagined performance. Last panel abruptly shows an ordinary bespectacled cat with work backpack calmly naming her profession.

**3. Romantic circus fantasy.** A young fluffy white cat at a window; his words make acrobats, lollipops, candy and lights blossom around her. Finally dreamy eyes turn thoughtful as she remembers her programming work. Radiance, flowers and a gentle emotional shift.

**4. Tired programmer after work.** Black hoodie-and-backpack cat in a night café, enormous eye shadows; earlier panels show endless messages and error screens. His delighted additional job offer receives a look suggesting the most absurd proposal imaginable. Entirely expressionless final line.

**5. Prodigy kitten.** A tiny ginger kitten barely reaches the cup; he sees an adorable wonder and imagines posters. Finally the kitten confidently opens a complex project on a laptop and asks the professional question. Childlike appearance contrasts with professional adulthood.

**6. Two worlds in a spread.** Left: he imagines her performing miracles in a grand ring. Right: she imagines herself at a computer among monitors, diagrams and notes. Their thoughts overlap above coffee centrally; the last line crystallises incompatible ideas of one character.

## **2. Twentieth-century French magazine cartoon**

Variants 7–12. Economical expressive drawing, subtle social observation, elegant absurdity.

**7. Impassive cat on a Paris terrace.** Tiny tabby, elegant cup, long thin waiter bending with exaggerated astonishment. Confident lines give wicker chairs, windows, oblivious passers-by. Her little final balloon seems entirely mundane.

**8. Huge waiter, miniature professional.** Long arms, open mouth, flying apron and raised brows fill almost the image. Below, a tiny grey cat on a high chair is barely visible behind coffee, yet delivers the decisive line. His agitation's scale also contrasts with events' actual importance to her.

**9. Circus opposite the café.** A huge window reveals tent and acrobat poster, making his offer natural. She looks poster-to-waiter, thoughtfully raises an ear and asks. Minimal extraneous detail; subtle compositional irony.

**10. Napkin drawings.** In a two-part cartoon, he draws a ring cat while she diagrams software on another napkin. Adjacent napkins on a round table reveal incompatible plans simultaneously. Faces remain remarkably calm for the absurd conversation.

**11. Everyone wants a use for a miracle.** Crowded customers watch the offer and imagine advertising, television, theatre or street performances. Alone oblivious to the excitement, she waits for pastry. Her line makes their fantasies an absurd attempt to control another's profession.

**12. Lunch interrupted.** Elegant calico cat, newspaper, neatly folded napkin; his surprise has dropped his notebook. Irritated, she raises her head and asks. Much empty space; the true centre is an almost unnoticed impatiently tapping tail.

## **3. Minimalist webcomic**

Variants 13–18. Simple figures, limited palette, short text, precise rhythm.

**13. Four squares, one turn.** “Coffee with cream”, “You speak?!”, offer, final line. Oval-with-ears cat and few-line waiter in equal squares emphasise the last one's surprise.

**14. Expectation and reality.** His imagined pedestal performer, applause, lights and money; then actual café cat, cup and “I'm a programmer”. Comedy reduces to two simple conflicting images.

**15. Two identical faces.** Five almost identical panels change only his face from ordinary to ecstatic; hers stays fixed. Finally he stops smiling. Minimalism reveals who considers this unusual.

**16. Growing speech balloon.** His balloon expands: uncle, director, circus, work, fame, success. She shrinks against enthusiasm until her short final balloon appears, restoring simple space immediately.

**17. Almost wordless comic.** Coffee pictograms order; he points amazedly and mimes circus/ring. She opens code on a laptop and spreads puzzled paws. The joke's final line is the sequence's only text.

**18. Endless coffee wait.** After her order, he surprises, dreams and talks at length. She never changes pose; her place stays empty. Final profession line has a small addition: “And where's my coffee?”

## **4. American newspaper political cartoon**

Variants 19–24. Grotesque social satire, explanatory labels, symbolic figures and emphatic contradictions.

**19. Qualifications problem.** “Recruiter” on him, “Labour Market” on the wall, “Programmer” on her pass. He points to “Talking Animal Vacancies”, ignoring profession. The line satirises recruitment by appearances.

**20. Talent shortage.** “Everyone Seeks Talented Staff” headline in a cartoon café. He offers circus to the sole talking cat, ignoring a briefcase stuffed with technical diplomas and certificates. Society sees strangeness but not competence.

**21. Uncle and connections.** A huge smug “Uncle” director sits at the back near a circus portrait; the waiter desperately seeks him a star. Arrows and labels explain family interests. She is the sole rational human — or rather rational cat — asking an unanswerable professional question.

**22. Talent industry.** Agent-like waiter lures her into a giant “Show Business” funnel, beyond which are posters, advertising contracts and ticket offices. Her little “Software Development” sign highlights how swiftly another's exceptionality becomes merchandise.

**23. Bureaucratic circus.** “Administration”, “Performers' Committee” and “Miracles Commission” signs protrude from a tent. He promises wonderful work; its poster already requires dozens of approvals. Her programming question challenges this absurd organisation's structure.

**24. Professional against stereotype.** Giant central cardboard cat labelled “Unusual Circus Act”; actual small laptop-and-diploma cat behind. He addresses cardboard, missing the living interlocutor. Her balloon returns imposed role to profession.

## **5. Noir comic**

Variants 25–30. Deep shadows, rain, anxiety, cinematic angles and dry humour.

**25. Rainy night café.** Black cat by rain-and-sign-lit window, tired waiter polishing glasses. Surprise briefly breaks silence; he offers circus work. Amid thick shadows the last line is as serious as solving a crime.

**26. Dubious business offer.** Across an empty café table he places his mysterious uncle's card. In his imagination it is the deal of the century; the card resembles evidence. She pushes it away and asks a simple professional question.

**27. Circus reflected in glass.** Back to a huge dark window reflecting his face and phantom tent, she listens. Acrobats and spotlights fill the reflection as though circus already pursues her. In reality she remains with coffee, dryly stating profession.

**28. Private detective who codes.** Trench coat, stern gaze, old case, night café. Inner narration explains she wanted only coffee and pastry after hard work, not his sudden career. The final panel opens the case: laptop, cables, technical documents.

**29. Interrogation instead of service.** Harsh overhead light on him, suspect-like cat in shadow. “Are you a cat? Do you speak? Do you need work?” becomes anxious interrogation close-ups. Her final question reverses roles: “Why does the circus need a programmer?”

**30. Dangerous circus secret.** Gloomy venue, whispered mysterious uncle and extraordinary terms. Briefly she imagines secret-contract intrigue, then calmly produces a work pass. A detective-story opening abruptly becomes ordinary misunderstanding.

## **6. English children's book illustration**

Variants 31–36. Cosy fairy tale, lively characters, soft watercolour, objects and warm humour.

**31. Morning in a little tearoom.** Fluffy grey cat on a cushion at a round table, tea service, pastry, cream jug. A freckled young waiter amazedly explains his uncle's circus. Her modest waistcoat and reply show adult judgement.

**32. Cat with briefcase.** Good-natured bespectacled tabby enters a village café with neat leather case. He dreams of artistic careers; she reveals laptop, work papers and homemade sandwich. A serious fairy-tale resident offered entirely the wrong profession.

**33. The world's loveliest circus.** Above a white cat, enormous colourful tents, merry jugglers, pastries and candy. Dreamily narrowing eyes, she seems to smell caramel and hear music. Returning, she asks how a programmer could help.

**34. An unusual day in a tomcat's life.** Small spread episodes: ginger tomcat leaves with work bag, walks, enters café, converses. Main table scene shows his surprised spread arms. Surrounding miniatures make a full fairy-tale story.

**35. Surprise through a child's eyes.** A girl with her mother notices speech first. While he explains circus, she studies the laptop and understands his mistake. The ending is also funny because a child understood before adults.

**36. Book of opposed worlds.** Left: cosy coffee shop, order, surprise. Right: vast imagined circus of colourful figures; her strict work waistcoat looks out of place. Her short question links pages and concludes.

## **7. Soviet children's book illustration**

Variants 37–42. Clear composition, expressive drawing, familiar everyday details, friendly irony.

**37. Pastry cafeteria.** Ginger cat, little Soviet café table, faceted water glass, saucer and appetising pastry. White-aproned waiter widens eyes; customers keep talking. Her serious, slightly hurt answer reflects ignored competence.

**38. Engineer tomcat on a business trip.** Tabby with leather bag, glasses and neat tie enters a station buffet. He is shown a bright poster as an attractive prospect. A serious technical specialist cannot understand why he should perform.

**39. Programming kitten.** A young black kitten with large eyes sits with cocoa as he offers circus fame. A little briefcase of programming notebooks occupies the next chair; bright poster on wall. Polite, curious professional question.

**40. Circus across the square.** Café window overlooks broad square, circus building, flags and balloon-holding children. His genuine enthusiasm briefly interests her; she then looks at her work bag and asks.

**41. Serious cat, frivolous adult.** Neat, independent, focused positive children's-book heroine with work papers. He waves enthusiastically, forgetting service. Gentle pedagogical irony: ask someone's occupation before offering work.

**42. Profession unexpectedly useful.** Classic conversation and puzzled programmer question precede a small closing image at a circus-lighting control panel. Such a specialist was useful after all. Misunderstanding leads to useful work, unanticipated by him.

## **8. Naive art**

Variants 43–48. Conventional perspective, decorative colours, simple shapes, sincerity and fantastic directness.

**43. Huge cat, tiny table.** She occupies almost all the bright flat image; waiter, chairs and pastries are tiny. Equal care without realistic proportions lends importance and grandeur, making his offer especially absurd.

**44. Professional-cat café.** Dozens of cats in a colourful hall: book, medical bag, instrument, laptop. He is amazed by the one ordering coffee and offers circus work. Her question exposes a world of professional cats inexplicably unnoticed by humans.

**45. Three worlds in one drawing.** Little café central, enormous striped circus left, computer-workers' city of monitor-shaped houses right. At their boundary she sits; he points with paw — rather, hand — circusward. A fairy-tale map of others' expectations versus one's own work.

**46. One cat, many moments.** The same white cat repeatedly enters, orders, listens, dreams and answers across a flat picture. No borders; one decorative narrative. Naive manner makes temporal illogic natural.

**47. Everything mentioned.** Coffee, pastries, laptop, ticket, candy, lollipops, keyboard and toy trapeze surround her on an enormous table. Amazed waiter behind; she examines the impossible collection. One symbolic still life unites the joke.

**48. Circus on the Moon.** A café on a small multicoloured planet overlooks a lunar tent. Ginger bespectacled cat orders coffee; he proposes visiting his uncle there. Incredible travel disturbs her less than changing profession.

## **9. Narrative painting of the old masters**

Variants 49–54. Historical composition, expressive light, complex gestures, allegory and lofty artistic pathos around mundane absurdity.

**49. Dramatic tavern scene.** Light picks out coffee-goblet cat and shocked waiter amid shadowed others. His gesture solemnly announces fateful news. Almost philosophically calm, she asks about profession.

**50. Dutch domestic scene.** Chequered floor, wooden furniture, brassware, tiny table cat. He bends; a customer reads a letter behind; her bag and modern keyboard lie below. Thorough everyday credibility makes profession the unexpected anachronism.

**51. Allegory of vocation.** Central cat, Circus personification left with masks, ribbons and trapeze, Science right with drawings, books and calculators. Mediating waiter insists on Circus; she chooses profession, making high allegory funny with a simple question.

**52. Baroque feast.** Dressed-up guests amid silver, fruit, pies and goblets stare at a tiny cat. He theatrically points to a poster as though offering royal title. Amid public shock, she cares only about professional terms.

**53. Still life, conversation behind.** Luxurious foreground cup, cream jug, pastry, book, keyboard, little ticket. A metal-pot reflection reveals open-mouthed waiter and cat. Conflicting objects create the joke; a small caption supplies the line.

**54. Vocation and temptation.** At her book and calculating device she sees a glowing festive performer procession outside. He points like someone diverting a hero from their path. Interested, she still asks; majestic composition clashes with modern everyday humour.

## **10. Impressionism**

Variants 55–60. Light, air, reflected colours, transience and everyday atmosphere outweigh precise drawing.

**55. Sunny terrace.** White cat under striped awning, shimmering sunlight and greenish shadows. He leans in amazement; street life continues unchanged. Concise programmer caption lets painting convey the unexpected conversation's atmosphere.

**56. Rainy city café.** Warm golden lamp reflections inside, blurred blue-violet passers-by outside. Grey cat answers a partially rendered surprised figure. Peacefully mundane treatment of impossibility produces humour.

**57. Sunset circus lights.** A lit tent dissolves into orange and lilac dusk outside. Dreamily pleased, she watches; he seems to invite her into festivity. A short question caption returns dream to earthly reality.

**58. Dream mixed with reality.** Both spaces seem simultaneous: candy colour merges with steam, ring lights with café lamps. At this painterly transition she interestedly gazes into space. Short text ends the dream with practicality.

**59. Station café.** A tired tomcat with small suitcase sits amid light and fleeting passengers/waiter. Coffee during a transfer receives a new-life offer. The line contrasts airy transience with professional certainty.

**60. Seen across the street.** As though through an artist's eye, window barely reveals cat and gestures. Passers-by, reflections, leaves and traffic occupy nearly equal space. The caption reinterprets an unnoticed incident within a living city.

## **11. Japanese ukiyo-e print**

Variants 61–66. Clear outlines, flat colours, decorative patterns, expressive asymmetry, historical stylisation.

**61. Edo teahouse cat.** Tabby on cushion at low table and cup; amazed attendant bows. Travelling-circus poster behind; modern laptop deliberately anachronistic. Small question lettering in empty background.

**62. Night conversation by a river.** White cat in a teahouse overlooking bridge and river receives a troupe invitation. Festive lanterns and acrobat silhouettes reflect in water, making the offer seductive. Her businesslike calm suggests only the poorly considered vacancy is unusual.

**63. Three-thought triptych.** Left: talking cat and astonished waiter; centre: colourful circus dream; right: laptop table. Decorative clouds link actual and imagined; the final right-side line establishes reading order.

**64. Poster as principal character.** A large engraved circus poster, fantastic performers and expressive director portrait hangs inside. He offers work by pointing; she studies it sceptically. None of the advertised acts relates to programming.

**65. Two expressive portraits.** Almost theatrical close-ups: stunned man with arched brows and delight, composed cat looking ahead with slightly closed eyes. Carefully stylised faces turn dialogue into conflicting personalities.

**66. Wave of offers.** A gigantic decorative wave of posters, tickets, candy, masks and promised fame rises behind him. The small cat sits solidly with coffee and laptop. Grand offers break against calm professional identity.

## **12. Soviet satirical magazine Krokodil**

Variants 67–72. Social grotesque, expressive faces, exposing captions and object-based satire.

**67. Family patronage.** His extraordinary zeal contrasts with an influential-looking director uncle's portrait. A table note requests “Find an extraordinary act”. Calmly finishing coffee, she asks why the family business needs a programmer.

**68. Where to put specialists?** Qualified bespectacled cat with technical-document folder. Without looking, he offers circus performance beside “We Need Talent!” Satire of assigning people by appearance while ignoring speciality.

**69. Business in miracles.** His thoughts fill with takings, tickets, queues and happy uncle. Coin-like eyes, grasping hands towards future profit. Slowly drinking, she destroys commercial dreams with programming.

**70. Amazing-performer quota.** A recruitment-plan chart needs exactly one talking cat. He puts her in the report, uninterested in skills. The question opposes meaningless targets and a real person — here a cat.

**71. Where is the real circus?** Outside, offices, officials, documents, “Do Not Enter” signs; almost empty ring but administrative chaos. His enthusiastic work description meets sceptical requests for actual duties.

**72. Identify a specialist by the tail?** Her complex system diagram lies on the table, yet he points only at tail and ears as grounds for circus work. “Do not judge profession by appearance” poster in corner; her line illustrates it briefly and sharply.

## **13. Ancient Egyptian painting**

Variants 73–78. Strict flatness, profiles, hierarchical scale, registers, ornament and anachronistic humour.

**73. Palace guest of honour.** Cat with drink bowl and pastry in Egyptian banquet hall; tray servant raises a surprised hand. Conventional signs convey public-performance offer. Caption gives profession; modern laptop by paws deliberately disrupts antiquity.

**74. Three horizontal registers.** Arrival and order above, servant's explanation middle, answer below. Acrobat, candy and computer symbols between; a wall chronicle of an astonishing event.

**75. Modern-era feline scribe.** Solemn scholarly posture, scrolls and writing tools, but laptop replaces papyrus. He offers artistry at the director's court. Ancient scholarship, modern technology and circus work form the central contrast.

**76. Pharaoh-sized uncle.** Egyptian waiter points at a director much larger than everyone else by hierarchical scale. Below sits a small confident coffee cat, unimpressed, asking only about programming duties.

**77. Weighing a vocation.** Mask, lollipop and little trapeze in one pan; keyboard, algorithm scroll and laptop in the other. Beside her, he tips towards circus. Her line asserts professional identity within solemn ritual.

**78. Talking-cat fresco for posterity.** Grand wall composition repeats figures through arrival, director's offer and question. Decorative text, ornaments, circus symbols and computing devices accompany scenes. A trivial anecdote commemorated like a major historical event creates humour.

### **B. Organising the climax**

**1. Hidden profession**

No programming clues before the ending. Last panel first reveals laptop or pass alongside her line.

**2. Obvious profession**

Laptop visible initially. Viewers understand the offer's absurdity before him and await exposure.

**3. Parallel conceptions**

He imagines circus in one half, she a workstation in the other. Viewers see both incompatible perspectives simultaneously.

**4. Broken expectations**

Rich circus fantasy fills almost everything; a tiny realistic cat stays in a corner. Her line makes fantasy excessive and funny.

### **V. The principal contrast**

Twelve further independent ideas vary the comic mechanism rather than style.

1. **Miracle versus routine.** He sees a miracle; she an ordinary coffee break.
2. **Biology versus profession.** He cares about species; she qualifications.
3. **Romance versus pragmatism.** Tents, candy and music versus duties.
4. **Grandeur versus triviality.** He promises global fame; she still wants pastry.
5. **Fantasy versus logic.** His grand act meets her precise task question.
6. **Enthusiasm versus fatigue.** He celebrates discovery; she barely survives workday.
7. **Big versus small.** Enormous human reaction to miniature cat.
8. **Tradition versus modernity.** Old circus, acrobats and trainers meet developer profession.
9. **Stereotype versus individuality.** Others see talking cat, miss individual.
10. **Chance versus career.** In seconds he decides a stranger's fate; she already chose her profession.
11. **Literal versus figurative.** A talking cat is invited to circus, while surrounding IT industry itself looks like one.
12. **Fairy tale versus reality.** Everything seems magical until vacancy and qualification arise.

## **Part V. Further variation within any of the 78 variants**

Subtler artistic decisions are easy to overlook.

**Degree of surprise.** Reveal programming early, hide it until the last panel, or give small clues understandable only afterwards.

**Who owns the visual fantasy.** Circus may appear in his imagination, hers, both, or not at all. He imagines celebrity; she sweets and shows; both allow incompatible fantasies.

**Professional detail's role.** Laptop need not be the main symbol: pass, code desktop, mouse, algorithms, unfinished project or characteristic fatigue may work. Conversely, nothing may betray her profession.

**Viewer participation.** Viewers may know everything and watch his mistake; share his surprise at speech; or initially accept circus logic, recognising the error only at the end.

**Cat–human boundary.** The strongest contrast often retains ordinary animal appearance with adult professional behaviour. Full anthropomorphism produces less miraculous, more social humour.

**Misunderstanding's scale.** An intimate conversation or dozens imagining her global fame. Large scale satirises public expectations; small scale precisely contrasts two perspectives.

**A second joke.** A tiny silent epilogue may show coffee arriving, uncle withdrawing contract, laptop opening or another talking cat appearing. It changes rhythm without rewriting the original.

Overall: **78 specific concepts, 17 visualisation axes, 12 principal comic contrasts and additional compositional devices**. These permit different styles and fundamentally different images within a style, from near-literal illustration to multilayered composition.
