Below are 37 variants. Each is placed under its leading axis, with further possibilities for that axis listed before the group. The variants can be freely crossed: a reading from one, a setting from another.

## “Reading” axis

Further possibilities for this axis:
- **The cat:** genuinely does not understand (speech is not a talent to her; her essence lies in her profession); understands everything and puts the waiter in his place; is tired of recruiters and hears yet another offer; seriously considers what software a circus needs; lapses into childhood; considers the circus beneath her dignity; lives in a world where every animal talks.
- **The waiter:** selfless delight; self-interest (a commission or a debt to his uncle); a reflex of finding everyone a place “with our people”; stalling because he does not know whether cats may be served; a failed circus performer; a catcher; sees a species rather than an individual.
- **The circus and uncle:** a literal big top; a metaphor (office, show business, politics); a cage; a child's paradise; the waiter's invention; the circus is already here, in the café; the uncle is an animal himself; the uncle as an offstage force (poster, photograph, telephone).

**1. The cat genuinely does not understand** — *minimalist webcomic.* Four panels, stick figures: the cat is two triangular ears and a squiggly tail, the waiter a little figure with a tray. The third panel is silent, the cat's eyes closed; the fourth has the programmer line. No scenery; the entire joke is in the words and pause.

**2. The cat understands everything** — *twentieth-century French magazine cartoon.* One drawing with a spoken line as its caption: a huge bistro drawn with a fine, trembling line and light watercolour wash. A tiny cat at a marble table does not look up from her newspaper; an obsequiously bent waiter looms above her. A barely perceptible smirk among her whiskers is the only sign that she is mocking him.

**3. The circus as metaphor** — *American newspaper political cartoon.* Dense crosshatching, exaggerated heads and labels on everything: “Specialist” on the cat, “Labour Market” on the waiter's apron, “Show” on the tent outside (alternatives: “High Politics”, “Corporation”). The uncle is a fat man in a top hat leaning out of the tent with a contract. The caption is the final line; the meaning is that spectacle is valued above skill.

**4. The circus as a childhood dream** — *manga.* Small, everyday café panels; then the cat closes her eyes and a borderless spread appears using shōjo devices: sparkles, a floral background, a kitten in the stands with cotton candy, acrobats beneath the big top. A small, dry panel follows, with an unflappable face and the line. The waiter falls out of the panel upside down.

**5. The waiter's last chance** — *noir comic.* A late-night diner, rain, stripes of shadow from blinds; the first-person narration is the waiter's, who owes his uncle money and sees salvation in this customer. The cat is black, wearing a trench coat, her eyes shining beneath her hat brim; the waiter's shadow on the wall has a top hat and whip. The final line sounds like a sentence: the deal has fallen through.

**6. Patronage and service** — *Krokodil.* Instead of bringing the order, a swaggering waiter with a towel over his arm tries to find the customer a job with his uncle; hungry customers languish at neighbouring tables. The cat is a young specialist with a drawing tube and a folder of punched cards. Heavy outlines, flat gouache, dialogue as the caption beneath the drawing; the targets are nepotism, service and employment outside one's speciality.

**7. A deity does not take employment** — *Ancient Egyptian painting.* A canonical offering scene: the cat Bastet on a throne before an offering table, a servant half her height presenting a jug and flatbread. Dialogue is recorded in columns of hieroglyphs; in the lower register are female acrobats and jugglers, as in tomb paintings: the “circus” of the uncle, a nomarch. Heads in profile, shoulders frontal, ochre and turquoise; the servant is astonished not by speech but by the appearance of a goddess whom he is inviting to become a dancer.

**8. The cat is the only logician** — *English children's book illustration.* Victorian pen-and-ink engraving in the tradition of nonsense books: dense hatching, a tea table excessively crowded with crockery, a waiter in tails with bulging eyes. The cat is anatomically accurate but has a human expression of polite bewilderment. The world is absurd here, while her reasoning is impeccable.

## “Characters” axis

Further possibilities for this axis:
- **Breed and colour:** a tabby moggy (at home in any café), Siamese (haughtiness), Persian (a lady), Sphynx (a burnt-out senior developer, also the Egyptian theme), black (noir), tortoiseshell-and-white (Japanese “good luck”), British Shorthair, Maine Coon (larger than the waiter), ginger.
- **Sex and age:** a lady; a junior girl with an enormous backpack; an elderly veteran cat with punched cards; a tomcat in the retelling; indeterminate beneath a hood.
- **Scale of humanisation:** a real cat on a chair → standing on hind legs with a bag → clothed → a human figure with a cat's head → a girl with ears and tail → a woman with feline mannerisms (cat as metaphor) → only a shadow → no cat in the frame (her point of view).
- **The waiter:** a student working part-time, an old moustached waiter, a waitress or buffet attendant, the owner, a former clown, a robot, a penguin in tails, a mouse.
- **Customers:** none; everyone on their phones, noticing nothing; everyone shocked; all animals, with the waiter the only human; circus performers after their shift; the uncle behind a newspaper in the corner; a child who alone is unsurprised; the cat's colleagues; an ordinary silent cat on the windowsill.

**9. Just a cat** — *Soviet children's book illustration.* In the manner of animal illustrators: a fluffy moggy drawn in soft lithographic pencil sits on a chair like a cat, on all fours. Nothing human except her line in the adjacent text. The waiter is a young man in a white jacket with a cup on a tray; the more convincing the animal, the stronger the final line.

**10. A girl with ears** — *manga.* The cat is a nekomimi: a woman programmer in a hoodie, ears sticking out of the hood, tail wrapped around a chair leg. The butler-waiter at a themed café takes her for a cosplayer until her ears twitch. Close-ups of eyes, sweat drops, speed lines on the word “programmer”.

**11. A lady or a cat?** — *Impressionism.* A boulevard café terrace, trembling brushstrokes, sunlight through foliage. At the table is a figure in a fur stole and little hat: a lady from a distance, but close up there are pointed ears among the feathers and a tail beneath the chair. The waiter freezes mid-step, and the viewer repeats his discovery independently.

**12. The cat from the signboard** — *naive art.* A frontal, solemn ginger cat against black, like an animal on an inn sign; she is twice the waiter's size because she is the main character. The table is shown from above so that cup, pastry and laptop are all visible; the final line appears at the top in clumsy letters. No perspective; every hair and button is painted with equal diligence.

**13. Nekomata** — *Japanese ukiyo-e print.* A kimono-clad beauty with a tortoiseshell-and-white cat's head; the shapeshifter's forked tail is visible beneath her hem: speaking comes naturally to her. A teahouse attendant recoils in a theatrical pose, sleeves flying. Clear outlines, flat planes of colour, a corner cartouche with a title and column of text.

**14. The astonished machine** — *minimalist webcomic.* The waiter is a robot trolley with a screen for a face displaying “:O”, “?!” and “loading…” in turn. A talking machine is stunned by a talking cat, giving the joke a second layer. An alternative: the waiter is a penguin in tails whom the programmer cat seems to have seen somewhere before.

**15. Nobody noticed** — *narrative painting of the old masters.* A crowded Netherlandish panorama: a town square on market day, hundreds of tiny figures, travelling players setting up their booth in the distance. The cat and servant at the tavern door must be sought in a lower corner. The miracle is lost in the bustle; exactly one person is surprised.

**16. Everyone here is an animal** — *English children's book illustration.* A watercolour tearoom with a waistcoated hedgehog, a female badger knitting, and rabbits at its tables; the cat in a tweed jacket is an ordinary customer. The only human is the boy waiter on his first working day; he is the odd one here. Soft colours, white margins, accurately drawn animals in clothes.

## “Setting” axis

Further possibilities for this axis: a Parisian bistro or terrace; a Viennese coffee house (coffee with cream and cake is a native order there); an English tearoom; an American diner; a Soviet ice-cream café, canteen, station buffet; a coworking café; an IT-company office kitchen; a cat café; a café-concert or circus refreshment room; a dining car; a Japanese roadside teahouse; a dukhan; a Dutch tavern; an Egyptian beer house or temple courtyard; a 2000s internet café.

**17. Cat café** — *manga.* Yonkoma, a vertical four-panel strip: the cat arrives as a customer where cats are part of the décor, and silent fellow cats look down on her contemptuously from shelves. The waiter decides that the “staff” has spoken, and the circus offer sounds like a promotion. Chibi proportions, an irritation symbol on her forehead.

**18. Coworking café** — *minimalist webcomic.* A long table, a row of identical hunched silhouettes at laptops; one differs only in having ears. The barista passes three times and only notices her when she orders. Everyone here is a programmer, which is why she does not understand the circus offer.

**19. Ice-cream café** — *Soviet children's book illustration.* Cheerful, clear outlines and bright colours: metal sundae dishes, a soda siphon, a rubber plant, a clown poster on the wall. The cat wears a beret, with a roll of punched tape sticking out of her string shopping bag; the waiter is a tousle-haired trainee in a bow tie. A kind drawing without venom: everyone smiles, even the poster.

**20. Bar with a mirror** — *Impressionism.* The viewer occupies the cat's place: before them is a marble counter with bottles and oranges; the waiter looks straight into the frame. The mirror behind him reflects the cat and the smoky café-concert room, while a female trapeze artist's legs dangle in an upper corner. The circus is already here; the cat simply has her back to it.

**21. Roadside teahouse** — *Japanese ukiyo-e print.* A landscape sheet featuring a post station: an awning beside the road, travellers, porters, a mountain in the distance. A travelling cat with a bundle and portable writing kit sits on a bench; across the river is a show booth with flags and a tightrope walker — the very “circus”. The figures are small; the sheet's protagonists are the road and the slanting rain.

**22. The café that is itself a circus** — *naive art.* The striped awning over the entrance resembles a big top; the waiter carries a pyramid of trays; acrobats in tights and a bear in a ruff dine at the tables. The cat with a laptop is the only one who looks ordinary. Bright flat colours; all figures face the viewer as in a family photograph.

## “Before and after” axis

Further possibilities for this axis:
- **Before:** release night and three days without sleep; dismissal or burnout; escaping from a circus and taking programming courses; yesterday people were equally astonished by her at the bank and in a taxi; she has been coming here for a year, but the waiter is new; that morning the uncle complained to his nephew that he lacked a star attraction; a kitten at a children's circus matinee.
- **After:** the order never arrived; cold coffee and a one-star review; she joins the circus as a sysadmin; a “live programmer” act in the ring; the uncle comes himself; the waiter quits and joins the circus or asks to become an apprentice; the next customers; the cat buys the circus; retelling it to colleagues at the morning meeting.

**23. Before: release night** — *manga.* A dark-toned prequel: an empty office, energy-drink cans, bags under the eyes, the clock past three in the morning, finally a green line on the screen. She enters the café only in the last panel, and the waiter's question falls upon a creature with no strength left to be a miracle. Dense screentones, small fragmented panels.

**24. Before: escape from the circus** — *noir comic.* A flashback in sharp black-and-white patches: a cage, a poster reading “A Talking Cat! Only Here!”, a silhouette slipping beneath the tent flap. Then evening classes, a new life, a diner — and the nephew of that very director. The final line becomes a lie to save herself: she knows perfectly well why a circus needs a cat.

**25. After: a live programmer in the ring** — *twentieth-century French magazine cartoon.* Wordless: an enormous ring, a spotlight, within it a tiny desk and a cat typing. Thousands of identical spectators in the half-dark; backstage, the director gnaws his top hat. Fine lines, much empty space, one colour accent — the yellow circle of light.

**26. After: she went after all** — *Soviet children's book illustration.* A book spread: backstage, an elephant, jugglers, a clown — and the cat at a reel-equipped computer setting up ticket sales, while the top-hatted director scratches his head. A happy ending where everyone does their own job. A detailed, cheerful drawing inviting close inspection.

**27. After: the uncle came himself** — *narrative painting of the old masters.* Caravaggesque chiaroscuro: a dark tavern, a slanting beam from the doorway; in the opening, a director in cloak and hat points towards the table. The waiter freezes with a jug, while the cat presses a paw to her chest: “Me? A programmer?” The composition of a calling scene, deep brown shadows, one light source.

**28. After: the cat bought the circus** — *American newspaper political cartoon.* The cat in a top hat, her waistcoat labelled “IT”, holds a hoop marked “KPI”, through which the uncle and waiter jump. The old sign on the tent is crossed out and overwritten with “Under New Management”. Hatching, grotesquerie, a brief venomous caption.

**29. After: the next customers** — *Ancient Egyptian painting.* Following satirical papyri in which animals perform human activities: a long scroll-frieze, a procession approaching the servant — a dog with a law code, an ibis bookkeeper, a baboon physician. He points each one towards the dancers with the same gesture, while the cat walks off the edge of the scroll, a bowl in her paw. Sketchy lines, profile figures, the repeated gesture setting the rhythm.

**30. After: the complaints book** — *Krokodil.* The coffee never came: the waiter is already phoning his uncle from the payphone by the cloakroom, while the cat writes in the complaints book, “Not served. Offered work outside my speciality.” The manager in the corner pretends not to be there. Flat colours, exaggerated types, a feuilleton-like caption.

**31. One table, three times of day** — *Impressionism.* Three canvases from the same viewpoint: in the morning, an empty table in blue shadow; at noon, a ginger patch of cat and white patch of apron; in the evening, a cold cup, a displaced chair and a circus poster on an advertising column in sunset light. The event appears only in the middle canvas; light tells the rest.

## “Objects and details” axis

Further possibilities for this axis:
- **The order:** cream as the only feline weakness; pastry (éclair, tartlet, kartoshka cake, macaron, mille-feuille); cup or saucer.
- **Professional clues:** a sticker-covered laptop, hoodie, conference badge, backpack, rubber duck, headphones, a mug with a joke; in historical styles — punched cards, abacus, slide rule, soroban, a scribe's palette.
- **Puns:** a computer mouse; bugs she catches; a tangle of wires; walking over a keyboard.
- **Circus clues:** a poster, a tent outside the window, the uncle's business card, a framed photograph, top hat, hoop, pedestal, cage, cotton candy, lollipop.
- **With the waiter:** a falling tray, notepad, towel, a fly by his open mouth; a “No Animals Allowed” sign, complaints book, “Help Wanted” notice.
- **Text in the frame:** balloons, captions beneath the drawing, labels, a cartouche, hieroglyphs, sound effects, a chalkboard menu.

**32. Mouse** — *twentieth-century French magazine cartoon.* The cat waits for her order, holding a computer mouse by its cable like prey by the tail; the waiter is now looking at the mouse rather than the cat. Clean line, empty background, the entire joke in a single object. The programmer line serves as the caption.

**33. Two bubbles** — *minimalist webcomic.* A single panel divided in half. In the waiter's bubble, a cat in a tutu jumps through a flaming hoop; in the cat's bubble, a server rack stands between elephants while acrobats string cable beneath the big top. Schematic figures, a caption below.

**34. Still life after departure** — *narrative painting of the old masters.* A Dutch still life without characters: on a dark tablecloth are unfinished coffee, a bitten pastry, an abacus, an open book of formulas, a circus ticket, the uncle's business card and a ginger hair. The side of the silver coffee pot shows a tiny distorted reflection of a man with his mouth open. The whole story can be read through the objects.

**35. Scribe and bugs** — *Ancient Egyptian painting.* The cat sits in the classic scribe's pose, cross-legged with a scroll on her knees; the columns of hieroglyphs on the scroll are indented like code. Scarabs crawl over the papyrus; she pins one with her paw: catching bugs, that is, debugging. Only the servant's astonished raised hands and the edge of his tray enter the frame.

**36. Dream of the circus** — *naive art.* Almost the whole canvas is occupied by what the cat sees with her eyes closed: acrobats hang from branches like fruit, jugglers toss lollipop suns, clouds of cotton candy drift by. She herself is small, in a lower corner on a bentwood chair, while the waiter peeks out of the foliage. Dense decorative greenery and fairy-tale solemnity.

**37. Tea-serving doll** — *Japanese ukiyo-e print.* The cat is a maker of clockwork dolls: beside her are a soroban, a scroll of calculations and a mechanical doll that serves the cup on its own, more briskly than the servant. The servant is doubly astonished. Flat colour, a gear pattern on the kimono, a cartouche with the line.

## Cross-cutting axis: format and point of view

This can be applied to any variant:
- **Format:** a single panel, a 3–4-panel strip, a page, a “what he sees / what she sees” diptych, a silent version, triptych, scroll-frieze, series.
- **Moment for a single panel:** entering, ordering, the waiter frozen, closed eyes, the final line, the silence after it.
- **Point of view:** through the cat's eyes, through the waiter's eyes, from the street through the window, from above, in a mirror.
