The point of the joke is verbal, so the variants differ not only in style but also in where the punchline resides: in a caption, a spoken line, the title of a painting, or a detail noticed last. Below are 34 variants grouped by style.

### Manga

**1. Yonkoma.** A vertical four-panel strip following the kishōtenketsu structure: the cat orders; the waiter is shocked (blank white eyes, a sweat drop); the circus offer and a dreamy squint; the deadpan line about being a programmer. In the last panel, the waiter falls out of the frame with his feet in the air. Chibi-proportioned characters, an almost empty background.

**2. Page with a dream panel.** Small, restrained café panels give way to an enormous panel in a shōjo aesthetic: sparkles, floral screentones, acrobats and lollipops surrounding the cat with shining eyes. Then comes a tiny final panel against an empty background: a flat expression and one line. The joke rests on the sudden change of scale and tone.

### Twentieth-century French magazine cartoon

**3. One of many (in the spirit of Sempé).** A huge Parisian café, dozens of tables, each occupied by someone engrossed in a laptop. The cat sits with a laptop like everyone else, and only beside her has a waiter frozen. Fine pen lines, plenty of air, a light watercolour wash; one line below the drawing — the final remark.

**4. Wordless drawing (in the tradition of Bosc and Chaval).** The cat and waiter at a table, each with a thought bubble. In the waiter's, the cat in a ruff jumps through a flaming hoop. In the cat's, she sits at a monitor in a circus caravan while a clown and strongman queue with a broken printer. Economical lines, no background or text.

### Minimalist webcomic

**5. Fixed camera.** Four identical panels: a stick figure and a cat drawn with a few lines; only the text in the balloons changes. The third panel is a wordless pause (the cat with closed eyes, a little lollipop above her), the fourth delivers the punchline. The drawing's utter imperturbability makes it funny.

**6. Two diagrams.** The same little scene drawn twice, annotated with arrows and handwritten labels. On the left, “what the waiter sees”: “CAT!!!”, “TALKS!!!”, “CIRCUS!” On the right, “what the cat sees”: “backend, 8 years' experience”, “not enough sleep”, “want a pastry”. The mismatch between the labels supplies the punchline; no dialogue is needed.

### American newspaper political cartoon

**7. Allegory with labels.** One panel with dense crosshatching and large-headed caricature figures, everything labelled: the cat is “Specialist”, the waiter holding a contract is “Employer”, the tent outside is “Show”. The joke becomes a comment on people who see a curiosity rather than a profession. In a corner, following the genre's tradition, a tiny mouse makes its own remark.

**8. Media circus.** The circus comes to her: a crowd of reporters with cameras and microphones bursts into the café, followed by the director uncle in a top hat; newspapers shout “Talking Cat!” At the centre of this chaos, the cat shields herself with her laptop. The caption in quotation marks beneath the picture is her final question.

### Noir comic

**9. The waiter's monologue.** A black-and-white page with deep shadows, slats of light through blinds, rain outside the window. Text runs in first-person caption boxes: “She walked in at half past eleven. Ordered coffee with cream.” The cat is a femme fatale in a trench coat and veiled hat. The comedy lies in the gap between the heavy tone and the trivial plot.

**10. View from the street.** One wide panel: a late-night diner behind a large window, wet asphalt, a torn circus poster on the brick wall. Inside are two silhouettes: a pointed-eared one at a table and another leaning over it. The only cold light source is the laptop screen illuminating the cat's face. The final line appears in a caption box below.

**11. Subjective camera.** The entire page is shown through the cat's eyes: we never see her, only her paws on the table, a cup, and the looming waiter at a steep low angle. In the final panel, her gaze drops: her paws rest on a keyboard, with lines of code on the screen.

### English children's book illustration

**12. Book page (in the spirit of Beatrix Potter).** A small watercolour vignette on a white field, with the joke printed beneath it. The cat, in a cape and bonnet, sits decorously before a porcelain cup; the waiter freezes with his tray. In this tradition a clothed, talking animal is normal, so the picture itself takes the cat's side and does not share the waiter's surprise.

**13. Engraved plate (in the spirit of Tenniel).** A black-and-white pen drawing with dense hatching, as in Alice: a cat with unassailable logic and a bewildered human, as though at another mad tea party. Below the picture is a quoted line from the “book”: “‘But what would they need a programmer for?’ asked the Cat.”

**14. Cover of a nonexistent book (in the spirit of Judith Kerr).** A simple colour illustration in the manner of The Tiger Who Came to Tea: the cat at a table, the waiter with a tray, everything cosy and ordinary. The cover title carries the punchline, for example, “The Cat Who Worked as a Programmer”.

### Soviet children's book illustration

**15. Picture story (in the spirit of Suteyev and Vesyolye Kartinki).** Six numbered pictures, each with a short caption and no balloons. Rounded, kind-looking characters, clean outlines, bright gouache: a cat with a bow, a moustached waiter with a towel over his arm, a circus tent with little flags in her dreams.

**16. Circus spread (in the spirit of Lebedev's illustrations for Marshak's Circus).** A small café scene on the left-hand page; the cat's dream fills the right. Acrobats, jugglers and a cotton-candy vendor are drawn as flat poster figures against white, without perspective. The final line is set in large type at the bottom of the spread, like a line of verse.

**17. What happened next.** The book's last picture: the cat has ended up at the circus after all, but she sits with a computer in the lighting booth beneath the big top, while acrobats and jugglers perform in the ring below. A lollipop and cotton candy lie beside the keyboard. Warm watercolour; the resolution is shown without words.

### Naive art

**18. Signboard (in the spirit of Pirosmani).** The scene is painted like a dukhan sign on black oilcloth: frontal, solemn, motionless. The cat sits majestically at a table with a cup and pastry; the waiter stands to attention with a towel. Across the top, careful, uneven letters read: “The Programmer Cat Drinks Coffee”.

**19. Dream of the circus (in the spirit of Henri Rousseau).** The cat reclines in a café armchair, while in place of walls a dream grows around her, painted down to the last leaf: acrobats, jugglers, gigantic lollipops, clouds of cotton candy. The waiter peeks out of this thicket like an animal in the jungle. The punchline is in the title on the frame.

**20. Decorative beast (in the spirit of Maria Prymachenko).** The cat is shown as a patterned fantastical beast against a bright, solid background; in front of her are a cup and pastry, to the side a small waiter and carousel flowers. At the bottom is a long, proverb-like title, as in the artist's own work; it tells the entire joke.

### Narrative painting of the old masters

**21. “The Calling” (after Caravaggio).** A dark tavern, a slanting beam of light, the waiter pointing at the cat with an outstretched hand, while she points a paw at herself: “Me?” Her other paw rests on the laptop lid like the tax collector's hand on the coins. The punchline is on the museum label: “The Calling of a Programmer to the Circus. Oil on canvas.”

**22. Dutch interior.** A quiet room with a chequered floor and light from a window on the left; the cat at a table with a Delft cup, a servant with a jug. A “picture within the picture” showing fairground acrobats hangs on the wall: this was how the Dutch concealed a clue to the story. The laptop is painted as a still-life object, with a glint on its screen, and is not immediately noticed.

**23. Panorama with a recurring heroine.** A crowded town square in the spirit of Bruegel, seen from above, using an early-master device: the heroine appears three times in one painting. She enters the café, sits with the waiter and, now in her dreams, wanders through the fair at the far edge among acrobats and sweet-sellers. As in The Fall of Icarus, passers-by mind their own business and do not notice the miracle.

**24. Triptych (in the spirit of Bosch).** The left wing is the café; the centre is the circus as a garden of delights, with acrobats and gigantic lollipops. The right wing is a dark server room with glowing screens, where the cat is finally in her element. The story reads from left to right, without words.

### Impressionism

**25. Bar with a mirror (after Manet's A Bar at the Folies-Bergère).** The waiter stands behind the counter facing the viewer, looking lost. The mirror behind him reflects the room and the cat customer; in a corner, as in the original, an acrobat's legs on a trapeze are visible. Free brushwork, shimmering lamps; the title supplies the rest, for example, “A Woman Programmer at the Folies-Bergère”.

**26. Closed eyes.** A table on a sunny terrace, patches of light through the foliage. The cat closes her eyes, and the steam over her cup dissolves into coloured brushstrokes in which one can make out a big top, trapeze, and pink cloud of cotton candy. The waiter is a white patch of apron at the edge of the canvas. No text; title: “Dream of the Circus. Portrait of a Woman Programmer”.

**27. Series (like Monet's Rouen Cathedrals).** The same table painted four times in different light: morning — the order; noon — astonishment; sunset — the dream; evening under gas lamps — the cat alone, her face lit by a screen. The series of paintings works as a comic.

### Japanese ukiyo-e print

**28. Cat in a teahouse (in the spirit of Kuniyoshi).** A sheet with flat colours and firm outlines: a kimono-clad cat at a low table, an attendant dropping a tray, a travelling showman's banner visible through the open shōji. In a corner, a title cartouche and red seal; dialogue is written in vertical columns in the empty space.

**29. Triptych.** Three vertical sheets read from right to left: order, offer, dream. The last depicts an Edo “circus”: acrobats on bamboo poles, spinning-top jugglers, a lollipop seller. The punchline appears in the third sheet's cartouche.

**30. Shadow on the shōji.** The waiter sees not a cat but a bakeneko shapeshifter: an enormous two-tailed shadow rises on the paper partition, as in ghost prints. On this side of the partition, a tidy cat sits at a laptop resembling a lacquer box. A print about the difference between what one imagines and what is there.

### Soviet satirical magazine (Krokodil)

**31. “Outside her speciality”.** The classic format: a heading above, dialogue introduced by dashes below the drawing. Heavy outlines, bright flat colour, an obsequiously bent waiter. The cat is a woman specialist from the early-computer era, with punched tape sticking out of her briefcase. The caption consists of the final two lines.

**32. In the personnel department.** The action moves one step forward: the circus director's office, posters on the walls, the uncle in a top hat offering a contract for a “talking cat”, while the cat places her diploma on the desk. Grotesque figures, a satirical-feuilleton tone; the caption beneath the picture is her question about a programmer.

### Ancient Egyptian painting

**33. Tomb painting.** Three registers with figures in profile and columns of hieroglyphs. In the upper register, a small kneeling waiter raises his hands before a large enthroned cat (already sacred to an Egyptian) and places coffee and a pastry on an offering table. In the middle, a procession of acrobats and jugglers, as in the paintings of Beni Hasan. In the lower register, the cat sits in a scribe's pose, with a laptop on her knees instead of papyrus.

**34. Satirical papyrus.** A horizontal scroll in the manner of actual Egyptian papyri with animals in human roles: light reed-pen drawing, crumbling edges, losses. The scenes follow one another, while a museum label with a “translation” supplies the punchline: “…but what would they need a scribe for?”

The concepts and styles are not inseparably locked together: two thought bubbles, a view through the cat's eyes, a triptych, or “what happened next” can be transferred to almost any other style on the list.
