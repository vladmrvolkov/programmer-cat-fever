**Classic four-panel strip (newspaper comic)**
The concept relies on comic timing and contrasting emotions across four successive scenes. In the first panel the cat briskly places her order; in the second, the shocked waiter leans towards her with his circus offer; in the third, the cat dreamily closes her eyes against a cloud containing cotton candy and acrobats; in the fourth, she asks the final question with a deadpan face. The style is concise outline drawing with flat fills in the spirit of Garfield or Simon's Cat.

**Single-panel cartoon in the spirit of The New Yorker**
The concept dispenses with the lengthy dialogue: the entire backstory is conveyed through visual markers in one panel, with only the final line beneath it (“Sounds tempting. But what would a circus need a programmer for?”). **Japanese manga (yonkoma format)**
Four vertical panels use exaggerated anime emotions to heighten the absurdity. The waiter's eyes pop out against dynamic speed lines; in the third panel, shining little stars and pastel lollipops appear around the cat; in the last, her glasses gleam ominously white while her face remains completely calm. The style is black-and-white manga with screentones and contrasting ink.

**Photorealistic digital art with hidden details**
The concept is to make the scene as plausible as possible, so that the absurdity works through realism and small Easter eggs. The style is detailed digital painting with cinematic lighting.

**Noir graphic novel (in the spirit of Blacksad)**
The scene is presented as an ironic detective story with a world-weary protagonist. The style is high-contrast noir with deep shadows and finely rendered fur.

**“Perception vs Reality” diptych**

The style is dynamic digital sketching with an emphasis on angles and optical reflections.
