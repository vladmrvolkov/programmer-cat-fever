The whole point of the joke is the clash between two perspectives: for the waiter, the miracle is that the cat can talk; for the cat, the most important thing about her is her profession. The variants therefore differ not only in style but also in how the ending is delivered: through words, details, or contrasting images.

## Single illustrations

1. **Magazine cartoon with a caption.** One panel: the cat at a table, with the dumbfounded waiter leaning over her. The entire story remains offstage; only the last line appears beneath the drawing. Ink and grey wash, minimal detail.

2. **A cloud of dreams.** The cat closes her eyes, with an enormous thought cloud above her containing acrobats, jugglers, lollipops and cotton candy. In one corner of the cloud, she herself stands bewildered in the ring with a laptop. Soft watercolour in the spirit of a children's book.

3. **“Two circuses” diptych.** On the left is the circus in the waiter's imagination: the cat in sequins jumping through a flaming hoop. On the right is the circus in the cat's imagination: she sits beneath the big top at a monitor, unable to see what needs fixing. Flat vector graphics, two contrasting palettes.

4. **The answer in the details.** An ordinary ordering scene without dialogue, but the cat's profession is legible in her belongings: a sticker-covered laptop, a hoodie, a conference badge, a backpack. The illustration works alongside the text of the joke. Detailed digital painting.

5. **Close-up.** Just the cat's face, her eyes dreamily closed, with carousel lights or lines of code reflected in her glasses. The waiter remains a blurred silhouette in the background. Cinematic lighting, realistic digital painting.

6. **Through the waiter's eyes.** A first-person view: hands holding a notepad and tray at the bottom, and opposite them an unflappable cat impatiently tapping her paw on the table. A quick marker sketch.

7. **Night café.** An empty room, light from the shopfront window, a solitary freelance cat after a deadline, and a waiter frozen with a coffee pot. The humour rests on the contrast between the melancholy picture and the absurd dialogue. Realistic painting in the spirit of American urban scenes of the 1940s.

8. **Isometric café cutaway.** The café is seen from above and to one side, like a game level: tables, counter, kitchen, tiny figures with speech balloons. The other customers do not react to the cat at all. Clean isometric vector graphics.

9. **“Circus” seek-and-find picture book.** A spread densely packed with circus life: ring, audience, cotton-candy vendors, dressing rooms. Somewhere backstage, the reader must find the cat setting up the lighting console. Small, detailed drawings with bright fills.

10. **“She accepted” epilogue.** The cat is at the circus, but not in the ring: she sits in a cubbyhole among props and clown wigs, repairing the ticketing software. A warm gouache illustration.

## Comics

11. **Four-panel strip.** The order, the waiter's astonishment, the offer with dreams of the circus, the final line. Newspaper style: simple outlines, flat fills, equal-sized panels.

12. **Strip with a silent pause.** The same, but before the ending there is a wordless panel: the cat closes her eyes while the waiter waits hopefully. The pause strengthens the impact of the final line. Concise black-and-white graphics.

13. **Full-page comic with 6–9 panels.** An expanded version: entering the café, customers' reactions, close-ups, changing angles. European “clear line”: even outlines, local colour, detailed interiors.

14. **Manga.** Screentones, shock lines around the waiter, shining little stars in the daydream scene. In the final panel, the cat is drawn in a simplified chibi form with a question mark above her head. Black-and-white graphics.

15. **Vertical webtoon.** A long scrolling strip: cramped grey café panels, then an enormous colourful circus panel several screens tall, abruptly cut off by the final line. Digital painting with soft gradients.

16. **Silent comic.** Not a single word, only symbols in the balloons: a cup and pastry, “!?”, a tent, a lollipop, and finally “</>” with a question mark. Minimalist graphics in two colours.

17. **Through the cat's eyes.** Every panel is from her point of view: a huge waiter looms overhead, her own paws and whiskers enter the frame. Dynamic drawing with wide-angle views.

18. **Camera pullback.** The first panel shows only the cat's face, then the camera gradually pulls back. By the ending, the laptop, cables and mug are visible, so the picture completes the line. Restrained graphics with one accent colour.

19. **Changing style within the comic.** The café is drawn in dull, realistic sepia, the circus dreams explode into bright “childlike” colours, and the ending returns to sepia. The style itself becomes the narrator.

20. **Stick figures.** Extremely schematic figures, the cat represented by an oval with ears, with all the emphasis on dialogue and pauses. The manner of geek webcomics: a black line on a white background.

21. **Continuous panorama.** One long drawing without borders: the characters recur several times from left to right, while the café gradually turns into a circus and back again. Line drawing with watercolour wash, like a frieze.

22. **Photo comic.** Staged photographs of a real cat at a table and a person in an apron, with speech balloons overlaid. An alternative: plasticine or felt figures photographed as frames from puppet animation.

23. **Comic with a “post-credits scene”.** The main story in 4–5 panels, followed by an extra: the uncle, the circus director, reads the cat's CV and scratches his head. Bright cartoon style.

## Stylisations after periods and traditions

24. **Lubok.** Flat figures, coarse outlines, colouring in three or four colours, explanatory text in ornamental lettering around the edges. The archaic form comically clashes with the word “programmer”.

25. **Medieval miniature.** The cat and innkeeper in the manner of a bestiary, dialogue written on fluttering scrolls, marginalia with jugglers and acrobats. Gold, vermilion, conventionalised perspective.

26. **Victorian engraving.** The action moves to the nineteenth century: a bonneted cat with a stack of punched cards, a waiter in tails, a circus big top. Dense wood-engraving-style hatching, a caption beneath the picture.

27. **Mid-twentieth-century satirical magazine.** A glass-walled café, a waiter with a towel over his arm, a cat with a roll of punched tape tucked under hers. Gouache, springy lines, muted colours.

28. **Constructivist poster.** Diagonal composition, a red-and-black palette, sans-serif type. The cat points her paw at the viewer, with the final line turned into a slogan.

29. **Retro circus poster.** The poster the uncle would print: “For the first time in the ring! A talking cat writes code!” — and the cat sitting bored at a desk under spotlights. Early-twentieth-century lithography with flourishes and stars.

30. **Japanese print.** A teahouse instead of a café, the cat in a kimono, travelling acrobats in a dream cloud. Flat areas of colour, expressive outlines, vertical inscriptions.

31. **Grand oil painting.** A genre scene in the manner of the old masters: dramatic window light, a still life with a pastry, dignified poses. The comedy lies in the mismatch between the elevated genre and the subject.

32. **Shadow theatre.** Black cut-out silhouettes against a light background: recognisable outlines of a cat, tray, tent and laptop. Paper cut-outs, one or two background colours.

33. **Child's drawing.** The joke as though drawn by a child: felt-tip pens, crooked letters, a sun in the corner, an arrow pointing to the cat labelled “progrummer”.

34. **Collage.** Characters assembled from cuttings of old magazines and engravings: a photographic cat's head on a drawn body, a circus made from advertising pictures. An absurdist aesthetic.

35. **3D render.** A still “from an animated film”: a fluffy stylised cat, warm café lighting, expressive faces. Possible textures: plasticine, low-poly, voxels.

## IT and digital formats

36. **Pixel-art adventure game.** A screen from an old game: a café in 16-bit graphics, a dialogue window with response options at the bottom, a laptop in the cat's inventory.

37. **Visual novel.** A static café background, a full-length cat sprite, a translucent box containing the speaker's name and text. Anime aesthetics, with the sprite's emotions changing from panel to panel.

38. **Terminal and ASCII art.** The cat and waiter are made of characters, the dialogue appears as console output, and the final line is formatted as an error message. Monospaced type, green on black.

39. **Flowchart.** The joke as an algorithm: “Cat enters” → “Can she talk?” → “Offer circus job” → “type mismatch” exception. Strict technical graphics; an alternative is a sequence diagram with “Cat”, “Waiter” and “Uncle” as participants.

40. **Diagram or graph.** Venn circles for “talking cats”, “circus performers” and “programmers”, with the cat standing in a different intersection from the one the waiter expects. Alternatively, a graph of “the waiter's astonishment and the cat's patience” over time. Business presentation style.

41. **Messages.** A series of “screenshots”: the waiter messages his uncle, the uncle rejoices, then a message arrives from the cat asking about the vacancy. A schematic chat interface with character avatars.

42. **The cat's CV.** One page: photograph, skills (“catching bugs”), work experience, with the circus director's decision scrawled over it in red pencil. Standard document layout.

43. **Meme.** A two-part image with text at the top and bottom, or an “expectation / reality” format. Deliberately simple graphics, bold outlined type.

44. **Sticker pack.** The story is divided into 6–8 standalone stickers: the cat with a cup, the waiter with his jaw hanging open, the cat daydreaming, the cat bewildered. Thick outlines, white borders, vivid emotions.

45. **Pictograms.** The joke as safety instructions: faceless figures, a cat symbol, arrows, a crossed-out tent. Alternatively, an emoji rebus.

## Objects “from the world of the joke”

46. **Newspaper clipping.** An item titled “Talking Cat Turns Down Circus” with a grainy “photograph” and a comment from the waiter as eyewitness. Old newspaper layout.

47. **Drawing on a napkin.** A ballpoint sketch with a coffee stain, as though the waiter drew what happened immediately after his shift. Alternatively, chalk on a blackboard menu.

48. **A set of clues.** An overhead view of arranged objects: a receipt for “coffee with cream, pastry”, the circus-director uncle's business card, the cat's badge. The story is reconstructed from the objects. A flat lay in the spirit of product photography.

49. **Calligram.** The text of the joke is laid out in the silhouette of a cat or a tent, with the final line highlighted in monospaced type. Pure typography without a drawing.

50. **Storyboard.** A sheet of panels for a future cartoon: camera-movement notes, timings, arrows. Rough pencil drawing.

Almost any variant can also be varied along three axes:

- **How human the cat is:** an ordinary cat on a chair or a clothed character.
- **When the profession is revealed:** only in the final line or through hints from the first panel.
- **Setting and period:** the present, the past, or an unspecified world.
