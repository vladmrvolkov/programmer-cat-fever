# **Map of axes of variation for illustrations and comics of the programmer-cat joke**

## **Principle of the map**

Three mismatches interact in the joke:

1. **Ontological:** the waiter finds a talking cat incredible; she considers speech unworthy of discussion.
2. **Social and professional:** he sees a circus attraction; she defines herself through profession.
3. **Informational:** readers first encounter an unusual cat; only at the end do they discover that programming is central to her self-image.

Distinguishing these is useful: visualisation may preserve events while changing meaning, reader sympathies and even the comic mechanism.

Below, an **axis** is a question on which the author makes a separate decision. Axes are organised by level, but related questions remain separate where they address different aspects.

Each axis has an identifier for subsequent analysis: A01, B01, V01, etc. In D, these show formal decisions becoming script decisions.

A, B and V list questions and significance only, **without catalogues of possible answers**. Specific visual languages occur solely in G.

## **G. Suitable visual languages and the script premises they bring**

Lists **artistic systems capable of guiding script readings**, rather than specific images.

Key distinction:

- **Tradition** brings historically developed depiction rules and character expectations.
- **Genre** brings particular models of events, conflicts and motives.
- **Graphic manner** brings a way of interpreting depicted reality with possible cultural meanings of its own.

These systems are not mutually exclusive. They combine but sometimes conflict. A references identify script questions a language tends to predetermine: a predisposition, not an obligatory outcome.

### **G4. Applied and media visual languages**

Less tied to classical art schools, these nevertheless have strong script assumptions of their own.

| Visual language | What it brings to the reading |
| --- | --- |
| **65. Infographics** | Makes events an explicable logical system. Focuses on A02, A03 and conflicting A20/A25 conceptions. |

| **67. Corporate illustration** | Normalises professional roles and institutional relationships. Supports A05, A07 and A12's social ordinariness. |
| **68. Recruitment advertising graphics** | Makes job offers and professional worth the central frame. Strengthens A19–A23. |
| **69. User-interface graphics** | Brings digitally mediated communication. Foregrounds A07, A25 and A29; may change understanding of the conversational situation itself. |
| **70. Cinematic storyboard** | Assumes observable physical action, acting and a causally connected scene. Heightens A10, A15, A19 and A23 without automatically establishing emotional meaning. |

G identifies **70 visual languages and artistic traditions**.

These are not 70 mutually exclusive styles. Some are traditions, others genres, graphic techniques or media-based representational methods. One language can combine several characteristics.

Crucially, some **almost automatically normalise a talking cat**, others make her violate world organisation, and others shift attention from unusualness to profession and social relations. Artistic choice may therefore settle central script questions before individual events are developed.

## **D. Which artistic and storyboarding axes effectively settle script questions**

The script/artistic boundary is not absolute here.

The distinction is:

**A formal choice becomes a script choice when, beyond showing a given event, it determines what happened, world rules, character motives or the meaning of actions.**

Dependency may be bidirectional: script limits graphics; chosen pictorial language limits script.

### **D3. Principal dependency nodes**

Six nodes particularly strongly connect screenwriter, artist and storyboarder.

**1. What sort of creature is this cat?**

A04–A06, A10, A12 ↔ B01–B03, B07, B14–B17 ↔ V17–V18

Canon and initial presentation can make explaining the talking cat necessary or superfluous.

**2. What does being a programmer mean?**

A07–A08, A11–A12 ↔ B17, B25–B26, B34 ↔ V19–V20, V32–V33

The visual profession can predetermine identity while depriving the final line of surprise.

**3. What is the circus to the cat?**

A09, A16–A17, A31 ↔ B24, B30, B33 ↔ V14, V21

Iconography and subjective-image editing may determine her imagined circus and why it matters.

**4. Who is funny, and who has power?**

A02–A03, A19–A23 ↔ B06, B18–B21, B27–B29 ↔ V22–V25

Character treatment, composition and viewpoint may establish social and meaningful hierarchy without changing dialogue.

**5. What does the last line actually mean?**

A13, A17–A18 ↔ B06, B19–B20, B32 ↔ V09, V15–V16, V29–V34

The programmer question depends on expression, pause, intonation, typography and response. This interprets script as well as presenting it.

**6. What does the viewer know, and when?**

A25 ↔ B12, B17, B25, B29 ↔ V06, V12, V17–V20, V30, V36

Information distribution directly controls comedy. Even a detail changing no depicted event can alter perception.

### **Which axes form hierarchies**

Some questions relate as a general parameter to its particular aspects. Preserve these links when developing the space of variants:

| General level | Specific parameters and the connection's meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **The fantastic world's organisation** | A04–A06 describe prevalence, social consequences and causal explanation. |
| **Professional identity** | A07 and A11–A12 distinguish social meaning, actual biography and personal self-definition. |
| **Image of the circus** | A09, A16 and A31 distinguish cultural meaning, subjective conceptions and actual world location. |
| **Anthropomorphism** | B14–B15 are aspects of one general decision: bodily structure and way of acting. |
| **Characters' physical expression** | B16–B21 separate individual appearance, social signs, expression and physical relationships. |
| **Pictorial style** | B07–B13 describe constructing artistic form; B01–B04 establish traditions and canons within which these methods acquire meaning. |

Thus these are not 106 wholly independent switches: some constrain others, some form hierarchies, some become mutually exclusive after choosing a canon or reading.

### **Main conclusion**

For this joke, distinguish three kinds of variation:

**Variation of events:** changing what happens, what exists and what characters know. Primarily A.

**Variation of interpretation:** changing viewers' understanding of identical events. B often outweighs script here: visual language pre-establishes normality, social roles and emotional judgement.

**Variation of disclosure:** changing when and in what order information reaches viewers. V describes this. With “programmer” as the last word, this level is fundamental: comedy itself can change without changing a fact.

Understand the space as **interconnected decisions with constraints**, rather than a product of independent lists. Tradition may predetermine script ontology, the cat's appearance her social role, and storyboarding the actual meaning of the ending.

These dependencies distinguish substantively different visualisations from mere changes of external treatment.
