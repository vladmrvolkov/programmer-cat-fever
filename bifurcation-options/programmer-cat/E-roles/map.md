# The programmer cat: a map for creating an illustration

## The joke

> A cat walks into a café and orders coffee with cream and a pastry. The waiter stands there dumbfounded, his mouth open, then asks in amazement:
>
> “You're a cat? A talking cat?! Incredible…”
>
> “Yes, I'm a cat. Are you bringing my order or not?”
>
> “Hold on a moment… Tell me, could you use a job? It's just that my uncle runs a circus, and he would gladly take you on, on any terms…”
>
> “A circus, you say?” The cat closes her eyes dreamily. “That's the place with the acrobats, jugglers, lollipops and cotton candy? Sounds tempting. But what would they need a programmer for?”

## How to use the map

Distinguish events in the story's world, characters' intentions, the author's attitude, and the reader's knowledge. Showing the laptop early may inform the viewer without changing what the waiter knows. A threatening angle may alarm the viewer without establishing a character's malicious intent.

When choosing a visual language, distinguish three kinds of connection:

- **A constraint of the adopted canon or format.** This applies if you have actually chosen that rule. Four panels constrain the storyboard but do not require an event after the final line. Stylising a familiar world does not require adopting all its laws.
- **A familiar association.** Manner, genre, colour or an object may encourage a reading. How readily it is recognised depends on the image and the viewer's experience.
- **Free authorial interpretation.** You may invent a biography, motive or plot circumstance and adopt it as a fact of your story. Simply do not assume that style automatically prescribes it.

Material, line, rhythm, texture and colour may have independent artistic value. They need not change the plot every time. Some questions in the map are nested within others; use the level of detail you need.

## Script and meaning

### Meaning and the world's rules

| Decision | Question | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The limits of adaptation** | How strictly must the visual story follow the original joke's events and structure of meaning? | Determines whether the work remains an illustration of the joke or becomes a reinterpretation. |
| **The main subject of comedy** | Which contradiction produces the main comic effect? | The joke combines fantastic incongruity, professional identity and a mistaken assessment of abilities. |
| **The bearer of common sense** | Whose account of events does the work invite us to consider reasonable? | The story may support one character's view, both in different respects, or maintain distance from both. This differs from choosing the protagonist. |
| **The normality of a talking cat** | How unusual is the existence of a talking cat in the depicted world? | Distinguish how unusual she is within the world's rules, to this particular waiter, and to the reader: these three judgements may differ. |
| **The social status of intelligent animals** | What place do intelligent animals occupy in this world's society? | Even if intelligent animals are known to exist, their social recognition, independence and access to professions remain separate questions. |
| **Explaining the miracle** | To what extent does the story's world call for an explanation of the cat's unusual abilities? | Determines the internal logic of events and how much attention is paid to the talking animal itself. |
| **The status of programming as a profession** | What social and cultural significance does programming have in this world? | The profession's social meaning affects the weight of the final line, while the truth of the cat's claim remains a separate question. |
| **The connection between programming and the circus** | How do programmers' professional skills relate to the circus's actual needs in this world? | Determines whether the final line is logically impeccable, ironic, or conceals a further contradiction. |
| **The meaning of the circus** | What role does the circus occupy in this world's values and perceptions? | The circus is both the subject of the waiter's offer and the object of the cat's initial interest. |
| **The degree of everyday plausibility** | By what rules does the cat participate in ordinary material activities of human life? | Ordering coffee and a pastry implies everyday independence that the animal's appearance alone does not explain. |

### The cat as a character

| Decision | Question | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The cat's biography** | What life and professional experience does she have at the beginning of the episode? | Her actual biography may match or differ from the self-image she presents in conversation. |
| **The cat's self-definition** | What place does her profession occupy in her personal identity? | The final effect rests on the clash between how others define her and how she defines herself. |
| **Awareness of being exceptional** | How well does the cat understand the impression she makes on people? | Determines the meaning of her composure before the astonished waiter. |
| **The purpose of visiting the café** | Why has the cat come here, and what does she want to achieve in this situation? | The initial order establishes her practical motivation and attitude towards interrupted service. |
| **The cat's temperament** | Which enduring character traits determine her behaviour? | The same lines can be understood very differently depending on the speaker's character. |
| **The cat's conception of the circus** | What knowledge, memories and associations shape her perception of the circus? | The list of acrobats, jugglers and sweets is both text and evidence of her inner world. |
| **Attitude to the offer** | How seriously does the cat consider working at the circus? | Determines the dramatic meaning of the moment when she closes her eyes dreamily. |
| **The intention of the final line** | What does she intend to express by asking why a programmer is needed, and how definite should that intention remain? | Understanding the offer, taking an interest seriously, and the purpose of the final line are different questions; the image can clarify them or preserve ambiguity. |
| **The truth of the profession** | Is being a programmer an established fact of the world, merely the cat's claim, or a question the story leaves open? | The ending may confirm identity or create a new mystery. This differs from whether she understands the waiter's offer. |

### The waiter, other participants and relationships

| Decision | Question | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The waiter's motive** | What prompts him to offer the cat a job? | His reaction moves from astonishment to a business initiative; the reason for this transition affects the entire scene's meaning. |
| **The waiter's conception of the cat** | What abilities, needs and qualities does he attribute to her? | His assumptions determine his offer. Whether it is mistaken also depends on whether the circus actually needs a programmer. |
| **The waiter's social role** | What position and personal circumstances determine his behaviour? | His own job, standing and connections make the job offer socially significant. |
| **The figure of the uncle** | What is the circus director's relationship to the waiter, how does the waiter see him, and how reliable is the account? | The absent uncle may lend support to the offer, but the waiter's words alone establish neither his capabilities nor intentions. |
| **The distribution of initiative and power** | Who actually controls the conversation and the situation's potential development? | Despite his surprise, the waiter begins proposing a new social role for the cat. |
| **Previous relationships** | What history of acquaintance and interaction precedes this encounter? | Affects the sincerity of surprise, trust, and manner of communication. |
| **The distribution of knowledge within the scene** | What does each participant know, assume or mistakenly believe about the others, events and the proposed job? | This is the characters' knowledge. The reader's knowledge and how it changes belong to section V; they cannot automatically be transferred to the characters. |

### Setting, time and surroundings

| Decision | Question | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The café's type and status** | What kind of establishment hosts the conversation? | The café establishes norms of service, social behaviour and expectations of customers. |
| **The public nature of events** | Who else is present, and how are bystanders involved? | The degree of publicity affects the event's exceptional nature and the social cost of the waiter's reaction. |
| **Cultural and geographical environment** | In what society and cultural space does the story unfold? | Ideas of cafés, circuses, work and talking animals are culturally specific. |
| **Historical and technological period** | In what historical and technological period does the action occur? | The programming profession, its prestige and visual signs depend on the period. |
| **The scene's time** | At what point in everyday life does the episode occur? | Time affects the café's atmosphere, other people's presence and the circumstances of the visit. |
| **The circus's actual existence** | Does the circus exist beyond the waiter's account and the cat's imaginings, and what place does it actually occupy in the world? | The circus as discussed, imagined and actually existing may differ. Images and editing can distinguish these levels or deliberately leave them uncertain. |

### Backstory, actions and consequences

| Decision | Question | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The immediate backstory** | What circumstances brought the characters to the start of this conversation? | May explain the cat's mood, the waiter's condition and the reason for their encounter. |
| **The fate of the order** | How does the cat's initial action — getting coffee with cream and a pastry — develop? | In the original joke, the job offer interrupts her practical objective and leaves it unresolved. |
| **The immediate continuation** | What happens immediately after her final question? | Subsequent behaviour may confirm or change the interpretation of the final line. |
| **Long-term consequences** | Does the encounter have long-term consequences for the characters, and what are they? | The episode may remain self-contained or connect with their later lives; the final line itself does not establish the outcome. |

### The author's perspective and intended audience

| Decision | Question | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Whose story this is** | Whose fate, objective and transformation organise the episode? | The protagonist need not be the character through whose eyes the scene is shown, or the one whose position the author supports. |
| **The tone of meaning** | What overall attitude and feeling should the story leave? | The tone may relate to the characters' dignity, curiosity, absurdity or vulnerability. Colour, light, manner and rhythm help make that attitude tangible. |
| **Type or individual** | To what extent are the characters recognisable masks, and to what extent particular people and beings with biographies of their own? | Individualisation changes the importance of backstory and whether this is perceived as an isolated joke or an episode in a life. |
| **The story's intended audience** | Who is the work for, and what does that audience already know about the joke, the profession and cultural references? | A reader familiar with the joke may find interest elsewhere. Which visual and cultural references they recognise also matters. |

## Visual language and depiction

### Tradition, canon and the affiliation of style

| Decision | Question | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Visual tradition** | To which historically and culturally established tradition of depiction does the work belong? | A recognisable tradition may evoke expectations about animals, social roles and comedy; their strength depends on the audience and its visual experience. |
| **Genre code** | Through which genre's conventions will viewers perceive the story? | The same events acquire different meanings under different genre expectations. |
| **Pictorial canon** | Which rules of the chosen canon are adopted, and which are used only for stylisation or quotation? | Obligation arises from an adopted rule, rather than the name of an artistic example alone. |
| **Quotation and stylisation** | How far should viewers recognise another artistic system or cultural example in the image? | Recognisable stylisation transfers meanings already associated with its source to the joke. |
| **The visual audience** | Whose visual literacy and cultural associations does the image address? | The meanings of circus, programming and talking cat depend on the audience's familiar visual codes. |
| **The drawing's emotional tone** | What sustained emotional attitude does the artistic form establish? | Regulates viewers' attitude towards the waiter's surprise, the cat's composure and the unexpected ending. |
| **Whose world dictates the style** | Which of the story's worlds — café, circus, programming — sets the image's language, and should its perspective be accepted as normal? | Form can express a participant's position before words do. The style's affiliation with that world remains an interpretation that must be made legible. |
| **Shared graphic rules for the characters** | Are the cat, waiter and environment drawn according to the same conventions? | Different graphic registers can emphasise otherness or conflicting views, but do not prove that the characters literally belong to different worlds. |
| **The period evoked by the manner** | What period does the manner evoke, and does it coincide with the period of the action? | Historical stylisation and historical setting may coincide, diverge or enter a meaningful conflict. |

### Conventionality, material and form

| Decision | Question | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The degree of pictorial conventionality** | How closely does the depiction follow visually observable reality? | Conventionality affects perception of the unusual but does not establish the world's laws. Separately decide whether the miracle is graphically distinguished or the cat is treated on a par with her surroundings. |
| **The degree of caricatural distortion** | How far does expressiveness depend on deliberately changing proportions and characteristics? | The contrast between the waiter's astonishment and the cat's calm lends itself to graphic exaggeration. |
| **Material and technique** | What pictorial material and method create the work? | Material establishes cultural associations, the character of images and the sense of an artistic world. |
| **Line and outline** | How is the system of graphic lines organised? | Determines the legibility of expressions, silhouettes and comic action. |
| **The modelling and geometry of forms** | What principles shape characters, objects and surroundings? | Shared forms may bring the cat closer to people; differences can emphasise otherness. |
| **Density of detail** | What level of visual information appears in characters and surroundings? | Details can support professional and everyday context, but also reveal the ending's meaning prematurely. |
| **Texture and materiality** | How are surfaces and physical properties conveyed? | Texture affects the cat's physicality, the food's appeal and the café's sense of plausibility. |

### Characters' appearance and behaviour

| Decision | Question | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The cat's anatomical anthropomorphism** | How far is her bodily structure adapted towards human appearance? | Bodily form affects how natural speech, ordering food and having a profession seem. |
| **Behavioural anthropomorphism** | How far do movement, poses and handling objects follow human behavioural codes? | Anatomy and action may express the animal–human boundary differently. |
| **The cat's individual appearance** | Which visible features make her a particular character? | Appearance may suggest character and life experience before her first line. |
| **Social signs in the cat's appearance** | How do appearance and accessories communicate social position and activities? | An accessory may convey an occupation, create a supposition or support a false impression. The truth of the profession and when viewers learn it are separate choices. |
| **The waiter's visual image** | How does his appearance express individuality and social role? | Determines the social contrast between the human server and the unusual customer. |
| **The system of facial expression** | What pictorial means convey expressions, speech articulation and emotional changes? | A talking feline face with closing eyes requires decisions about expression. Facial expression may clarify intention or preserve multiple readings; it need not be unambiguous. |
| **The language of gestures and poses** | How does bodily behaviour show reactions, intentions and relationships? | The scene mainly consists of conversation; gestures make it visual action. |
| **Relative physical scale** | How do the characters' sizes, proportions and physical presence compare? | The relationship between a small customer and a human waiter affects perceptions of power, status and the situation's strangeness. |

### Space, objects and symbols

| Decision | Question | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The café's visual organisation** | What does the service space look like, and how does it express cultural affiliation? | The café's appearance creates a context of service and social contact. It need not confirm either the cat's uniqueness or ordinariness. |
| **The visual role of the order** | How are coffee, cream, pastry and service objects presented? | The order is the cat's initial objective and a material counterpoint to the sudden career discussion. |
| **Visual signs of the circus** | Which visual signs denote the circus? | If shown, these signs guide associations. Who owns the image and how reliable it is remain separate choices. |
| **Visual signs of programming** | Which visual signs denote the profession? | They may confirm, suggest or mislead; a laptop alone does not establish competence. |
| **Signs of period and culture** | How do objects, clothes and surroundings indicate the events' cultural and temporal affiliation? | Establish period, social roles and the profession's cultural meaning. |
| **Pictorial symbolism** | How do visual images express meaningful relationships beyond literally depicting events? | The central contradiction concerns whom others see the cat as and who she is in her own eyes. |
| **Spatial model** | What principles structure space, depth and objects' relative positions? | Space determines physical distance, the nature of contact and the situation's plausibility. |
| **Compositional hierarchy** | How is visual attention distributed among characters, actions, objects and surroundings? | Attention may preserve the ending's surprise, reveal the profession early or shift interest towards relationships. |
| **The environment's fit to the cat** | How do furniture and tableware fit her body and way of acting? | Physical contact with a human environment can emphasise independence or a mismatch of scale. Convenient furniture does not prove cats are served regularly. |

### Colour, light, text and movement

| Decision | Question | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Colour dramaturgy** | How are colour relationships and emotional functions organised? | Colour can distinguish emotional states, visual worlds and emphases of meaning. |
| **Light-and-shadow dramaturgy** | How does lighting model space and emotional atmosphere? | Lighting changes mood and directs the eye. It can present the cat as a spectacle before the circus offer. |
| **The appearance of lettering and text** | What artistic appearance do letters and other textual signs have? | The final line carries the ending, making its graphic treatment part of the work's visual language. |
| **Marking subjective images** | Which artistic signs distinguish a character's inner world from surrounding reality? | The cat's dreamy reaction makes conveying her imaginings especially important. |
| **Material plausibility** | How accurately are objects' construction and functions depicted? | Accuracy or deliberate violations of physical logic affect the world's credibility and technical hints; distinguish artistic choice from accidental error. |
| **The graphics of movement** | How does a static image denote movement, intensity and duration? | Determines the physical expressiveness of sudden astonishment, a calm answer and a dreamy pause. |

## Staging, comics and reading the image

### Format and scope of events

| Decision | Question | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The form of the visual narrative** | What basic narrative form does the image take? | Determines how the conversation's sequence and the ending's surprise can be shown at all. |
| **The medium's format** | What are the physical or screen conditions of presentation? | Determine overview, reading sequence and control over access to the ending. |
| **Number of panels** | How many separate images constitute the story? | Determines the scope for isolating events and independent emotional reactions. |
| **Division into dramatic units** | Which moments receive independent panels? | Choosing the boundaries between events creates comic editing. |
| **Scope of events** | How much of the story's world is shown directly to the reader? | Circumstances beyond the source text may change the encounter's interpretation. |
| **Point of entry** | When does the reader begin observing the story? | The beginning determines initial knowledge of the cat, café and situation's strangeness. |
| **Point of exit** | At what moment does the visual narrative end? | The joke depends on a closing line; continuation can change the punchline's force and meaning. |

### Rhythm, pauses and page organisation

| Decision | Question | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Narrative pace** | How does the duration of depicted events relate to reading time? | The joke depends on the contrast between quick surprise, dreamy delay and the final turn. |
| **Panel size and significance** | How is image space distributed among dramatic moments? | An event's visual duration and significance depend on its allotted space. |
| **The interval between panels** | What work must readers do to connect neighbouring panels mentally? | Gaps regulate inferred actions, changes of mood and anticipation of dialogue. |
| **Simultaneous visibility of information** | How is access to different parts of the story organised within one view? | If the ending is visible too early, the sequence of comic discovery is disrupted. |
| **The pause of the waiter's astonishment** | How much narrative attention does his initial reaction receive? | Its weight changes attention to the talking cat, but duration alone does not prove sincere surprise. |
| **The pause of the cat's imagination** | How long and compositionally significant is her dreamy state? | Its duration affects expectations; serious interest and ownership of the depicted fantasy require separate decisions. |
| **The pause before the punchline** | How is anticipation organised immediately before the final line? | The transition from dreamy agreement to a professional question determines the comic effect's precision. |
| **The pause after the punchline** | How does the narrative direct attention after revealing the profession? | The final reaction and time for absorbing it may change who becomes the joke's target. |

### Revealing information

| Decision | Question | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The cat's first appearance** | When and how fully does the reader first see the protagonist? | Her appearance itself establishes expectations about speaking. |
| **Revealing the ability to speak** | When and from which signs does the reader understand that the cat speaks? | Readers may discover it with the waiter or know beforehand: different organisations of knowledge, rather than different world rules. |
| **Revealing the profession** | When does the reader receive the professional claim, and when grounds for considering it reliable or doubtful? | Revealing the claim and confirming the fact need not coincide. Decide separately whether she really is a programmer and how certain this remains for the reader. |
| **Indirect informational clues** | How are signs regulated that let readers infer circumstances not yet stated? | The profession may become visually apparent before its verbal disclosure. |
| **Editing the actual and the imagined** | How does image sequence connect events with characters' inner representations? | Editing should allow a choice, or intentional uncertainty, about whose inner image is shown. A circus vision does not prove its existence outside the character's mind. |
| **Narrative point of view** | Whose perception and access to information limit the narrative, and how stable is that limit? | The observational position, protagonist and author's judgement need not coincide. |

### Viewpoint and staging

| Decision | Question | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Shot size** | How far from events is the imaginary camera at each significant moment? | Regulates visible expression and context. The final line may show the speaking cat, listening waiter or their interaction. |
| **Angle and viewpoint height** | From what angle does the reader observe characters and space? | May change perceptions of human–cat relationships, position and initiative. |
| **Blocking and directions of gaze** | Where are participants positioned and looking across the sequence? | Gazes convey surprise, interest and changes in the conversation's subject. |
| **Continuity of editing** | How are spatial and temporal relationships between successive images kept understandable? | Coherence or deliberate breaks affect reconstruction of the conversation; broken continuity need not mean an event within the world. |
| **Visible and off-panel space** | What information remains outside each image's boundaries? | Unseen participants, objects and reactions may change meaning without direct depiction. |

### Text, speech and reader attention

| Decision | Question | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The narrator's voice** | What function does the original joke's narration retain? | The original partly conveys its initial situation and emotional transitions through a narrator's words. |
| **Distribution of dialogue** | How is each character's speech distributed across images and dramatic moments? | The transitions from order to surprise, offer and answer structure the joke. |
| **Dialogue reading order** | How does text placement guide the sequence of verbal information? | Reading order may preserve, reorganise or prematurely reveal the punchline. |
| **Retention of the original text** | How fully are the narrator's and characters' words reproduced? | Cutting, moving or preserving words changes the text–drawing relationship. Fidelity does not require depicting every mentioned object. |
| **Division of labour between word and image** | What information does text convey, and what does the reader derive directly from the drawing? | A verbal joke must become a visual narrative while preserving its sequence of meaningful discoveries. |
| **The typography of the punchline** | How is the final line distinguished through text placement and organisation? | Affects emphasis and intonation; an informational turn occurs only insofar as the reader does not already know what is stated. |
| **Depicting silence and the unspoken** | How are significant wordless moments indicated? | The waiter's astonishment and the cat's dreamy pause have independent dramatic functions. |
| **Sounds and nonverbal reactions** | What role do graphically represented sounds and nonverbal reactions play? | May affect perceived duration and character of communication without changing dialogue wording. |
| **The trajectory of reader attention** | How does the overall organisation guide the direction and order of visual exploration? | The exact moment information is noticed matters as much as its presence. |

## Catalogue of visual languages

Broad traditions, genres, modes of depiction, materials and specific cultural references coexist here. They can be combined. The described readings are possibilities, rather than obligatory image properties. A suitable manner may leave many script questions open.

A specific artistic or literary reference must work in the image. If borrowing only a graphic manner, do not transfer a character's biography, period or another world's laws without a separate decision.

## Coordinating decisions

### What an adopted rule establishes

| Adopted rule | Its consequence | What remains to decide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One image | Everything directly visible is placed in one image, which can still have a reading sequence. | Which moment is shown, what is implied beyond it, where text appears. |
| Exactly four panels | Material is distributed across four panels. | Where the punchline occurs; the final panel may contain the line itself, without subsequent events. |
| Animal speech is ordinary in the story's world | The waiter's surprise must be consistent with that norm. | What surprised him, animals' standing, how this cat differs. |
| Transferring particular properties of a canonical character | Adopted properties become part of the new story. | Which traits are borrowed or changed, and how recognisable the reference is. |
| Preserving dialogue verbatim | Characters speak the given words. | Intonation, truthfulness, intentions and the relationship to what the drawing shows. |

### What form may suggest

| Artistic decision | Possible effect on reading | What is chosen separately |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tradition, genre and recognisable references | Evoke expectations of characters, world and conflict. | Actual world rules and specific motives. |
| Emotional register, colour and light | Support sympathy, distance, anxiety or calm. | Truth of assertions, biography and hidden intentions. |
| Caricatural exaggeration | Emphasises a comic target, trait or social type. | Authorial judgement and individuality. |
| The cat's human movement | Helps her appear a customer, interlocutor and specialist. | Prevalence and social standing of intelligent animals. |
| Appearance, clothing and work belongings | Suggest occupation, experience and standing. | Which signs are reliable, ambiguous or misleading. |
| Cat size and furniture design | Emphasise independence, vulnerability or a physically mismatched environment. | Whether the café accommodates cats generally or this one has adapted. |
| Depicting the circus | Emphasises pleasure, labour, spectacle or possible danger. | Whose circus is shown: actual, imagined by the cat, or conceived by someone else. |
| Composition, angle and distance | Give one character visual advantage, bring participants together or separate them. | Actual power, initiative and motives. |
| Light isolating the cat | May put her on display before the circus invitation. | Whether she notices or accepts the attention, and whether a literal spectacle exists. |
| A manner belonging to one of the story's worlds | Circus, café or programming may set the whole image's language and initial norm. | Whether the author supports, contests or juxtaposes perspectives. |
| Different graphic rules for characters | Make otherness and conflicting norms visible. | Whether they share one world or the difference is expressive alone. |
| Historical stylisation | Evokes a period, creates distance or contrast with the profession. | When events occur. |
| Pause, movement, expression and lettering | Guide intonation and impressions of reactions. | Sincerity, intention and permissible ambiguity. |
| Early profession reveal | May replace surprise with anticipation of another character's reaction. | What each character knows; viewers' knowledge does not transfer automatically. |
| Line, material and texture | Organise vision, physicality, generalisation and mood. | Whether this choice needs a plot connection at all. |

### Where depiction becomes a substantive choice

- **Profession.** Decide whether you show evidence of competence, an outward sign alone, or a deliberate false hint. Distinguish the cat's claim from its truth.
- **Fantasy.** Decide whose vision it is and whether it can be distinguished from events. Vividness alone establishes neither sincerity nor reality.
- **Order.** If the fate of coffee and pastry matters, give legible signs. An absent cup in one panel does not mean the order never arrived.
- **Backstory and continuation.** Distinguish what exists beyond the episode from what is shown. The characters' later lives can remain unknown.
- **Final shot.** Choose whose reaction holds attention. This can change the joke's target and sense of closure.
- **Narrator.** Decide what viewers can trust and where the image conveys a private perspective, dream or exaggeration.
- **The image as a statement.** If the whole work is a circus poster, it may already sell the cat as an attraction. The final line can then argue with the waiter and the image's very form.
- **Professional language as environment.** Interface, diagram or corporate graphics may encompass the whole narrative. Choose whether they express the cat's perspective, authorial irony or literal events.

## Examples of complete concepts

These illustrate how decisions can combine into a work. They are not a mandatory menu and do not exhaust the map. You may invent your own combination or reinterpret any example. In all cases, character, biography and circumstances are free authorial choices, rather than inevitable consequences of visual language.

The cat is a social metaphor for an urban resident; the joke targets class or professional snobbery, with the line placed in a caption.

Visual basis: single-panel magazine cartoon.
