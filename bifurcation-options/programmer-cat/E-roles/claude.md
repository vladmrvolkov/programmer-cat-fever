The standard by which significance is assessed below: the joke rests on a collision between two classifications of one creature. The waiter sees the species (a talking cat is a curiosity); the cat sees the profession (she is a specialist). Three of its properties matter to visualisation:

- The resolution is a word conveying information, and the picture can give it away prematurely.
- A pause and a false trail are built into the text (“closes her eyes dreamily,” “sounds tempting”).
- A talking animal is a miracle in the joke, but a convention in comics and animation.

## A. The screenwriter's axes

**Meaning**

**A1. Target of the joke.** *Question:* whom or what does the image laugh at—and does it laugh at anyone at all? *Why:* the text contains two incompatible views of one cat, and the joke does not say whose is defective. The picture will inevitably emphasize one side.

**A2. The cat's position at the end** (parent axis).
- **A2.1. Sincerity.** *Question:* does the cat understand why she is really being invited to the circus? *Why:* the text does not tell us, and the answer determines who is smarter in the scene and whose side the reader takes. A drawn face permits only one reading.
- **A2.2. Seriousness of her interest.** *Question:* how seriously does she consider the offer, and how does she feel about her own work? *Why:* “sounds tempting” and the final question can be performed with different weight. This changes the heroine's character and the possible continuation.
- **A2.3. Who recognizes the misunderstanding.** *Question:* does anyone within the scene see that the speakers are talking about different things? *Why:* the joke breaks off at a mismatch understood only by the reader. Give that knowledge to a character, and the joke becomes a different kind of scene.

**A3. Whether the profession is genuine.** *Question:* is “programmer” a fact of this world or merely the cat's words? *Why:* the resolution rests on one word. Whether the world confirms it determines whether the joke ends with an established fact or a new puzzle, and whether the image is entitled to present evidence (see V6).

**A4. Tone.** *Question:* what feeling should remain after the smile? *Why:* beneath the joke lie themes easily turned in different directions: a creature seen as a curiosity; an offer “on any terms”; the circus as a place of exhibition. This axis belongs in block A because it concerns meaning; colour, light and manner merely convey it.

**A5. Whose story this is.** *Question:* who is the main character? *Why:* the joke begins with the cat, its first strong feeling belongs to the waiter, and the last word is hers again. The choice of protagonist determines whose knowledge and ignorance the reader shares. The axis relates to V8 but is not identical: there the concern is optics, here it is fate.

**World**

**A6. The world's norm** (parent axis). *Question:* who is the anomaly here—the talking cat or the astonished waiter? *Why:* the cat behaves like an ordinary customer, the waiter like a witness to a miracle, and both norms are presented seriously. The image will have to show the world around them, and that world will take someone's side.
- **A6.1. Witnesses.** *Question:* are there others in the café, and how do they regard what is happening? *Why:* the text leaves the two in a void; a picture usually lacks that luxury.
- **A6.2. Integration.** *Question:* how integrated is the cat into the human order—money, documents, work? *Why:* the order and profession imply that she is; the waiter's surprise implies that she is not.
- **A6.3. Uniqueness.** *Question:* is she the only one of her kind? *Why:* the value of the waiter's offer depends on this.

**A7. Nature of the miracle.** *Question:* what explains the talking cat, and is an explanation needed? *Why:* the joke explains nothing, and that composure is part of its absurdity. Any explanation changes the genre and shifts the weight from the resolution to the world's construction.

**A8. Chronotope** (parent axis).
- **A8.1. Establishment.** *Question:* what kind of place is this, and what is its standing? *Why:* this determines what “waiter” and “order” mean, and who belongs here.
- **A8.2. Period.** *Question:* when does this happen? *Why:* “programmer” ties the action to the computer age, whereas the circus with an uncle as director and cotton candy pulls toward the past. The distance between these markers is a resource for the joke.
- **A8.3. Culture and geography.** *Question:* where does this happen? *Why:* cafés, circuses and programmers look and mean different things in different cultures.

**A9. The circus and the uncle.** *Question:* what lies behind the word “circus” in this world, and do the uncle and his circus exist outside the waiter's words? *Why:* the joke contains two circuses—the place offering work and the one the cat remembers—and they do not coincide. What the real one turns out to be decides what the offer amounted to.

**Characters**

**A10. The cat's personality.** *Question:* what is her disposition, how does it change across three lines, and how feline are her habits? *Why:* the text gives three different notes—a businesslike order, irritation and dreaminess—which must be assembled into one character. “Closes her eyes” is the only stage direction, simultaneously feline and human.

**A11. Sex, age and social circle.** *Question:* is “female cat” merely grammar or a characteristic of the heroine, how old is she, and what milieu does she belong to? *Why:* taking the feminine gender seriously gives the joke a second layer: a professional who is not seen as a professional. Her age affects how her childlike list of circus delights sounds.

**A12. Professional type.** *Question:* what image of the profession does the cat embody? *Why:* the reader instantly supplies a ready picture for the word “programmer.” The picture prepared determines who recognizes themselves in the heroine.

**A13. The waiter: personality and motive.** *Question:* what drives him to forget the order and offer the circus? *Why:* the text gives admiration, an uncle and “on any terms,” but does not say whose interests he pursues. His motive determines the offer's moral colouring and whether the final rebuff is deserved.

**A14. Distribution of status.** *Question:* which of the two is higher—in position, means, confidence, the right to control the situation? *Why:* formally they are customer and service worker, yet the waiter behaves as a benefactor, while the resolution suggests that the cat already has a job. The joke overturns a hierarchy, and we need to know which one.

**A15. Type or individual.** *Question:* are the characters the anonymous masks of a joke or individuals with biographies? *Why:* types make a joke brief. Individualization turns it into a scene from someone's life and opens the way to a series (V1), but slows the joke.

**Time and frame**

**A16. Before.** *Question:* how did the cat get here, and is this the first time she has heard something like this? *Why:* her irritation in the second line sounds different depending on whether this reaction is familiar. It also decides why this particular waiter is surprised.

**A17. After.** *Question:* does the story end with the last line, and what happens to the order and the misunderstanding? *Why:* the joke breaks off on an unanswered question, and the coffee has still not arrived. Any continuation resolves the mismatch in someone's favor.

**A18. Distance from the text.** *Question:* how far may the image change, supplement and reinterpret the plot? *Why:* this traveling story has versions with other animals and professions. The author decides what to remain faithful to—the words, the joke's mechanism or only its motif.

**A19. Intended audience.** *Question:* who is this for, and what does the viewer already know? *Why:* “programmer” works differently for insiders and outsiders, and a viewer familiar with the joke needs another source of pleasure rather than surprise. The axis belongs in A rather than V: the audience primarily determines the meaning that can be read; format follows.

## B. The director-artist's axes

**B1. Visual language** (parent axis; list in section G). *Question:* in what tradition, canon and genre is this drawn? *Why:* no language is neutral; each arrives with its own world.
- **B1.1. Genre perspective.** *Question:* through what genre is the scene seen? *Why:* genre brings ready-made roles for both characters and its own understanding of a “job offer.”
- **B1.2. The period evoked by the style.** *Question:* to what period does the manner itself refer, and does it match the period of the action (A8.2)? *Why:* stylisation can side with the circus, side with the programmer or bring them into collision.
- **B1.3. Cultural school.** *Question:* whose visual tradition tells this story? *Why:* each school has its own “cats in human roles” and its own attitude toward them.
- **B1.4. Whose world dictates the style.** *Question:* does one of the joke's three worlds—the café, circus or programmer's world—determine the graphics? *Why:* a style borrowed from one world makes its viewpoint the norm and may reveal the resolution before the words do.
- **B1.5. Quotation.** *Question:* does the image draw on recognizable images created by others? *Why:* a talking cat in an establishment has famous predecessors, especially for Russian-speaking readers, and they arrive with their own personalities.

**B2. Degree of stylisation.** *Question:* how far does the depicted world obey the laws of the real one? *Why:* a miracle is possible only against a norm. The more stylised the drawing, the more natural a talking cat becomes within it and the stranger the waiter's surprise.

**B3. Degree of grotesque exaggeration and acting.** *Question:* do the graphics themselves joke, or seriously depict something unserious? *Why:* the joke's humor is dry and verbal; an exaggerated drawing can duplicate or drown it out. Furthermore, the person the drawing caricatures most strongly is the one laughed at (A1).

**B4. Consistency of the characters' graphic conventions.** *Question:* are the cat and waiter drawn by the same rules? *Why:* the two live by different norms, and graphics can make that difference literally visible. This determines whether they belong to one world.

**B5. The cat's anthropomorphism** (parent axis). *Question:* how much of a cat is the cat? *Why:* see the sub-axes. This axis belongs in B although it also concerns character. The text says only “cat”; the degree of the human emerges only on paper, and a screenwriter cannot specify it without describing appearance.
- **B5.1. Body, pose, paws.** *Question:* how is she built, and how does she use human things? *Why:* she must enter, sit, order and perhaps hold a cup, and each action requires a decision.
- **B5.2. Scale.** *Question:* how big is she relative to the waiter and furniture? *Why:* the entire staging and who looks down on whom depend on this.
- **B5.3. Clothing and belongings.** *Question:* does she have anything besides fur? *Why:* any object speaks of status and occupation and thus relates to disclosure (V6).
- **B5.4. Breed, coat and grooming.** *Question:* what kind of cat is she? *Why:* appearance is read as origin and standing, while coat colours have cultural meanings.
- **B5.5. Vocabulary of facial expressions.** *Question:* does she perform through human or feline expressions? *Why:* the ending requires a face on which the answer to A2.1 can be read.

**B6. The waiter's appearance.** *Question:* what does he look like—age, uniform, bearing, degree of caricature? *Why:* he represents the “normal” world while violating a professional norm. His appearance communicates the establishment's standing and the value of his offer.

**B7. Marking the miracle.** *Question:* does the image single the cat out as extraordinary or present her on equal terms with everything else? *Why:* in this way the picture votes for the waiter's or the cat's viewpoint before any dialogue.

**B8. Environment.** *Question:* how fully is the café shown, and what do its details tell us? *Why:* the interior is the principal carrier of period, standing and norm. A detailed environment also provides a place to conceal or accidentally reveal information about the heroine.

**B9. Colour.** *Question:* what is the palette, and does colour have a dramatic role? *Why:* the everyday café and festive circus exist on different planes in the joke. Colour is a natural way to separate reality and imagination.

**B10. Light.** *Question:* what light illuminates the scene, and where is it directed? *Why:* light establishes time of day and mood. In a story in which the heroine is invited into the ring, it is not neutral: it can put her on display before the waiter's words.

**B11. Material and texture.** *Question:* with what and how is this made? *Why:* a handmade quality and digital cleanness carry different tones. In a joke where the showman's craft meets the programmer's craft, the material itself takes someone's side.

## V. Comics and storyboarding technique

**V1. Format and medium** (parent to V2–V4). *Question:* one image or a sequence, a standalone work or an episode in a series, and where and how will it be viewed? *Why:* the joke is a dialogue with a delayed resolution. A single picture must fit it into one moment, a sequence must distribute it, and the platform determines how much text and time the viewer has.

**V2. Segmentation.** *Question:* how many panels, of what size and shape, and what gets a panel of its own? *Why:* the text contains a setup, three exchanges of dialogue, a stage direction and a resolution, and they are not equal in weight. Whatever receives its own panel becomes an event.

**V3. Choice of moments.** *Question:* which moments are shown, and which remain between panels or outside the picture? *Why:* the joke contains almost no spectacular events; the main thing is spoken. For a single image, this is the choice of the sole moment that must hold both the miracle and the resolution.

**V4. Order of narration.** *Question:* is the story told in the order in which it happened? *Why:* the joke is built on withheld information, and rearranging it changes the very type of comedy.

**V5. Rhythm** (parent axis).
- **V5.1. Pause before the resolution.** *Question:* how much time is given to “closes her eyes dreamily”? *Why:* the text contains a false trail: the cat seems to agree, and the length of this “almost yes” determines the sharpness of the turn.
- **V5.2. Static or changing view.** *Question:* does the camera position change from panel to panel? *Why:* the action amounts to two people talking. A static view makes the slightest facial change an event; a moving one shifts the event into the editing.

**V6. Managing disclosure** (parent axis). In content, this is both a screenwriting question (what is known about the heroine) and an artistic one (what she looks like). The axis belongs in V because its essence is not what is true but when the reader learns it.
- **V6.1. Revealing the cat.** *Question:* when and how does the reader learn that the customer is a cat? *Why:* in the text it is the very first word, but readers can share the waiter's astonishment only by learning it with him.
- **V6.2. Revealing the profession.** *Question:* when and through which channel—word, image or both—does the reader learn that she is a programmer? *Why:* this is the central difficulty: an image shows everything at once and can get ahead of the line.
- **V6.3. Clues and rereading.** *Question:* is there something in the image noticed only once the resolution is known? *Why:* this determines whether it is a one-use joke or a picture to return to.

**V7. Position of the resolution and the following panel.** *Question:* where on the page does the final line sit, and is there anything after it? *Why:* in comics, timing is a location on the page. A panel after the resolution answers A17, which the joke leaves open.

**V8. Focalization.** *Question:* through whose eyes is the scene shown? *Why:* the joke contains two incompatible views of the same thing. A camera taking someone's side makes that view the norm.

**V9. Camera height and angle.** *Question:* from what height and at what angle are we looking? *Why:* the characters differ in height; one sits, the other stands. Camera level always belongs to someone, and the angle literally depicts who looks down on whom.

**V10. Shot size and framing.** *Question:* how much of the world enters the frame, and what is cut off? *Why:* a wide shot shows the world's norm (A6); a close-up shows the facial nuances on which the ending depends. The frame is also the simplest way of hiding something.

**V11. Staging and order of dialogue.** *Question:* how are the characters positioned relative to one another, the table and the frame edges? *Why:* the figures' positions establish the order in which lines are read and the distance between the speakers. Between them stand a table and the waiter's undone work.

**V12. Who is in the frame when a line is spoken.** *Question:* should we show the speaker or the listener, especially at the final phrase? *Why:* this determines whether the reader laughs with someone or at someone (A1).

**V13. Word and image.** *Question:* what part of the joke remains text, what becomes drawing, and what is cut? *Why:* the joke is almost entirely verbal, including its resolution. For a joke whose meaning lies in one word, the division of labour between text and picture is a matter of survival.

**V14. Narrator and paratext.** *Question:* does the story have a narrator's voice, title or caption? *Why:* the joke is an oral genre, and the direction about “closing her eyes” belongs to the narrator. The title is read before everything else and can either prepare the resolution or give it away.

**V15. Lettering and voice.** *Question:* what does the characters' speech look like, and does the cat's speech differ from human speech? *Why:* the miracle in the joke is specifically speech. Intonations (stupefaction, irritation, dreaminess) are specified in the text through punctuation and directions; in comics, balloon shape and lettering carry them.

**V16. Inner images.** *Question:* should we show what the characters imagine, and how should the imaginary be distinguished from what happens? *Why:* the misunderstanding is a mismatch between two pictures in two heads. Showing them makes the joke visible, but risks explaining it before or instead of the resolution. The axis belongs in V: the style of the imaginary is the artist's concern, but the decisive question is whether and when to include these images in the narrative.

## G. Visual languages and what they bring

**Cartoons and strips**
- **Single-panel magazine cartoon with a caption (the New Yorker tradition).** An animal in a bar or office is a genre norm that surprises nobody: A6 is decided against the waiter, and the tone is deadpan. The form requires choosing one moment and giving the resolution to the caption (V3, V13).
- **Soviet and post-Soviet magazine cartoons (Krokodil and its successors).** Bring satire of manners and the service sector as a familiar target: A1 shifts to the waiter, A8 to recognizable public catering, and the programmer becomes a research-institute engineer (A12).
- **Three- or four-panel newspaper strip.** Brings a “setup—pause—punch” scheme and stock characters (A15), but seriality immediately encourages a recurring character.
- **Yonkoma.** A four-part structure with a turn in the third panel requires a fourth, an “after”: A17 ceases to be open.
- **Office and IT webcomics (schematic figures).** The audience is insiders (A19), the target is the profession and its self-image (A1). Extreme schematization almost removes the question of B5.
- **Memes and internet images.** The cat is the internet's totem animal, at home here, and “programmer” sounds almost tautological: A6 sides with the cat.
- **Underground and expressionist grotesque.** Bring anger and social criticism: “any terms” and the circus read as exploitation (A4), and the waiter as a wheeler-dealer (A13).

**Anthropomorphic animals**
- **Classic funny animals (the golden age of animation and comics).** All animals talk, so the miracle disappears and the waiter's astonishment is inexplicable. The language requires a human waiter and an answer to how this cat differs from the others (A6, A7).
- **European animal comics where species is a character type.** The world is entirely animal; the miracle is abolished. A10–A12 are resolved through breed.
- **Fable engravings and nineteenth-century satirical animal art (Grandville).** Animals are allegories of social estates. The joke gains a moral and a social target (A1); the period is the past.
- **Edwardian postcards of feline society (Louis Wain).** Humans are replaced by cats: if the waiter is also a cat, the joke collapses; if human, he becomes the curiosity (A6 is reversed).
- **Cozy children's book illustration (British and Soviet schools).** A clothed talking animal is an unquestioned norm; the tone is warm. The cat is sincere (A2.1), the circus a genuine celebration (A9), and satire recedes.
- **The fairy-tale and lubok cat—the learned cat, Puss in Boots, the Cat of Kazan.** The cat as a trickster smarter than humans: A2.1 leans toward guile, A14 favors the cat, and the time is that of fairy tales.
- **The Bulgakov canon (Behemoth in a Moscow establishment).** Brings devilry, the 1930s and a provocateur cat: A7 is supernatural evil, A2.1 mockery, and a scandal with the staff is expected (A17).
- **Japanese cats in human roles—ukiyo-e, bakeneko, maneki-neko.** The cat either parodies townspeople or is a shapeshifter (A7: the mystical). The establishment becomes a teahouse, and the cat a lucky emblem of the establishment itself.
- **Manga and anime: kawaii, neko characters.** B5 is immediately resolved as a girl with ears; A11 as a young heroine. The miracle vanishes; the tone is endearing.
- **Furry and contemporary anthropomorphic illustration.** A human body with a species identity in a world of similar beings: species is a personality trait rather than a curiosity (A6), and the waiter's question sounds tactless.
- **Soviet animation with a practical, domestically capable talking cat.** Brief surprise, after which the cat proves more practical than humans: A14 favors the cat; the tone is good-natured irony.
- **Adult animated series with flat graphics.** Animals live among humans without comment. The cat is a tired adult with work and bills (A10, A12); the tone is sarcastic with bitterness.
- **Puppet and clay animation.** Everyone is equally handmade: B4 is resolved through unity; the world is theatrical convention, the tone warm and slightly sad.

**Painting, posters and “serious” languages**
- **Realist genre scene.** The world obeys physics, so the cat is a genuine miracle and the waiter is right (A6). A6.2 has to be addressed seriously.
- **Photography, photomontage, documentary sketch.** The image functions as evidence: the event “happened,” the world is ours, and the time is now (A8.2). A7 becomes an insistent question.
- **Magic realism and surrealism.** The miracle is presented without explanation or surprise (A7, A6). The joke drifts into quiet strangeness.
- **Painting of urban solitude (a night café in the manner of Hopper).** Brings night, alienation and fatigue: the cat is a solitary worker after a shift (A16); A4 is melancholy.
- **The Parisian café of the Impressionists and Toulouse-Lautrec.** A period when café and circus are neighbors and both real (A9, A8.2). “Programmer” becomes an anachronism, changing the nature of the resolution.
- **Art Nouveau and cabaret posters (Steinlen, Le Chat Noir).** The cat is an emblem of the establishment and the spectacle: the language already sides with the waiter; she is already a star (B7, A1).
- **Circus posters and fairground bills.** The style comes from the uncle's world (B1.4): the picture itself sells the cat as an attraction, and the resolution argues with the image rather than the waiter.
- **Sign painting and naive art (Pirosmani).** The tradition of tavern signs featuring feasts and animals: provincial timelessness, directness, no surprise (A6, A4).
- **Marginalia and the “world turned upside down” (medieval drolleries).** Animals at human occupations as carnivalesque parody: an inverted world is the rule (A6), and humans in general are the target (A1).
- **Agitprop posters and Constructivism.** Bring the rhetoric of labour: profession is dignity, circus is idleness. A1 shifts toward “you are your work”; the tone is ironically heroic.
- **Mid-twentieth-century magazine and advertising illustration, retrofuturism.** Programming is a new heroic profession, while the circus is still mass entertainment: both markers coincide in time (A8.2); the cat is a white-collar worker (A12).
- **Fashion illustration and glossy-magazine sketches.** The cat is an elegant customer in an expensive establishment: A14 is resolved in her favor; the target is the waiter's awkwardness.

**Genre perspectives**
- **Noir.** A night bar, a tired heroine, someone with a dubious offer: the last line becomes a biting retort (A2.1), the uncle a shady figure (A9).
- **Mainstream superhero and adventure comics.** A miracle demands an origin (A7), “programmer” becomes a civilian identity (A3), and the story asks for continuation (A17).
- **Science fiction and cyberpunk.** The explanation is ready—technology (A7); a programmer is natural, a circus archaic. The waiter turns out to be behind the times (A6).
- **Horror and Gothic.** The cat is a being of another order, the circus a freak show, the offer a trap (A9, A13). A4 darkens.
- **Silent cinema and slapstick.** Dialogue moves into intertitles (V13, V14), and acting is exaggerated (B3). The period is the early twentieth century, when the circus is contemporary but a programmer impossible.
- **Autobiographical graphic novels, slice of life.** Everyday life, fatigue, psychological detail: the characters are individuals (A15), the joke an episode in someone's life, and A2.2 leans toward “she really wouldn't mind.”

**Languages of signs and the programmer's world**
- **Pixel art and old-game aesthetics.** The style comes from the heroine's world (B1.4): she belongs here, while the waiter is a character with a preset line. The resolution is partly given away in advance (V6.2).
- **Interface: chat, terminal, ASCII.** The cat's body disappears, leaving speech: the miracle must be justified anew (A7). But the theme “online, no one can see what you are” emerges (A6).
- **Diagram, flowchart, pictogram, instruction sheet.** Remove personalities and emotions, leaving the logic of the misunderstanding—two classifications of one object. A1 is the category error itself; the audience is insiders (A19).
- **Corporate flat illustration.** This is the language of the IT industry and its HR departments: the waiter's offer reads as clumsy recruitment (A13); hiring culture is the target.
- **Ligne claire (“clear line”).** Stylized characters in a credible environment: the world is detailed (A8), both characters are drawn on equal terms (B4), and the tone is even. This is a rare case in which A6 remains almost open.
