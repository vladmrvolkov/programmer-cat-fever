## **Concept 1. “The Wrong Specialist”**

### **Concept**

The waiter is astonished by a talking cat and immediately regards her as a future circus attraction. The cat considers herself an ordinary customer with a profession and a specific order. She does not reject the offer, but sincerely asks why the circus needs her professional skills.

**Meaning of the ending:** a human sees an exceptional outward characteristic but misses the personality and competence of the individual before him. Common sense belongs to the cat.

Before the final line, the viewer sees an unusual customer and an enterprising waiter. Afterwards, they understand that the two characters have fundamentally different ideas about the possible job.

The cat's actual qualifications, the circus's real need for programmers, and the outcome of the offer remain open.

### **Key axes and visible decisions**

- **L36 — “The main subject of comedy”:** misjudging abilities. The waiter admires the very fact of a talking cat, while she regards the offer as professional.
- **L37 — “The bearer of common sense”:** the cat. Her composure contrasts with the waiter's expressive reaction.
- **L51 — “The cat's self-definition”:** profession matters more than unusual appearance. She behaves as an independent adult customer.
- **L64 — “The waiter's motive”:** an enthusiastic desire to find a sensation for the circus, without malice. He shows the cat an advertising brochure depicting the ring.
- **L201 — “Revealing the profession”:** only in the final caption. No laptops, code or other direct clues appear in the image.
- **L147 — “The visual role of the order”:** the empty place before the cat and the cup the waiter has left on the counter remind us that her request remains unfulfilled.

On the right, a waiter in a white shirt and dark apron leans towards her. His mouth is slightly open, his eyes wide; his hand with the circus brochure reaches forward. His silhouette is diagonal and fidgety; the cat is almost motionless.

> “A circus?.. Sounds tempting. But what would they need a programmer for?”

The scene itself conveys the beginning of the conversation, without separate narration.

**Coordination of decisions.** The adopted constraint is a single image, so poses and reading order replace extended temporal pauses. Magazine cartoons are associated with deadpan absurdity but do not establish the world's laws. **Authorial addition:** the waiter has already produced an advertising brochure, although the joke never mentions one. ### **Concept**

This is a deliberate reinterpretation of the joke. The cat really works as a programmer, and the circus really needs a developer for its electronic ticketing system. But neither the cat nor the waiter knows about the coincidence.

The waiter offers a job because he sees a future circus act in the talking cat. The cat is sure that her speciality is of no use at the circus.

**Meaning of the ending:** both characters make mistaken assumptions, although they could potentially reach an agreement. The viewer knows more than either of them, making the final line ironic.

Before the ending, the viewer already knows the cat's profession and notices the job advertisement. Her question adds another comic layer: the circus does need a programmer, but the characters are discussing an entirely different job.

Whether they notice the coincidence and whether she gets the job remain open.

### **Key axes and visible decisions**

- **L42 — “The connection between programming and the circus”:** the circus genuinely needs a technical specialist. A visible job advertisement confirms this.
- **L58 — “The truth of the profession”:** the cat really is a programmer. She is shown fixing code, rather than merely sitting beside a laptop.
- **L70 — “The distribution of knowledge within the scene”:** the waiter does not know the cat's profession; the cat does not know about the circus vacancy. The viewer sees both pieces of information.
- **L64 — “The waiter's motive”:** wanting to acquire an unusual performer. His gesture points at a circus drawing with acrobats, rather than the vacancy text.
- **L149 — “Visual signs of programming”:** a working code editor, checking tests, meaningful interaction with the laptop.
- **L113 — “Whose world dictates the style”:** IT's language encompasses even the café and conversation. But pixel form does not mean that the characters are literally inside a video game.

### **Manner and coordination**

Pixel graphics with clear, stepped silhouettes, as in contemporary independent games. The cat is short, with a large head and expressive eyes; the waiter is elongated, in a geometric apron. Both follow the same drawing rules, preserving the world's coherence.

The palette: dark blue, the café's warm amber lighting, a turquoise laptop glow, and purple circus accents. Dialogue uses rectangular windows with a pixel font; decorative images distinct from the real background convey the circus dream.

**Adopted rule:** three successive panels with an early profession reveal. Pixel art is used as a familiar association with computer culture, rather than an indication of a historical period or game NPCs.

**My additions:** Orbit Circus, the ticketing-developer vacancy, the cat's actual professional experience, and the advertisement in the café. These new circumstances turn the original joke about external judgement into a comedy of mutual ignorance.
