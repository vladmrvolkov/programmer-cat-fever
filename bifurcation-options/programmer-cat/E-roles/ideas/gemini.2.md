The three concepts for visualising the joke are arranged to distribute knowledge differently between viewer and characters, offer contrasting genre frameworks and use different narrative formats.

---

## Variant 1. Single illustration: “The Impresario's Blindness”

### Developing the overall concept

**What is happening, what it means, and open questions**
The cat is anatomically an ordinary animal, but also a tired remote developer who has come into a morning café for coffee and a pastry and to finish her code in peace (an authorial addition). At the sound of her voice, the waiter instantly loses interest in service: the excitement of an operator who has spotted a living gold mine awakens in him. The cat's final line carries no sarcasm — it is a practical assessment of a recruiter's offer: “the benefits package with candy floss is excellent, but what tasks do you have that match my profile?” Where the cat's intelligence and speech come from, whether the uncle-director really exists or the waiter is bluffing to keep a rare animal there, and whether she will finally get her coffee are deliberately left open.

**Visual language**
The basis is a clash of two graphic systems within one sheet: the real café world is rendered in the tradition of **Franco-Belgian *ligne claire***, while a translucent decorative arch in the style of an **Art Nouveau vintage circus poster** grows around the cat from the waiter's gesture. Through its even, clear contours, local colours and object clarity, *ligne claire* establishes the scene's objective everyday reality. The invading poster graphics, with vignettes, stars and acrobat silhouettes, make visible the waiter's commercial fantasy: he is already mentally selling the cat as a fairground attraction.

**Format and the dynamics of viewer knowledge**
A single illustration (**one image**) is chosen, with dramaturgy built on the eye's downward path. Before reading the final line at the bottom, the viewer sees not only the waiter's circus projection and the dreamily closed-eyed cat, but also an open ultrabook in front of her, showing a code editor and bearing IT-conference stickers (an authorial addition). The viewer reads her profession before the waiter does, so the final line works not as sudden absurdity but as an exact verbal hit exposing the human's selective blindness.

**Coordinating decisions**
The constraint of the chosen **single-image** format is the need to combine several phases of dialogue in one staging and establish a strict top-to-bottom reading order. The adopted *ligne-claire* canon requires abandoning tonal hatching and blur in the café's real space. The conventional association of the **vintage circus poster** style is spectacle, fairground entertainment and turning a living being into advertised merchandise. A free authorial interpretation (and my addition) is the cat's specific work equipment — a laptop with an open terminal, a hardware security key in its port, reading glasses on the table. These do not follow from *ligne claire*, but are introduced as material evidence against the waiter's illusions.

### Brief presentation of the concept

1. **Title and main idea**: “The Impresario's Blindness” — a single-panel illustration about a man whose desire for profit makes him see a circus animal before him while completely overlooking the IT worker at the table.

2. **Key axes chosen and decisions**:
* **The main subject of comedy**: The waiter's mistaken assessment of the cat's abilities, while her profession is completely obvious to the viewer.

* **Whose world dictates the style**: A clash between the café's material world (*ligne claire*) and the circus-poster world imposed by the waiter.

* **Indirect informational clues**: An early visual clue to the profession through work belongings on the table, before the verbal punchline.

* **The cat's anatomical anthropomorphism**: Fully feline anatomy with human everyday independence.

* **The division of labour between word and image**: Text is shortened to the offer and reply, while the background of the order and profession is conveyed through objects.

3. **How the decisions become visible in the image**:
* The clash of viewpoints appears through flat golden-red poster graphics superimposed on the café's clear contour drawing.

* The waiter's mistaken assessment is visible in his presenting hand literally blocking his own view of the laptop screen.

* The undelivered order is shown through an empty porcelain stand and a coffee pot forgotten on his tray.

### Description of the concept

* **Specific scene and action**: The morning terrace of a European café; the waiter has frozen beside the table, pressing a tray with unserved coffee and an éclair to his chest with one hand while tracing an imaginary circus dome in the air with the other, in front of the cat at her laptop.

* **The characters' appearance, poses and expressions**: An ordinary short-haired black-and-white cat sits on a high stack of dictionaries (an authorial addition), her paw on the touchpad, eyes blissfully closed; the waiter in a formal waistcoat leans forward with burning eyes and an ingratiating smile.

* **Composition and significant details**: A diagonal composition leads the eye from the waiter's face and raised hand (upper left), through a decorative arc of jugglers and lollipops, to the cat's face and laptop screen with coloured syntax highlighting (lower right).

* **Visual manner, colour and light**: A clear black contour of uniform thickness (*ligne claire*), a restrained bluish-ochre morning-café palette, and the contrasting warm glow of the imagined poster (carmine, gold), picking the cat out as if with a stage spotlight.

* **Dialogue and placement**: At upper left above the waiter, an italic speech balloon: “Hold on a second… Listen, could you use a job? It's just, my uncle runs a circus, and he'd gladly take you on, on any terms…” Below the cat's table, a balloon divided into two joined boxes: the first in soft rounded lettering (“A circus, you say? That's the place with the acrobats, the jugglers, the lollipops, the candy floss? Sounds tempting.”), the second in a strict monospaced terminal font (“But what would they need a programmer for?”).

## Variant 2. Four-panel manga (*yonkoma*): “Candy Floss and a Cold Stack”

### Developing the overall concept

**What is happening, what it means, and open questions**
Both characters are completely sincere: the waiter is delighted by a miracle and genuinely wants to arrange a future for the unusual visitor, while the cat is seriously enchanted by the circus as a celebration with unlimited sweets. In her dream she sees herself not as a performer in the ring, but as a happy spectator with a giant cloud of candy floss (an authorial addition). The final line means an abrupt collision between this childlike hedonism and her adult professional identity: she sincerely tries to understand how a programmer could benefit a circus so that she can legitimately receive lollipops there. The truth of her profession is deliberately left open (is she really a developer, or does she merely seriously define herself as one?), as is whether the uncle-director will find her a vacancy.

**Visual language**
The language is Japanese black-and-white manga with abrupt shifts of genre register (**gag manga**). The first and second panels are drawn in the calm manner of **slice-of-life seinen manga** (clean thin contours, a credible everyday coffee shop). The third explodes into the visual codes of **shōjo manga** and ***kawaii***: soft rounded proportions, radiant screentones, stars and flowers around a peaceful little face. The fourth instantly switches to stern **gekiga** graphics with dense cross-hatching, sharp shadows and an utterly serious gaze.

**Format and the dynamics of viewer knowledge**
A vertical strip of four panels is chosen (**exactly four panels** — *yonkoma*). Until the last panel there is not a single hint of technology or computers (**revealing the profession** is reserved strictly for the final text). Before the last line, the viewer thinks the joke concerns how easily an independent cat can be bought with a promise of sweets. After the final line, panel four completely reverses what was read: the viewer realises that the cat was assessing the circus not as a performance venue but as a potential employer with good benefits.

**Coordinating decisions**
The **yonkoma** canon imposes a strict four-part structure (setup — development — climax of the dream — punchline) and cuts the story off exactly at the punchline, without showing the waiter's subsequent reaction. The conventional association of **shōjo / *kawaii*** in panel three is the heroine's naïveté and compliance, and of **gekiga** hatching in panel four, stern businesslike concentration. The free authorial interpretation is that the cute treatment in panel three implies neither stupidity nor agreement to perform in the ring: she sincerely dreams of sweets while remaining a calculating specialist.

### Brief presentation of the concept

1. **Title and main idea**: “Candy Floss and a Cold Stack” — a four-panel strip where the cat's sincere delight in circus sweets suddenly runs up against her stern programmer's pragmatism.

2. **Key axes chosen and decisions**:
* **Form of visual storytelling**: Japanese four-panel manga (*yonkoma*), read vertically.

* **The pause of the cat's imagination**: The dreamy state receives a separate third panel with a change of graphic register.

* **Revealing the profession**: No advance visual clues whatsoever; revelation occurs only in the last sentence.

* **Attitude to the offer**: The cat is genuinely tempted, but assesses the offer through her speciality.

* **Exit point**: The story ends directly on the cat's question, in a close-up of her face.

3. **How the decisions become visible in the image**:
* The dream's sincerity is conveyed by replacing the café background in panel three with a glowing screentone featuring floating lollipops, a tent and jugglers.

* The shift to professional calculation in panel four is expressed by the instant disappearance of the stars, hard vertical shadow lines on the cat's forehead, and heavy angular lettering.

### Description of the concept

* **Specific scene and action**: An intimate urban coffee shop; a fluffy cat sits on a high bar stool at the counter, talking to a young barista-waiter across it.

* **The characters' appearance, poses and expressions**: A normally built cat with a neat bow tie on her collar (an authorial addition initially read as part of a “circus” image); the waiter wears an apron, with wide eyes and sweat drops from excitement.

* **Panel contents and sequence**:
1. *Panel 1 (Medium shot)*: The cat impassively taps the menu at the counter with a paw; the waiter drops his notepad in shock.

2. *Panel 2 (Over the cat's shoulder)*: The waiter bends across the counter, gesturing enthusiastically with both hands and offering a job with his uncle.

3. *Panel 3 (Close-up, shōjo/kawaii register)*: The cat dreamily closes her eyes, paws pressed to fluffy cheeks; trapeze acrobats, jugglers' clubs, lollipops and clouds of candy floss circle around her against a sparkling background.

4. *Panel 4 (Extreme close-up of the cat's eyes and muzzle, gekiga register)*: The cat opens one eye; dense dramatic shadows outline her face, her expression intensely businesslike and puzzled.

* **Visual manner, colour and light**: Black-and-white ink drawing with screentones; a sharp light-and-shadow contrast between the dazzlingly bright third panel and the gloomy, hatched fourth.

* **Dialogue and placement**:
* *Panel 1*: Waiter (an uneven, trembling balloon): “You're a cat? A talking cat?! Incredible…” Cat (a smooth oval balloon): “Yes, I'm a cat. Are you bringing my order or not?”

* *Panel 2*: Waiter: “Hold on a second… Listen, could you use a job? It's just, my uncle runs a circus, and he'd gladly take you on, on any terms…”

* *Panel 3*: Cat (an airy balloon with a floral outline): “A circus, you say? That's the place with the acrobats, the jugglers, the lollipops, the candy floss? Sounds tempting.”

* *Panel 4*: Cat (an angular rectangular balloon with bold lettering at the bottom): “But what would they need a programmer for?”

## Variant 3. A three-panel satirical sheet: “Lunch Break at the Computing Centre”

### Developing the overall concept

**What is happening, what it means, and open questions**
The action is moved to the Soviet era of flourishing cybernetics in the 1960s: a glass-fronted cafeteria beside the Computing Centre (an authorial addition). The waiter is a typical crafty counter worker with “connections”, who has seized a chance to place a talking cat with his uncle, a state-circus administrator, and collect his percentage. The cat is a seasoned programming-laboratory employee. Her dreamy eye-closing and list of sweets are a subtle, caustic game of letting him win: she lets the waiter believe she can be bought with a lollipop, then uses a single sentence to put his patronising nepotism in its place. The origin of her intelligence (a bionic research-institute experiment or a convention of a satirical fairy tale in the Strugatskys' manner) is deliberately left open, as is whether her irony is pure mockery or conceals fatigue from emergency computer work.

**Visual language**
The chosen language is **1960s Soviet satirical magazine cartooning** (in the manner of *Krokodil* and Evgeny Migunov's illustrations), combined with elements of **Constructivist poster graphics** in the final panel. A lively, prickly ink line with watercolour-gouache patches and grotesque movement immediately establishes a framework of social satire about labour, bureaucracy and judging people by their clothes.

**Format and the dynamics of viewer knowledge**
The image is a magazine page of three asymmetrical panels (two medium-sized upper panels and one wide lower panel across the whole sheet). Before the final line, an attentive viewer may notice a bulging cardboard folder with punched cards protruding on the chair beside the cat (an authorial addition), but the detail does not advertise itself and reads as a modest period accessory. In the broad final panel, after the cat's line, attention is divided between her ironic squint and the waiter slumping in bewilderment, making this self-assured “master of connections” the target of the joke.

**Coordinating decisions**
The chosen format of a captioned satirical magazine triptych requires distributing the dialogue across three distinct stagings while maintaining an outside observer's distance. The conventional association of **1960s Soviet satirical graphics** is the conflict between petty-bourgeois wheeling and dealing and the prestigious work of physicists and cyberneticists. The free authorial interpretation (and my addition) is the specific historical placement of programming in the era of punched cards and large computers, and the reading of the cat's final sentence as a deliberate sarcastic trap rather than simple bewilderment.

### Brief presentation of the concept

1. **Title and main idea**: “Lunch Break at the Computing Centre” — a satirical triptych in the aesthetic of Thaw-era cartooning, in which a cyberneticist cat subtly mocks a shirking waiter trying to place her in the circus through patronage (blat).

2. **Key axes chosen and decisions**:
* **Historical and technological period**: The 1960s, the era of early computers, punched cards and the cult of cybernetics.

* **The intention of the final line**: Subtle irony and sabotage of the waiter's overfamiliar offer under an appearance of thoughtfulness.

* **The waiter's social role**: An enterprising service worker who thinks in terms of connections and easy money.

* **Degree of caricatural deformation**: Contrast between the waiter's ingratiating S-shaped bend and the cat's composed, stable silhouette.

* **The post-punchline pause**: The waiter's crestfallen reaction is included in the wide final panel alongside the cat's line.

3. **How the decisions become visible in the image**:
* Period and profession appear through the modernist “Glass Box” café interior, a soda siphon and a pack of characteristically perforated punched cards in the cat's folder.

* The ironic turn in panel three is expressed through an inset Constructivist thought cloud in which a circus juggler tries to juggle punched cards and reels of magnetic tape (an authorial addition).

### Description of the concept

* **Specific scene and action**: A 1960s café-patisserie with panoramic windows, beyond which stands an institute façade marked “Computing Centre”; a conversation at a triangular, slender-legged table.

* **The characters' appearance, poses and expressions**: A large tabby with a nicked ear sits upright and solidly, resting a paw on a tied folder; a tall, stooped waiter in a white jacket with a napkin over his arm bends lower and lower towards her from panel to panel, until he finally freezes in a ridiculous stupor.

* **Panel contents and sequence**:
1. *Panel 1 (Upper left)*: Beside a cash register and a display of basket-shaped pastries, the waiter spreads his hands in astonishment, mouth open; the cat at the table taps her tail impatiently.

2. *Panel 2 (Upper right)*: The waiter looms conspiratorially over the table, covering his mouth with his palm and pointing his thumb outside towards a round advertising column with a “CIRCUS” poster; the cat listens with her head tilted.

3. *Panel 3 (Wide lower panel)*: The cat closes her eyes with a sly half-smile; above her, inside a graphic inset outline, a Constructivist acrobat holds a roll of punched tape and a rooster lollipop in bewilderment; at right the waiter goes limp, dropping the napkin to the floor.

* **Visual manner, colour and light**: Free pen-and-ink drawing with dynamic hatching and local watercolour fills offset from the contours (ochre, muted turquoise, vermilion on the warm background of yellowish magazine paper).

* **Dialogue and placement**: Text appears beneath each panel in the magazine-satire tradition (a serif typeface in the manner of Literaturnaya):

* *Beneath panel 1*:
“— You're a cat? A talking cat?! Incredible…
— Yes, I'm a cat. Are you bringing my order or not?”

* *Beneath panel 2*:
“— Hold on a second… Listen, could you use a job? It's just, my uncle runs a circus, and he'd gladly take you on, on any terms…”

* *Beneath panel 3*:
“— A circus, you say? That's the place with the acrobats, the jugglers, the lollipops, the candy floss? Sounds tempting. **But what would they need a programmer for?**” (The last sentence is set in bold italic with expanded letter spacing.)
