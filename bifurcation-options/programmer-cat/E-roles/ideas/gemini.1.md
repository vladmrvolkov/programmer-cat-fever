The three concepts for visualising the joke use different ways of managing the viewer's knowledge, different formats and visual languages. Each resolves the story's central conflict in its own way: the clash between the outward perception of the cat as a curiosity and her professional self-definition.

---

## Concept 1. “Poster for an Attraction That Never Happened”

### Overall concept

* **The characters' relationship and the meaning of the ending:** The waiter sees the talking cat solely as a commercial curiosity and tries to act as his uncle's impresario, while the cat is a pragmatic specialist who has dropped in for a snack. Her dreamy pause is sincere (she really likes sweets and spectacles), but her final question is entirely unironic: she is practically assessing a vacancy in her field and does not understand what task she would have to solve in a circus. Why she can talk, whether she is the only such cat in the world, and whether she will eventually get her coffee are deliberately left open.

* **Visual language:** A combination of a **vintage circus poster** and an **Art Nouveau cabaret poster**, with a reference to Théophile Steinlen. This language supports a reading in which the image's very form tries to “sell” the cat as a fairground wonder, while the final line argues not only with the waiter but with the entire picture's decorative frame. Recognisable features: flowing floral and ribbon ornaments of Art Nouveau, decorative display lettering, warm lithographic texture, and a compositional spotlight illuminating the central figure.

* **Format and the dynamics of viewer knowledge:** A single illustration read vertically from top to bottom. Before reading the lower caption, the viewer sees lavish circus framing and a cat with her eyes closed, assuming this is a poster for a magical circus cat. After the final line at the bottom of the sheet, the eye catches a deliberately subdued detail in the shadow under the table — a bag with a laptop and badge — completely reversing the meaning of the formal portrait.

* **Coordinating decisions and distinguishing connections:**
* *Constraint of the adopted rule:* choosing one image requires the reality of the café, the imagined circus and all the dialogue to coexist on a single sheet through compositional zoning.

* *Conventional association:* turn-of-the-twentieth-century poster style prepares the viewer for fairgrounds, trained animals and theatrical deception.

* *Free authorial interpretation (and my additions):* the historical Art Nouveau manner does not dictate the period of the action — by authorial choice the events occur today, and the decorative circus arch around the characters is not a real wall but a visualisation of their shared circus fantasies. *(My addition: the contrast between the vintage lettering of the waiter's imposed “poster” and the dry monospaced computer type used for the cat's lines.)*

### Brief presentation of the concept

1. **Title and main idea:** “Poster for an Attraction That Never Happened” — a deceptive single-panel illustration in which the lavish circus-poster form reflects the waiter's gaze, while the cat's dry answer at the bottom destroys the illusion.

2. **Key axes chosen and decisions:**
* **Form of visual storytelling** — a single illustration read vertically.

* **Whose world dictates the style** — the imagined circus sets the overall decorative frame, which the story disproves at the end.

* **The cat's anatomical and behavioural anthropomorphism** — an ordinary feline body, with a human posture at the table and an unruffled customer's manners.

* **Indirect informational clues** — an inconspicuous professional accessory in shadow, read only after the punchline.

* **The appearance of lettering and text** — a clash between the waiter's fairground lettering and the cat's console font.

3. **How the decisions become visible:** The waiter's gaze is embodied in a golden shaft of light and billowing coffee steam that forms a circus tent with acrobats; the cat's sober position is visible in her strict posture, the laptop backpack hidden in shadow, and the terminal font of her words.

### Brief description of the concept

* **Scene and action:** A round marble table in a Parisian or urban bistro. The waiter freezes in a theatrical half-bow, pressing an empty tray to his chest with one hand and pointing upwards with the other, like a compère in the ring. The cat sits on a high bentwood chair before an empty tablecloth, dreamily narrowing her eyes.

* **The characters' appearance, poses and expressions:** A large, sleek black cat, unclothed but sitting with a perfectly straight back, front paws folded on the table edge; her face expresses quiet gastronomic bliss. The waiter is a lanky man in a white apron and waistcoat, with wide-open eyes and a mouth slightly open in an enthusiastic smile.

* **Composition and significant details:** A centred poster composition. The upper third is a decorative arch of intertwined ribbons, lollipops, clouds of pink candy floss and silhouettes of jugglers, growing out of the characters' imagination. At the centre are the brightly lit cat and the waiter leaning towards her. At the very bottom, in deep shadow beneath the crossbar of her chair, stands a partly open modern grey backpack, with the corner of a metal laptop bearing a code-bracket sticker `{ }` and a magnetic access pass on a lanyard peeping out *(my addition)*.

* **Visual manner, colour and light:** Art Nouveau lithographic poster graphics with clear black contours and grainy paper texture. The palette combines warm ochre, carmine red and cream with the cat's deep black silhouette. Light directed from above picks her out like a stage spotlight.

* **Dialogue and placement:**
* Along the upper semicircle of the ornamental arch, a shaped vintage ribbon carries the waiter's words: *“You're a talking cat?! My uncle runs a circus; he'd take you on, on any terms!”*

* At the bottom of the sheet, on a strict rectangular box over the ornament, the cat's answer is set in a clear monospaced font (as in a code editor): *“A circus, you say? That's the place with the acrobats, the jugglers, the lollipops, the candy floss? Sounds tempting. But what would they need a programmer for?”*

## Concept 2. “A Dopamine Glitch”

### Overall concept

* **The characters' relationship and the meaning of the ending:** The cat is a city dweller tired of routine, for whom coffee with cream and a pastry are a legitimate way to recover. The waiter rudely interrupts her break, first with exuberant amazement, then with an intrusive career offer. When she closes her eyes and lists the circus's attractions, her mind genuinely drifts for a second into a world of simple childhood pleasures, but professional conditioning immediately brings her back to earth. Whether the uncle-director really exists or the waiter is merely a fantasist remains open, as does the cat's history before entering the café.

* **Visual language:** Four-panel Japanese manga (**yonkoma**), combining **slice-of-life seinen manga** with an abrupt shift to **gag manga (the codes of *chibi* and *kawaii*)** in the third panel. This language makes an abrupt change of emotional and graphic registers the joke's driving force: from everyday dryness to an exaggeratedly sensual dream and back to stony *deadpan*. Recognisable features: black-and-white ink drawing with screentones, dynamic shock lines behind the waiter, sudden sparkles and stars in the dream panel, sharply simplified expressions.

* **Format and the dynamics of viewer knowledge:** A vertical four-panel strip. Throughout the first three panels, neither waiter nor reader suspects the cat's profession — not a single technical object is shown. The professional claim appears only in the last speech balloon of the fourth panel, instantly explaining both the cat's businesslike tone in panel two and her weary pragmatism.

* **Coordinating decisions and distinguishing connections:**
* *Constraint of the adopted rule:* the yonkoma canon strictly sets four panels and a structure with a turn in panel three and resolution in panel four; the story stops exactly on the line, without showing the waiter's subsequent reaction.

* *Conventional association:* a shift to *kawaii* with shining stars is usually associated with the heroine's naïveté and childishness.

* *Free authorial interpretation (and my additions):* despite the third panel's cute graphics, the cat is not a naïve child but a pragmatic adult professional whose lack of sugar and rest has caused a brief “dopamine rush” at the mention of candy floss *(my addition)*.

### Brief presentation of the concept

1. **Title and main idea:** “A Dopamine Glitch” — a four-panel comic strip (yonkoma), built on the contrast of graphic modes between harsh everyday life, a flash of sugary dreaming and an unruffled professional ending.

2. **Key axes chosen and decisions:**
* **Number of panels and division into dramatic units** — exactly four panels: 1) the waiter's shock and the demand for the order; 2) the business offer; 3) the cat's dreamy pause; 4) the final punchline.

* **Consistency of the characters' graphic rules** — a deliberate change in the cat's own graphic register between panels (from realistic movement to grotesque *chibi* in the dream and back to dry contours).

* **Revealing the profession** — no advance visual clues at all; the profession is revealed exclusively through the word at the end.

* **The pause of the cat's imagination** — given its own third panel, without the real café background.

3. **How the decisions become visible:** The black-and-white screentones of the first two panels give way to a dazzling floral background in the third; in the fourth, a hard horizontal border cuts it off and leaves a completely blank white background behind the cat's impassive face.

### Brief description of the concept

* **Panel contents and sequence (a vertical yonkoma strip):**
* **Panel 1 (Exposition and shock):** A medium shot across the counter of a modern minimalist café. The waiter recoils in horror and delight, radial black *speed lines* around his head, mouth wide open. Opposite him, an ordinary tabby sits on a stool with tired, half-closed eyelids, looking dryly at the menu.

* *Text in the waiter's balloon (jagged outline):* “You're a cat? A talking cat?! Incredible…”

* *Text in the cat's balloon (smooth oval outline):* “Yes, I'm a cat. Are you bringing my order or not?”

* **Panel 2 (Business initiative):** A slightly side-on angle; the waiter abruptly leans over the table towards the cat, gesturing conspiratorially, enthusiasm shining in his eyes. The cat raises one ear slightly, listening sceptically.

* *Text in the waiter's balloon:* “Hold on a second… Listen, could you use a job? It's just, my uncle runs a circus, and he'd gladly take you on, on any terms…”

* **Panel 3 (The pause of imagination):** The real café interior disappears completely. A close-up of the cat drawn in a soft, round-cheeked *kawaii* manner: she blissfully closes her eyes and presses her fluffy paws to her cheeks, while enormous spiral lollipops, clouds of candy floss and tiny juggler silhouettes float around her in a shining space of stars and bubble screentones.

* *Text in a cloud-shaped balloon:* “A circus, you say? That's the place with the acrobats, the jugglers, the lollipops, the candy floss? Sounds tempting.”

* **Panel 4 (Punchline):** An abrupt return to hard, detailed drawing. An extreme frontal close-up of the cat's face against a completely blank white background without a single sparkle. Her eyes are open, her gaze utterly flat, heavy and unblinking (*deadpan*); at the edge of the panel, only part of the waiter's stupefied face is visible, with a sweat drop on his temple *(my addition)*.

* *Text in a strict rectangular balloon at the bottom:* “But what would they need a programmer for?”

* **Visual manner, colour and light:** Black-and-white ink drawing with traditional screentones and a single colour accent: the lollipops and candy floss in panel three are pale pink and mint, intensifying the dream's interruption when the strip returns to black and white in panel four *(my addition)*.

## Concept 3. “A Break near the Computing Centre”

### Overall concept

* **The characters' relationship and the meaning of the ending:** The action is transferred to a Soviet science town in the late 1960s — an era of romantic enthusiasm for cybernetics and large computers. The cat is a senior research fellow and computing-centre programmer, for whom her own intelligence has long been part of ordinary working life. The cafeteria waiter is a typical enterprising service-sector operator who judges customers by appearance and seeks easy profit through a relative in variety entertainment or the circus. The cat's final line carries an intellectual's calm irony at her interlocutor's narrow outlook. How she acquired her qualifications remains open (the result of a research-institute experiment or a natural part of this fantastical world).

* **Visual language:** **Soviet satirical magazine cartooning** (in the tradition of *Krokodil* and Evgeny Migunov's illustrations for the Strugatsky brothers' stories), combined with **presentation through two juxtaposed views**. This language turns the joke into social satire about work, service culture and the mismatch between an outward assessment of a person (or cat) and their real qualifications. Recognisable features: lively, rapid pen-and-ink lines, free watercolour washes spilling outside contours, distinctive caricatured movement, and precise everyday signs of 1960s Soviet modernism.

* **Format and the dynamics of viewer knowledge:** A diptych of two horizontal panels on one magazine spread, using dramatic irony. Unlike the second concept, the viewer learns the cat's profession **before** the first line: in the first panel, a worn briefcase with punched paper tape and a slide rule protruding lies on the table beside her. Yet the waiter, blinded by the mere fact of a talking animal, completely overlooks these clues; the viewer therefore awaits less a revelation than exactly how the cat will put him in his place.

* **Coordinating decisions and distinguishing connections:**
* *Constraint of the adopted rule:* revealing the profession early changes the punchline's mechanics — the final sentence does not surprise the reader with a new fact, but gives the pleasure of striking the waiter's blind spot precisely.

* *Conventional association:* Soviet cartoon graphics create expectations of bureaucratic satire and moralising.

* *Free authorial interpretation (and my additions):* the reference to 1960s Soviet research institutes does not make the cat a malicious Bulgakovian trickster; by authorial choice, she is a good-natured, slightly tired intellectual who is not even angry with the waiter, and her order (coffee with cream and a basket-shaped pastry) is already on the table, cooling because of the conversation *(my addition)*.

### Brief presentation of the concept

1. **Title and main idea:** “A Break near the Computing Centre” — a satirical diptych in the aesthetic of 1960s Soviet magazine graphics, where the viewer sees the accessories of a computer programmer from the outset, and the comedy rests on the enterprising waiter's shortsightedness.

2. **Key axes chosen and decisions:**
* **Historical and technological period** — the late 1960s, the era of vacuum-tube and transistor computers, punched cards and paper tape.

* **Visual signs of programming and revealing the profession** — an early visual clue for the viewer (paper tape and a flowchart on the table), unnoticed by the waiter.

* **The fate of the order** — coffee and pastry have arrived by the start of the second panel, but the waiter prevents the cat from starting to eat.

* **Narrative voice and text placement** — typeset captions beneath the drawings, traditional in Soviet humour magazines, rather than comic balloons.

3. **How the decisions become visible:** From the outset, a roll of punched paper tape and a sheet with a diamond-shaped algorithm flowchart lie on the cafeteria table, while a modernist building marked “Computing Centre” is visible through the café's glass frontage in the background *(my addition)*.

### Brief description of the concept

* **Panel contents and sequence (a horizontal diptych — two drawings side by side):**
* **Drawing 1 (Left — Order and astonishment):** The interior of a bright 1960s cafeteria, with slender-legged tables and plastic chairs; through the panoramic window is an institute façade with a concrete punched-card relief. A plump orange cat sits at a table, round horn-rimmed glasses on the tip of her nose; one paw rests on a leather briefcase from which hangs a long perforated paper tape, alongside the protruding edge of *Science and Life* magazine *(my addition)*. The waiter, in a white jacket with a notepad, stands rooted to the spot, having dropped his pencil, which hangs in the air (indicated by motion strokes).

* **Drawing 2 (Right — Circus temptation and rebuff):** On the table before the cat now stand a faceted glass in a metal holder, a cream jug and a pastry with a cream rose on a saucer. Forgetting etiquette, the waiter crouches right beside the table and enthusiastically draws a circus dome in the air with his hands; above his head, a tent marked “Circus”, a juggler with clubs and a rooster-shaped lollipop float in a light transparent watercolour outline. The cat has dreamily closed her eyes, smiling faintly at the corners of her whiskers, but her claw businesslike presses a sheet with a geometric algorithm flowchart to the table.

* **Visual manner, colour and light:** A quick, ironic pen-and-black-ink drawing with a light transparent watercolour wash in warm tones (ochre, turquoise, burnt sienna) against yellowish magazine paper. Diffuse daylight pours through the cafeteria's broad window.

* **Dialogue and placement:** Text is arranged in the classic magazine manner — typeset dialogue beneath each drawing.

* *Caption beneath Drawing 1:*
“— You're a cat? A talking cat?! Incredible…
— Yes, I'm a cat. Are you bringing my order or not?”

* *Caption beneath Drawing 2:*
“— Hold on a second… Listen, could you use a job? It's just, my uncle runs a circus, and he'd gladly take you on, on any terms…
— A circus, you say? — the cat closes her eyes dreamily. — That's the place with the acrobats, the jugglers, the lollipops, the candy floss? Sounds tempting. But what would they need a programmer for?”
