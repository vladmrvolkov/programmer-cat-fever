## 1. “A Poster with a Correction”

**Idea.** The entire picture is a circus poster the waiter has already printed in his mind: he looks at a customer and sees the star attraction. Inside the poster, the cat simply waits for coffee. Her final sentence does not fit the poster's language and is pasted over it on a strip of paper, like a “Performance cancelled” notice. The line argues with the waiter, the picture itself, and the viewer who, a moment ago, was also inspecting the cat as an attraction.

**What remains open:**
- whether she really is a programmer: the poster knows nothing about it;
- whether she understood that she was being invited as an exhibit: the line could be innocent or devastatingly polite;
- whether her order will arrive.

**What the viewer knows.** Before the strip: a talking cat is a sensation. Afterwards: the label was someone else's, and the poster expresses a desire rather than a fact.

| Axis | Decision | How it becomes visible |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Form of visual storytelling; Number of panels | A single image | A vertical sheet with a border, headline and “star” in the centre |
| Whose world dictates the style | The circus, hence the waiter's gaze; the author argues with it | All the poster's lettering is assembled from the waiter's words |
| Shared graphic rules for the characters | The final line follows different rules | A strip of office paper with monospaced type over the lithograph |
| The truth of the profession | A statement only | No laptop or other accessories; only the typeface speaks of a profession |
| The intention of the final line | Unspecified | Eyes closed, muzzle turned towards the tray rather than the audience |
| Light-and-shadow dramaturgy | Light puts the cat on display even before the invitation | A spotlight cone; the stool is indistinguishable from a circus pedestal |
| The fate of the order | The order is suspended | The waiter balances the tray on his fingertips as a trick |
| The period evoked by the manner | A manner from around 1900, present-day action | Printer paper and sticky tape on an “old” sheet |

**Coordination.**
- Accepted constraints: one sheet, so there is no “after”. The headline, central star and enthusiastic tone come from the poster; its period is not transferred to the action.
- Associations: spotlight and pedestal read as an “act”, a slanted pasted notice as a cancellation, monospaced type with a cursor as the computer world. They do not award the cat a diploma.
- Free choice: the poster conveys the waiter's gaze; he is an enthusiastic barker, not a villain.
- My additions: the pasted strip, the stool-pedestal, the tray-trick, the name “My Uncle's Circus”, and the cat as a spectator in a medallion.

**Scene.**
- Action: the cat sits on a high bar stool at a round table. The waiter presents her to the public with a sweeping gesture, holding a tray of coffee and pastry above his head with his other hand. The cat's table is empty.
- Cat: an ordinary grey tabby with a white chest, unclothed. She sits upright, tail around her paws, eyes dreamily closed, nose reaching towards the cream.
- Waiter: young and lanky, in a white jacket and black bow tie. His mouth is wide open in a barker's cry; he looks straight at the viewer.
- Composition: vertical 2:3. The top third is occupied by an arched headline. The cat is central; the circle of light on the floor resembles a ring. The waiter is on the left, partly cropped by the border.
- Corner medallions: acrobats, jugglers, lollipops, candy floss. In the last, a tiny cat with a cloud of candy floss sits in the audience.
- Manner: flat fills with stippled shading, black outlines, cream paper, red, yellow, dark blue, slight misregistration, fold marks. The strip is cold white, sharp and shadowed: the only “real” object on the sheet.

**Text.**
- Top ribbon: “INCREDIBLE!”
- Headline: “A TALKING CAT”
- Starburst beside the waiter: “ON ANY TERMS!”
- Bottom banner: “MY UNCLE'S CIRCUS”
- Ribbon from the cat's muzzle, in small type: “A circus, you say? Sounds tempting.”
- A strip slanting across the lower edge, over the line of dates: “But what would they need a programmer for?█”

The list in the cat's line is conveyed by medallions rather than words.

## 2. “The Candy-Floss Flowchart”

**Idea.** The waiter is kind and wants to help. The cat takes his words as an ordinary job vacancy and considers it conscientiously. The circus dream begins as childlike delight, but habit takes over: her mind automatically divides the circus into blocks and arrows. The completed chart has no place for her, hence the question. The final line is a calculation's result, not a barb. He does not see her as a specialist; she does not see that she herself is the act.

**What remains open:**
- what the circus is really like: only her dream is shown;
- whether she will understand that she was invited because she can speak;
- whether she would accept if there were a place for her.

**What the viewer knows.** They suspect how her mind works before the word “programmer”, as the dream acquires a diagram. The line names what has already been seen. Afterwards the waiter freezes for a second time, now for another reason.

| Axis | Decision | How it becomes visible |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The medium's format | A vertical scrolling strip | The ending is below the screen edge and cannot be seen in advance |
| Attitude to the offer | Serious, as towards a vacancy | The cat closes her eyes and “calculates” rather than joking it away |
| Marking subjective images | The dream is separated from the café | Framed café panels; a full-width unframed dream; entry through a closing eyelid, exit through an open eye |
| Revealing the profession | First through drawing, then through words | The gouache circus gradually turns into a flowchart |
| The truth of the profession | A fact of the story | Her way of thinking is the evidence; no laptop is shown |
| The pause before the punchline | The longest silent stretch | The transformation proceeds without a single word |
| The waiter's motive | Goodwill | He crouches to speak at her eye level; his shapes are equally rounded |
| The pause after the punchline | A brief mirrored reaction | The final panel repeats the posture of his first amazement |

**Coordination.**
- Accepted constraints: the strip shows one screen at a time. The rule “frame = reality, no frame = dream” is never broken, so the whole circus belongs to the cat and does not establish its existence.
- Associations: rectangles, arrows and monospaced labels read as engineering thought; warm gouache makes both characters appealing.
- Free choice: the waiter's kindness, the cat's seriousness, and the truth of her profession.
- My additions: an order in pictograms, a dream turning into a chart, an empty dashed block, a wisp of candy floss over her head, a mirrored ending.

**Appearance and manner.**
- Cat: orange, round, without clothes or belongings. The chair is too low, so she rises and places her front paws on the table edge.
- Waiter: round-faced, in an apron, sleeves rolled up.
- Café: matte gouache, soft edges, ochre, terracotta, milky white, daylight from the window.
- Dream: the same colours at candy-like saturation — raspberry, lemon, turquoise.
- Diagram: even, thin grey-blue lines, right angles, lowercase monospaced labels. The gouache beneath fades into a white sheet.

**Screens.**
1. Café. The cat orders; her balloon contains two pictograms rather than words: a cup with a swirl of cream and a pastry.
2. The waiter freezes, his pencil falling. “You're a cat? A talking cat?! Incredible…”
3. The cat taps the menu with her paw. “Yes, I'm a cat. Are you bringing my order or not?”
4. The waiter crouches beside the table, palm to his chest. “Hold on a second… Listen, could you use a job? It's just, my uncle runs a circus, and he'd gladly take you on, on any terms…”
5. Close-up: the cat's eyelids meet. “A circus, you say?” The panel's lower border dissolves.
6. A long unframed section: a tent dome, acrobats, jugglers, lollipops, a pink cloud of candy floss. The cat herself sits in the front row with an enormous candy floss. Words float alongside the pictures without balloons: “That's the place with the acrobats, the jugglers, the lollipops, the candy floss?” At the very bottom: “Sounds tempting.”
7. No words. Ball trajectories become dashed arcs with arrows; trapeze ropes become connecting lines. Figures shrink into blocks labelled `acrobats`, `jugglers`, `lollipops`, `candy_floss`. At the bottom the chart is complete; an empty dashed block with a blinking cursor remains to one side, with no arrow leading to it.
8. A framed panel again. Eye open, posture as on screen 3, a pink wisp of candy floss above her head. “But what would they need a programmer for?”
9. A small panel: the waiter in the same pose as on screen 2, his pencil falling again. No text.

## 3. “Alibi”

**Idea.** Late evening, an almost empty café. The cat hears the waiter's offer as a threat: a talking animal in a circus means a cage. She stalls by pretending to daydream, spots a clue — a glowing laptop on the next table — and produces an excuse. The final line is a trickster's move: she declares herself a specialist with no place in a circus.

**What remains open:**
- whether there was a threat: we see the waiter through her eyes; perhaps he is simply an enthusiastic nephew;
- whether she is lying: the laptop might be her own or merely the first thing she spotted;
- what will happen to the order.

**What the viewer knows.** Before the line, they notice the tense tail, the absence of any picture of a circus, and the screen's glint in her pupil one panel before the word. Afterwards, the dreaminess is reread as an act, and under even lighting the waiter turns out to be an ordinary bewildered young man.

| Axis | Decision | How it becomes visible |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Genre code | Comic Gothic: unease without established villainy | Dense hatching, elongated figures, rain outside |
| Narrative point of view | The cat's gaze, neutral at the end | Panels 2–4 are shot from her height, panel 5 from human height |
| The waiter's motive | Not established | In panel 2 his face is half-shadowed; in panel 5 it is lit and guileless |
| The intention of the final line | Evasion | An ear turned back, a hooked tail-tip, an eye open a slit |
| The truth of the profession | Doubtful | The screen's yellow rectangle is reflected in her pupil before the word “programmer” |
| The pause of the cat's imagination | Fantasy not shown | During the list of sweets, only her muzzle and a shadow on the wall are visible |
| The cat's anatomical anthropomorphism | None | An ordinary black cat without clothes or possessions |
| Light-and-shadow dramaturgy | Light suggests a cage | The window-frame shadow falls like bars across the table and cat |

**Coordination.**
- Accepted constraints: the lines are verbatim, so the cat's fear can only be shown. Camera height attaches the “threat” to her viewpoint. Gothic contributes lighting and hatching but not the supernatural: the cat is not a shapeshifter.
- Associations: the barred shadow reads as a cage, a low angle as a threat, a black cat as cunning. They put the viewer on guard without making the waiter a villain.
- Free choice: her fear, her calculation, the hour before closing.
- My additions: the neighbouring laptop and its glint in her pupil, the pencil line slipping across the notepad, the change of camera height at the end. The stylistic reference is darkly comic pen drawing in the manner of Edward Gorey (a reference absent from the map).

**Manner.** Black ink, cross-hatching, grey wash. The only colour is warm yellow: the cat's eyes and the laptop screen. It connects them before the plot does.

**Panels.** A page: a wide panel at the top, two tall ones in the middle, a narrow slit and the final panel below.
1. Wide. Rain on the glass; some chairs are already upside down on tables. The cat sits on a chair, head and shoulders above the tabletop. A tall, thin waiter in a long black apron has frozen. The notepad reads “coffee w. cream, pastry”, then the pencil line slips downwards. In the background, an open laptop and cup stand on a neighbouring table with no one beside it. Waiter: “You're a cat? A talking cat?! Incredible…” Cat: “Yes, I'm a cat. Are you bringing my order or not?”
2. Tall, from below. The waiter leans over the table and fills the panel, smiling; his shadow covers the cat, the window casts bars on the tabletop. “Hold on a second… Listen, could you use a job? It's just, my uncle runs a circus, and he'd gladly take you on, on any terms…”
3. Tall, close-up. Eyes closed, whiskers forward, the cat acts blissful. One ear points backwards; the tip of her tail hooks over the chair edge. The waiter's shadow on the wall is twice his height. “A circus, you say? That's the place with the acrobats, the jugglers, the lollipops, the candy floss?”
4. A narrow horizontal slit. One eye is partly open, a tiny bright rectangle in its pupil. “Sounds tempting.”
5. Medium shot from neutral height. The cat sits upright and looks the waiter in the face. He has straightened and stepped back; the barred shadow now lies on the floor between them, the notepad hangs from his hand. The laptop glows in the background. “But what would they need a programmer for?”
