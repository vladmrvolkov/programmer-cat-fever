I propose three concepts differing in visual style and in **the joke's mechanism, character relationships, and what viewers understand from the final line**.

1. **“Coffee First”** — an ironic magazine cartoon. The waiter sees an incredible creature; the cat sees ordinary bad service.
2. **“Almost a Dream Job”** — four-panel manga. Viewers know the cat's profession beforehand and watch a sincere circus dream collide with a practical question.
3. **“An Unprecedented Performer!”** — a vintage-circus-poster illustration. The image itself turns the cat into a spectacle; her final line contests that role.

The map has no alphanumeric axis codes, so their **exact names** serve as identifiers below.

## **1. “Coffee First”**

Single illustration · Magazine cartoon · Restrained absurdity

### **Concept**

The cat came for coffee with cream and a pastry. She speaks freely, orders independently and sees no need to explain her existence.

The waiter is stunned. He regards speech as a unique stage talent and offers work at his uncle's circus. His intention is friendly, but he takes no interest in who his customer is beyond her unusual appearance.

The cat neither protests nor mocks him. She simply does not understand why a circus needs someone in her profession. **The final line unexpectedly brings the conversation from miracles back to ordinary professional competence.**

The cat bears common sense. Her question expresses businesslike puzzlement rather than categorical refusal.

The reasons she can speak, her actual qualifications, a suitable circus vacancy and the order's eventual fate remain open.

### **Key map axes**

| Axis identifier | Decision and visible expression |
| --- | --- |
| **The main subject of comedy** | Misjudging ability from appearance. The waiter enthusiastically examines the cat, missing her practical interest in food. |
| **The bearer of common sense** | The cat. Her calm pose contrasts with his excessive expression. |
| **The truth of the profession** | Her statement alone. No computers, badges or other confirmation before the ending. |
| **The visual role of the order** | Coffee and pastry are already on the tray, but he has forgotten to serve them. The central object-based joke. |
| **Revealing the profession** | Exclusively in the final line at the bottom of the image. |

### **Specific scene**

A small urban café. An ordinary unclothed tabby sits on a high chair by the window. She has placed a folded cushion under herself for stability. Forepaws rest neatly on the table; her tail hangs over the seat edge.

A tall, thin waiter in white shirt, dark waistcoat and apron stands nearby. Mouth slightly open, eyebrows raised, he gestures excitedly with one hand and holds coffee and pastry on a tray with the other.

The tray is almost under her nose, but he is absorbed in his offer.

She looks at him, then the pastry. Her face is calm, eyes slightly narrowed. There is no haughtiness, rather mild fatigue at the protracted conversation.

## **2. “Almost a Dream Job”**

Four panels · Yonkoma and gag manga · Warm comedy

### **Concept**

The cat genuinely works as a programmer. She entered with a small laptop, finished a task and ordered something sweet.

The waiter meets a talking cat for the first time. He sincerely offers to introduce her to his circus-director uncle, assuming such an unusual performer would be a real find.

Unexpectedly, she becomes absorbed in the offer. Fame does not attract her: the circus as a wonderful place of acrobats, jugglers and especially cotton candy does.

For a moment she genuinely imagines herself part of it.

Then a simple thought arises: **she is a programmer, after all. What task are they hiring her for?**

The ending neither exposes deception nor expresses sarcasm. It is a sincere question from someone thinking professionally, after a second of childlike delight.

The circus's real needs, her subsequent decision, and how well her idea of circus life matches reality remain open.

### **Key map axes**

| Axis identifier | Decision and visible expression |
| --- | --- |
| **Revealing the profession** | Viewers see her work environment in panel one, before verbal disclosure. |
| **The distribution of knowledge within the scene** | Viewers know about programming; the waiter does not. Screen faces cat and viewer, not him. |
| **Attitude to the offer** | Sincere brief interest. Closed eyes, ears and relaxed pose convey joy. |
| **Marking subjective images** | Pastels, soft edges and decorative stars distinguish the imagined circus. |
| **The pause before the punchline** | Panel three is the dreamiest and richest; four abruptly restores calm everyday geometry. |
| **Number of panels** | Exactly four, without another image of a reaction after the final line. |

### **Appearance and visual manner**

A small ginger shorthair cat with large expressive eyes and a compact face. Her body remains feline, but forepaws can perform precise keyboard actions. A loose dark-green cardigan leaves ears and tail visible.

The waiter is young and awkward, with long arms, a neat apron and a highly mobile face.

## **3. “An Unprecedented Performer!”**

Single illustration · Circus poster and collage · Social satire

### **Concept**

Here the waiter sees both a miracle and a chance to impress his circus-director uncle. He already imagines being the man who discovered a unique attraction.

His offer is not necessarily malicious. Yet he primarily sees a spectacle. To her, speech is neither a profession nor a service.

She understands quite well what they want. Briefly interested in the circus itself, she then deliberately asks a professional question.

**“But what would they need a programmer for?” means “You are offering me work without even asking what I can do.”**

A tactful resistance to someone else's definition of her role, rather than a direct refusal.

Whether the circus could offer suitable work, whether he understands his mistake and how the conversation ends remain unknown.

### **Key map axes**

| Axis identifier | Decision and visible expression |
| --- | --- |
| **The waiter's motive** | Finding an unusual performer and earning his uncle's approval. Delight on his face, an almost advertising-presentation gesture. |
| **Whose world dictates the style** | Initially the image fully obeys circus language, placing viewers within his conception. |
| **Pictorial symbolism** | A decorative poster circle encloses her as an announced exhibit, but paw and work belongings cross its boundary. |
| **The intention of the final line** | A conscious test of professional meaning rather than simple puzzlement. |
| **Editing the actual and the imagined** | The vintage poster metaphorises his perspective; it is neither an actual poster nor evidence the circus exists. |
| **The typography of the punchline** | Austere plain type sharply contrasts with decorative circus lettering. |

### **Specific scene**

A large vertical illustration resembles an old circus poster, its lower portion seemingly torn open to reveal the actual café.

The upper two thirds are an imagined circus world. At the centre, a black cat with a white chest sits under stage lights. Red-and-gold curtain, stars, garlands and a round old-poster frame behind her.

The waiter poses as a compère nearby: one hand pointing to her, the other presenting her to the audience. His work apron remains visible under the stylised formal costume, identifying the same waiter rather than a real circus host.

The cat does not participate. She sits on an ordinary chair at a small table, laptop bag nearby and untouched coffee before her. Eyebrow slightly raised, she looks at someone who has missed the obvious question.

One paw crosses the decorative border. This is the main visual action: **a living being literally does not fit the frame of an imposed role**.

### **Composition, colour, light and text**

A vertical poster, roughly 2:3. The eye moves from enormous advertising headline through the circus image of the cat to the everyday space below.

The upper part is bright: vermilion, gold, ochre, black. Theatrical spotlights turn her into an object of universal attention.

The lower portion is almost monochrome, in soft café daylight. There she exists outside the advertising fantasy.

Different manners do not mean the characters literally inhabit different worlds: two ways of seeing the same being collide.

**Image text:**

Top: a decorative author-added headline, not dialogue from the joke:

# **TALKING CAT**

His speech balloon on the right partly overlaps the advertisement:

In the calm lower portion, her final balloon:

> But what would they need a programmer for?

Clear geometric type without flourishes. It need not be larger than other words: surrounding circus typography already supplies emphasis.

**Order of discovery:** viewers first see a promised spectacle, then the job offer, and finally her professional question at the very bottom. This reveals the poster as another person's projection, rather than her essence.

**Status of decisions.** Graphic rules and associations with spectacle come from the vintage poster, while action remains in a contemporary café. His enterprise, wish to impress his uncle and her consciously critical position are authorial interpretation. Neither circus costume nor poster literally exists within the scene.

## **Comparing the three concepts**

| | “Coffee First” | “Almost a Dream Job” | “An Unprecedented Performer!” |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Main effect** | Unexpected profession reveal | Sincere dream clashes with professional logic | Criticism of turning an individual into an attraction |
| **Cat** | Practical, unflappable | Enthusiastic, curious | Calm, dignified |
| **Waiter** | Sincerely surprised | Friendly and enthusiastic | Enterprising, inclined to judge appearances |
| **Viewer knowledge** | Profession revealed at end | Profession known from start | Profession revealed at end, role conflict visible earlier |
| **Final line** | Businesslike puzzlement | Sincere practical question | Polite objection |
| **Artistic centre** | Tray with forgotten order | Circus dream and return to reality | Contrast of poster and living cat |

All three preserve the joke's central contradiction: the waiter primarily sees an unusual animal, while she defines herself through profession. Its meaning changes, however.

**The first** best preserves the classic structure and final surprise. **The second** adds emotion and makes the punchline a pleasant resolution to an already understood misunderstanding. **The third** is most independent as an artistic statement: viewers first occupy the waiter's position, seeing an “attraction”, then recognise that perspective's limitations through the cat.

These concepts can develop independently: none requires transferring every other map decision into the image.
