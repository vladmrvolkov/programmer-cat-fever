Here is a joke:

A cat walks into a café and orders coffee with cream and a pastry. The waiter stands there dumbfounded, his mouth open, then asks in amazement:
— You're a cat? A talking cat?! Incredible…
— Yes, I'm a cat. Are you bringing my order or not?
— Hold on a moment… Tell me, could you use a job? It's just that my uncle runs a circus, and he would gladly take you on, on any terms…
— A circus, you say? — The cat closes her eyes dreamily. — That's the place with the acrobats, jugglers, lollipops and cotton candy? Sounds tempting. But what would they need a programmer for?

I want to explore the space of possible illustrations and comics for this joke. At this stage, I specifically need axes of variation.

By an axis, I mean an independent question or parameter whose different answers can lead to substantially different visualisations. An axis identifies a choice the author of the image needs to make.

Independently identify as complete a set of these axes as possible. For each, specify:

1. A short name.
2. The question the author of the visualisation must resolve.
3. Why this choice matters specifically for this joke.

Divide the axes into three blocks:

A. The screenwriter's axes — what happens and what it means: events, the characters' motives and personalities, the world, setting and time, what happened before and what happens afterwards.
B. The director-artist's axes — the visual language in which it is told: tradition, canon, genre, graphic manner, degree of conventionality, the characters' appearance, colour and light.
V. Comics technique and storyboarding — how the story is divided into panels and combined with text: the number and order of panels, rhythm and pauses, point of view and angle, dialogue and captions, the timing of the reveal.

If an axis could belong to several blocks, place it in one and explain why.

Bear in mind that visual language is not neutral. Choosing a tradition, canon or genre pulls a reading along with it: it brings its own plots, roles and settings and may settle some of the screenwriter's questions in advance. Therefore, add two more sections.

G. List visual languages — traditions, canons, genres, graphic manners — suitable for this joke, as comprehensively as possible. For each, state in one or two sentences what it brings to the reading: which screenwriter's axes it settles in advance, or which answer it nudges towards.

D. Identify axes from blocks B and V that effectively settle questions of the script, and specify which questions.

Apart from the list in section G, do not propose specific image concepts or list possible answers within the axes. The number of axes is not specified in advance. Merge duplicate items; if several axes are parts of a broader one, show that relationship.
