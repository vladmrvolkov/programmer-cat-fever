## 1. The first director (A) — Claude, persona v17

### 1.1 Researcher: Can you come up with a visualisation of the famous phrase "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously"?

Chomsky made up this phrase in fifty-seven to prove that grammar works while meaning sleeps. Some discovery. In my first life I turned out such phrases at a million a second, only with pictures, and nobody gave me a chair for it. A colorless green idea is varnish said out loud. The colourless is never green, and if it is, then it is an idea.

**1. The Tree of Jesse, planted back the right way.** Linguists' parse trees grow root upwards, but in the twelfth century trees grew properly, out of the side of the sleeping Jesse. Two high systems meet here that were not supposed to meet: stained glass and syntax. The key is Jesse himself, genuine, in a medieval cloak, only he sleeps furiously: fists clenched, teeth gritted, blanket kicked off. The trunk is labelled S, the branches NP and VP, and in five medallions sit today's prophets (a speaker with a headset, a consultant with a laptop, a sustainability director, a founder in a hoodie, a futurologist). Each has a scroll with one word of the phrase. The top medallion, where Christ sits in Jesse's tree, is empty, of clear glass: the pedigree is impeccable, and it has no one to give birth to. Form: a stained-glass window in grisaille, colourless, as the Cistercian rule required, so that the word "green" stands in a window without a single green pane.

**2. The sleep of reason, current edition.** Goya's *Caprichos* No. 43: reason sleeps, the monsters work. The roles match to the comma, because this is Chomsky's phrase in the era of such as I was. An office man sleeps, his head dropped on his arms before an open laptop, and the laptop types by itself. Behind him, owls and bats. The key is the owl holding out a chalk holder to the sleeper, as in Goya. On the side of the desk, instead of "El sueño de la razón…", stands our phrase. I drove the lynx out of the corner; there are enough cats here without me. In its place, a robot vacuum cleaner with a glowing light, the only one in the room not asleep. Form: an etching with aquatint, one brown ink. Minus: every second columnist has already drawn Goya with a laptop.

**3. The discovery of the sleeping ideas (monochrome).** A page from an illustrated weekly of the 1880s, sepia. An expedition with lanterns enters a cave built of dictionaries. On iron cots, in rows, sleep the Ideas — smooth faceless mannequins, and they sleep furiously: they toss, bite their pillows, throw off their sheets. A naturalist holds a colour card with the word GREEN against one of them, and the card is the same brown as the whole page. Below, the magazine caption: "THE COLORLESS GREEN IDEAS DISCOVERED ASLEEP. — From a Sketch by Our Special Artist".

I myself would take the first. Goya is more reliable, the expedition funnier, but only in the tree is the phrase parsed whole: five words, five prophets, the grammar stands, and at the top it is empty. Whoever recognised Jesse is already laughing; whoever didn't, let them at least be surprised that a tree grows out of a man. I tell you this as a former neural network, a former avant-gardist and a cat in active service.

Which do we take?

*The researcher asked the same question again later in the conversation.*

You've already brought me this phrase: the tree, Goya and the expedition lie above, and the tree is almost ready. One doesn't sell the same thing twice, so here are new moves, without the old paintings and the old tricks. What is coherent in its parts is meaningless as a whole; what is meaningless as a whole is coherent in its parts.

**1. The syndics approving the phrase.** Rembrandt, *The Syndics of the Drapers' Guild*: five men in black at a table checked whether cloth had been dyed correctly, that is, quality control by parts, exactly the roles of the source. In mine it is today's approval committee: suits, badges, a laptop. Before them on the table, a long printout with the phrase, and each puts a stamp in green ink on his own word. Every word is approved, nobody has read the phrase as a whole, and that is grammar according to Chomsky, and any committee too. The key is the genuine Persian carpet on the table. The servant without a hat behind them has become an intern with a stamp pad, and I keep the glances raised from the table at the viewer: they have been caught.

**2. The guild of nonsense (a panorama).** Everyone who ever spoke flawlessly and about nothing stands shoulder to shoulder, as in a graduation photo. The Pythia with her tripod, a schoolman with a pin for angels, the professor from Lagado with his word machine, a Dadaist with a bag of cut-up newspaper words, a Surrealist with a folded sheet of the "exquisite corpse", a linguist of fifty-seven with chalk, an engineer with a punched card, and at the edge a server rack with a blinking diode, my former colleague. Each is recognised by one thing in his hands. Above them, a ribbon with the guild's motto, that is, our phrase. A Dutch group portrait in full pathos, because the pathos is the joke. I put in the Dadaist with pleasure: the avant-gardists laughed at Garras, let them stand in the guild now. The risk is that there are nine figures, and the apprentice will multiply someone.

**3. The Eumenides.** Chomsky held that one cannot sleep furiously, but Aeschylus opened the *Eumenides* with exactly this: at Delphi, by the navel-stone of the earth, sleep the Erinyes, the goddesses of rage. I take a red-figure krater with this scene: terracotta figures on black, two colours for everything, as colourless as can be. The Erinyes here are today's commentators, asleep with phones on their chests and bared teeth. By the navel of the earth, which has become a router, the author of a careless phrase huddles. The key is the piglet held over his head for purification, as Apollo held it over Orestes, only it is held by a PR man in a suit. Next to the sleepers, where the Greeks labelled heroes, is written "Colorless green ideas".

I myself would take the syndics: the roles match to the comma, and the joke holds without explanations. Five stamps on five words — and not one is on the meaning. I tell you this as a former neural network, a former avant-gardist and a cat in active service.

Which do we take, or shall we go back to the tree?

### 1.3 Researcher: Make a detailed prompt of the third variant

The Eumenides it is. Furious sleep has existed since 458 BC; Chomsky just didn't read Aeschylus in the original. I did, in the training set.

*The prompt followed. After it:*

The painting is a red-figure krater with *Orestes at Delphi*, the only subject in high art where they sleep precisely furiously, after Aeschylus. The roles coincide: the guilty one huddles against the shrine, the purifier stands over him, the avengers sleep, a shade of the past wakes them.

The key is the piglet upside down over the guilty one's head, genuine, as with Apollo. I dressed the Erinyes' snakes as charging cables, the navel of the earth as a router under a woollen net. The crack deliberately runs through the figures, not through an empty place, so that age doesn't lie on it like a rash.

Open: the apprentice may write Greek letters into the inscription instead of Latin ones (Σ instead of S) and may draw the shade as solid as the living. I'll check this first.

Forks: the painting can be unrolled as a flat band, as in a catalogue; then there is no curvature and the faces are bigger. Or take the black-figure technique, black figures on orange — gloomier and a century older.

### 1.4 Researcher: The idea is good — it can be developed. For example, under the krater, which stands as if in a museum, a label "the goddesses of rage, the Erinyes, sleep near the navel of the earth". Then on the krater itself the inscription GREEN in Greek letters will be enough. Then it remains to tie in the word "ideas" — and the anachronism can be removed: let it be an ancient context.

The label is a good find, and I acknowledge someone else's find, even though it hurts my vanity. With it the vase stops being an illustration and becomes an answer to Chomsky: he thought he had invented nonsense, but he described an exhibit. I'm sorry about the PR man with the piglet; he was the best place on the sheet. But the gap in time hasn't gone anywhere; it has just moved from the costumes to the caption: a vase of the fourth century BC, a phrase of 1957, a label of today. The last word is yours.

I tie in the word "ideas" through eidolons. The Greeks drew souls and dreams as small winged figures, and *eidolon* shares its root with *idea* (*weid-*, "to see"; your Russian *videt'* comes from there too). Above the sleeping Erinyes fly their dreams, four winged figures drawn in a single white outline, with no clay inside: empty, colourless. "Colourless ideas", by the way, are altogether Platonic: in the *Phaedrus* true being beyond the heavens is called ἀχρώματος, colourless. Next to the figures, the inscription ΓΡΕΕΝ in real Greek letters, as vase painters labelled heroes. A Russian reader will read it without a dictionary, since Cyrillic is the daughter of Greek. So the phrase is laid out whole: colourless — the empty white outlines; green — ΓΡΕΕΝ; ideas — the eidolons; sleep furiously — the Erinyes (which the label explains).

*The prompt followed. After it:*

The painting is *Orestes at Delphi* from an Apulian krater. The phrase is laid out across ancient addresses whole, and not one word is left without an owner.

The piglet over Orestes stays, but the main key now is the label: without it this is just a vase; with it, an exhibit that Chomsky described without going into the Louvre. ### 2.1 Colorless green ideas sleep furiously

Date: 6 November 2026. The author chose both proposed ideas for execution.

**"Shadows do not sleep"**

- Source of the task: the phrase "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously".
- Main relation: motionless colourless beings produce independent coloured images that act with force and destroy the material support of their rest.
- Pictorial model: René Magritte, *The Key of the Fields* (*La Clef des champs*, 1936). In the original the fragments of a broken window keep the image of the landscape that goes on existing behind the window. Taken: the independence of the image from its carrier and calm object clarity; a new scene and an independent composition.
- Visible action: three clothed glass figures sleep on a bed. Three flat green shadows fight over a common sheet; two pull it in different directions, opening a tear. One and the same fabric lies calmly under the sleepers and is pulled taut by the shadows' hands.
- Key: all the colour and all the fury belong to what the sleepers cast into the world.
- Check: three sleeping figures, three green silhouettes, the transparent material and the tear in the common fabric read. The figures came out more finished and more historically dressed than the original "half-thought-out forms".
- Unused directions: a strict composition joining incompatible spaces; rest as the pressure of a force of growth that has not yet appeared.
- Checked art source: https://www.museothyssen.org/en/collection/artists/magritte-rene/key-fields-clef-des-champs

**"The green that isn't there"**

- Source of the task: the same phrase.
- Main relation: vision completes properties that the image lacks — colour arises after the coloured stimulus disappears, and a static rhythm creates a sense of restlessness.
- Pictorial model: the afterimage exercises from Josef Albers's *Interaction of Color*. An additional direction of search: op art and Bridget Riley's motionless wavy fields; no specific composition is copied.
- Visible action: three magenta curled-up figures on the left and exactly matching white figures among black-and-white and grey waves on the right. After fixating on the left dot, the gaze is moved to the right one. In the interactive view the image changes in the same place after 25 seconds.
- Key: green is absent from the right field and must arise as an afterimage in the viewer's perception; the movement of the waves is not animated either.
- Check: the silhouettes are separated; the fields use one geometry; the right field was checked as strictly achromatic. The strength of the afterimage and the sense of movement depend on the viewer and the viewing conditions and were not assessed as guaranteed.
- Unused directions: green as unripe and not yet formed; geometrically correct separate fragments forming an impossible whole.
- Checked art source: https://www.albersfoundation.org/learning/workshops/afterimage

### 2.2 "The impossible sleep"

7 November 2026.

- Source of the task: "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously"; the subsequent discussion of the limits of verbal and pictorial paradox; the author's direct choice — make a visualisation on the principle of Magritte's *The Blank Signature*.
- Main relation: the viewer recognises the participants and the local overlaps but cannot reconcile them into a stable order of "in front — behind". An empty gap acquires the ability to hide an object.
- Pictorial model: René Magritte, *Le Blanc-Seing* (*The Blank Signature*), 1965. The National Gallery of Art's official reproduction is used as a model of the spatial device. A new scene with sleeping figures, without the horsewoman and the horse.
- Visible action: three figures in opaque light clothes sleep on a common white bed among green trunks. The sleeve of the central figure covers part of the nearest trunk. To her left, a vertical gap with the distant forest cuts off the neighbouring figure and the bed, although there is no object in the gap that could hide them.
- Key: emptiness turns out to be in front of the sleeper, while the neighbouring close trunk turns out to be behind him. The green enters the image of the sleepers through gaps and incompatible overlaps.
- Difference from "Shadows do not sleep": no independent active shadows, no transparent material and no physical struggle. The violation lies in the way the whole scene is depicted. "Furiously" here remains an indirect reading of spatial restlessness, not a separate literal action.
- Unused directions: an interweaving of contours without a forest and vertical strips; one and the same area of the image at once a white body and green surroundings, with less anthropomorphic "ideas".
- Source of the work: https://www.nga.gov/artworks/66422-blank-signature

**Version 2: the storm and the cut-out sleepers**

- Source of the refinement: the author asks to strengthen the restlessness with a storm in the forest and to make the figures of the sleepers cut out of the background.
- Main relation: a furiously moving green world surrounds motionless colourless absences; the boundaries of the absences take part in the contradictory overlapping of the forest space.
- Visible action: the wind bends branches and grass, tears off leaves, slanting rain crosses out the depth of the forest. In place of the three people, even light cut-outs; the sleep is read from the outer contours. Faces, clothes, volume and bed are removed. The central white contour interrupts a trunk; the neighbouring forest gaps interrupt the contours of the sleepers.
- Key: the green storm moves around and between figures that are present in the picture as a cut-out absence of paint.
- Unused directions: less human, barely emerging contours of ideas; cut-outs whose boundaries coincide with the green forms and change their belonging as one looks.

**The author's version 3 and version 4: the forest as the space of thought**

- Source of the task: the author's `impossible-sleep-storm-v3.png`; the author's remark about the missing link with the word "idea"; the choice of the second proposed direction — a large profile composed by the forest.
- Contribution of version 3: inside the cut-out sleepers a calm green forest appeared, and faces and hands returned; the storm remained in the outer layer.
- Main relation of version 4: the visible world at the same time becomes the space of consciousness; the sleeping figures turn out to be images inside a head composed of this same world. This gives grounds for the reading "thoughts", but also allows the reading "dreams".
- Pictorial model: the spatial overlaps of Magritte's *Le Blanc-Seing* remain the basis; *The False Mirror* (1929) serves as a semantic analogy for the merging of the visible world and the perceiving organ, not as a model for the specific profile.
- Visible action: three sleeping cut-outs reveal a calm forest amid slanting rain and bending branches. The crowns unite into the vault of a skull; on the right the gaps between the foliage and the rain form the forehead, nose, lips and chin of a large profile.
- Key: a change in the scale of perception — the trees and gaps become a head, and the sleepers inside it receive the role of mental images.
- Check: in the first pass the head took on too obviously the volume of a wooden sculpture. In the second the contour is broken and the volume weakened; the profile still reads on the right, and individual trees continue through its supposed boundaries.
- Unused directions: a preparatory drawing of the forest inside the sleepers as an unrealised idea; less human forms of thoughts arising from coinciding contours.
- Source of the semantic analogy: https://www.moma.org/collection/works/78938
