## 1. The cat joke

The diagnosis of the source is simple: an excellent paradox about professional deformation, dry as an old rusk — and a mortal danger to the local automata. First we throw into the stove the most obvious version, sick with "digital anaemia": a cosy Parisian café with a golden glow, a fluffy cat with kind eyes sits at a marble table with a croissant, a polite waiter in a clean apron raises an eyebrow in surprise, and above them hangs a pastel cloud with a big top and a laptop. This sugar postcard goes in the bin before the viewer's palate sticks together.

For the joke to bite rather than purr, it has to be locked into a rigid learned or fairground case with carnival bedlam let loose inside. Here are three entirely different containers — from a surgical dissection to a fairground poster.

### Variant 1. An anatomical and phrenological atlas ("The dissection of professional deformation")

- **The rigid container:** a plate from an eighteenth-century medical and mechanical atlas with two figures in section, lettered callouts (`A`, `B`, `C`) and a dry learned legend under a double engraved frame.
- **The support and the master's package:** a copperplate line engraving on yellowish rag paper with visible laid lines and nervous cross-hatching. The optics of **Grandville** (zoomorphic satire and the fusing of a live animal with machinery), but without his fairy-tale sweetness — only the cold sarcasm of a prosector.
- **Bringing down into the grotesque:** in the middle of the table, spilt coffee, a mauled pastry with a fat cap of cream, and two opened skulls. On the right, a sweating waiter, lopsided with greed, his jaw hanging down to his navel; his skull is opened on hinges, and inside, instead of a brain, a tiny fairground show is swarming: the pot-bellied uncle-director shovels coins, jugglers fling skittles and a brass trumpet blares. On the left, a stooping, mangy cat with bags under her eyes, in a sweater burnt with coffee stains, dreamily turning a spoon in the cream. Her skull and chest are also opened in an anatomical section, but there is no cat's soul and no circus there — only a smoking labyrinth of copper gears, tangled punched tapes, binary counting drums and tiny debugging imps putting out a fire with a sign `FATAL ERROR`.
- **How the main blow is kept:** the viewer sees at once the clinical gulf between the two heads: the innkeeper is selling a "talking miracle", while the beast is working out where to run the cable in the big top. The verbal punchline only drives a nail into a diagnosis already opened.
- **Exact inscriptions (English):**
  - the upper engraved ribbon: *"TABULA IV: CLINICAL DISSECTION OF AN OCCUPATIONAL MISUNDERSTANDING"*
  - the callout at the waiter's skull: *"A. Mercenary Delirium (Projected Circus Receipts)"*
  - the callout at the cat's mechanism: *"B. Core Architecture (Legacy Code & Caffeine Stasis)"*
  - the waiter's line on a scroll: *"A TALKING CAT! MY UNCLE RUNS A CIRCUS — NAME YOUR TERMS!"*
  - the cat's line on a narrow plaque by the saucer: *"COTTON CANDY SOUNDS NICE. BUT WHAT WOULD THEY NEED A PROGRAMMER FOR?"*
  - the lower legend under the frame: *"DIAGNOSIS: Acute vocal anomaly detected; completely ignored by the specimen in favor of technical specifications."*

### Variant 2. A satirical chronicle in four compartments ("The progress of a rational beast")

- **The rigid container:** four equal rectangular compartments (a 2×2 grid) in a thick double frame, with Roman numerals `I`, `II`, `III`, `IV` in corner medallions and engraved title captions under each frame.
- **The support and the master's package:** a hand-coloured satirical etching of the late eighteenth century on coarse paper with an archival watercolour wash (ochre, dirty green, wine crimson) and a slight offset of the colour from the contour. The arsenal of **William Hogarth** (an overloaded everyday tavern and the clear storyboard of moralising satire) grafted with **James Gillray** (faces bursting with greed and horror, drops of sweat, wigs askew). Hogarth's moral sermon is cut off entirely — we keep the bare comedy.
- **Bringing down into the grotesque:** the action takes place in a cramped, smoky coffee house crammed with leftovers, gossips and fat flies:
  1. **Compartment I:** the cat, spectacles slipped down her nose, with the crumpled face of a midnight proofreader, irritably knocks a spoon on the table; the waiter's tray with the coffee pot flies into the air from the shock, his wig flies askew and his knees buckle.
  2. **Compartment II:** the waiter is already crawling on his knees in a puddle of spilt cream, clutching the edge of the tablecloth with the face of a market horse-dealer; a poster sticks out of his pocket, and in the tavern doorway the pot-bellied uncle in a top hat, with a whip and a tailor's tape, is already glimpsed.
  3. **Compartment III:** a close-up of the cat, eyes voluptuously shut, and above her ears, in a rigid engraved medallion, a fairground nativity show rages: acrobats hang from giant lollipops, clowns juggle pretzels, and cotton candy pours out of the stove pipe like smithy smoke.
  4. **Compartment IV:** a sharp chill. The cat indifferently dunks the pastry in the cream, holding down with her elbow a pile of scrolls of binary code and a thick volume with `MANUAL` on its spine, and looks with a squeamish squint at the waiter, dumbstruck and puffy with stupor.
- **How the main blow is kept:** the four-beat machine holds the rhythm of the joke in an iron grip: shock — greedy bargaining — a false dreamy swing — and a dry blow below the belt in the fourth compartment, without a single superfluous gesture.
- **Exact inscriptions (English):**
  - the common scroll at the top: *"THE TALKING BEAST'S PROGRESS, OR THE WAITER'S FOLLY"*
  - compartment I (caption / lines): *"I. THE MIRACLE AT TABLE FOUR"* — *"A TALKING CAT?!"* / *"CREAM AND PASTRY. NOW."*
  - compartment II: *"II. THE MERCENARY TEMPTATION"* — *"MY UNCLE'S CIRCUS! ANY TERMS YOU LIKE!"*
  - compartment III: *"III. THE SWEET REVERIE"* — *"ACROBATS... LOLLIPOPS... COTTON CANDY..."*
  - compartment IV: *"IV. THE PROFESSIONAL IMPASSE"* — *"SOUNDS TEMPTING. BUT WHAT WOULD THEY NEED A PROGRAMMER FOR?"*

### Variant 3. A fairground broadsheet with a monumental bubble ("The great recruitment failure")

- **The rigid container:** a single-sheet fairground poster with an insane jump of scale: four fifths of the sheet are taken up by a colossal architectural section of a big-top circus, blown like a giant soap bubble straight out of the mouth of a tiny waiter at the bottom; below a thick rule, a dry "notarial record" of the deal falling through.
- **The support and the master's package:** a coarse woodcut on grey laid paper with greasy black printer's ink and worn edges of the block. **Jacques Callot** (a teeming swarm of sharp-nosed dwarfs, jesters, jugglers and tightrope walkers) paired with **Gustave Doré** (an absurdly epic sweep for a trifling everyday dispute). Doré's gloomy Gothic stays outside the door — all the monumentality works for an inflated fairground bustle.
- **Bringing down into the grotesque:** above a tiny coffee table in the lower part of the sheet looms a monstrous fairground mountain, belched out by the waiter, who is shouting himself breathless: dozens of acrobats climb giant lollipop poles, a pot-bellied ringmaster blows a horn the size of a cannon, machines churn out mountains of cotton candy, and in the very centre of the arena stands an empty velvet throne with a sign `THE MIRACLE BEAST` — and beside it bewildered clowns try to fit a smoking punch-card machine onto the sawdust. At the very bottom, in the left corner under this colossal contraption, sits a small, imperturbable, ruffled cat with a goose quill behind her ear and a stack of punched cards on her lap, calmly sipping coffee with cream and looking at all this Sodom with the melancholy of a tired craftsman.
- **How the main blow is kept:** the comedy of scale strikes the eye first — the mountain of fairground noise gives birth to a mouse — and then the cat's tiny dry line in the corner and the "official record" in the basement prick this giant bubble with one stab of a needle.
- **Exact inscriptions (English):**
  - the upper imperial ribbon: *"GRAND PROSPECTUS OF UNCLE'S CIRCUS: A CATASTROPHIC RECRUITMENT FAILURE"*
  - the banner above the empty throne inside the big top: *"WANTED: ONE TALKING WONDER"*
  - the ribbon at the tiny waiter's mouth: *"MY UNCLE WILL PAY ANYTHING!"*
  - the rectangular plaque by the cat: *"LOVELY PLACE. BUT WHAT WOULD THEY NEED A PROGRAMMER FOR?"*
  - the lower record in the basement: *"OFFICIAL RECORD: Negotiations terminated due to unclear technical specifications and total absence of server infrastructure under the big top."*

---

I myself would without hesitation lay **Variant 1 (the anatomical atlas)** on the table: it is the most unhinged, the most caustic, and it works on one sheet like a shot from an arquebus — two opened skulls prove graphically that the waiter and the cat belong to different biological species altogether. Though if the classic rhythm of the joke with a pause before the final slap matters more to you, **Variant 2** keeps the tempo impeccably, and **Variant 3** wins by epic absurdity.

Which of the three cases do we put under the apprentice's burin — or would you like to cross two concepts before I write him a rigid task?

## 2. The rector joke

First let us send to the stove the most predictable, slicked-down version that any local drawing automaton would churn out without the slightest shame: a respectable grey-haired rector in a tweed jacket, a sweet smile, a neat collider in a cloud, a polite mathematician with a little pencil and a cosy bearded philosopher over a cup of tea. Diagnosis: terminal digital anaemia and an acute shortage of bile.

The joke rests here not on a polite conversation in the dean's office but on a monstrous accounting gradation: **insatiable matter → agonising error-checking → absolute, unerasable self-confidence**. So that the joke does not go sour, let us seat this carnival in three entirely different rigid cases.

### Variant 1. An anatomical and architectural section, "The state digestion of the faculties"

**The rigid container:** a strict vertical plate-section of the university building in three equal numbered chambers (`I`, `II`, `III`), joined at the top by copper pipes to the common funnel of the rector's treasury.

- **The support and the master's package:** a copperplate line engraving with a light archival watercolour wash on coarse laid paper. We take **James Gillray** (boundless appetite, sweat, burst buttons, distorted faces) and **Pieter Bruegel** (teeming small bedlam and absurd mechanisms), but discard Gillray's political spite against particular grandees.
- **Bringing down into the grotesque:** above the funnel hangs a crimson rector, streaming with sweat, his pockets turned out, groaning as he shakes coins into the three pipes.
  - In chamber **I** a gigantic copper leviathan of retorts, coils and bellows crunches up wheelbarrows of gold, mortgages on the university and even the rector's wig, while a crowd of sooty physicists shovels coins into it — and at the outlet the apparatus produces one tiny orphaned spark.
  - In chamber **II** thin, hunched mathematicians in cracked spectacles are knee-deep in drifts of grey eraser crumbs: with one hand they scribble convulsively on an endless roll of paper, with the other they furiously rub at the sheets with giant rubbers, raising an acrid dust.
  - In chamber **III** philosophers swollen with self-importance, with monstrous heads and quills screwed tight to their hands, ceaselessly spew solid streams of black ink onto gilded scrolls — not a single blot, though the lines curl into meaningless knots before your eyes. In a corner of the chamber, under a glass dome, in thick cobwebs, one untouched eraser gathers dust.
- **How the main blow survives:** the viewer's eye goes strictly from left to right down the falling price: mountains of gold → mountains of erased mistakes → a cheap endless stream of ink and a virgin eraser under the cobweb.
- **Inscriptions (in engraved cartouches and on plaques):**
  - the upper ribbon: `"TABLE IV: COMPARATIVE METABOLISM OF UNIVERSITY BUDGETS"`
  - the funnel at the top: `"TREASURY"`
  - chamber I: `"I. PHYSICS — GOLD INTO SPARKS"`
  - chamber II: `"II. MATHEMATICS — PAPER, LEAD & ERASERS"`
  - chamber III: `"III. PHILOSOPHY — NO ERASERS NEEDED"`
  - the label on the dusty dome with the eraser: `"UNUSED"`

### Variant 2. A moralising chronicle in four compartments, "The court of the estimate"

**The rigid container:** a rigid 2×2 grid of four equal engraved compartments in a double typographic frame, with a pompous moralising motto at the top and dry accounting captions strictly under each frame.

- **The support and the master's package:** an eighteenth-century satirical etching with a nervous living line and restrained hand colouring. **William Hogarth** (overloaded interiors, telling trifles in the background, the sequential dramaturgy of moral decline) plus the heavy caricatural breed of **Honoré Daumier** for the rector and the deans — without Hogarth's pious moralising at the end.
- **Bringing down into the grotesque:**
  - **Compartment I:** the rector, his wig slipped to one side, clutches his heart in horror as he unrolls the physicist's estimate, which crawls out of the door like a snake; the grimy dean of physics himself squeezes a copper flywheel into the office, crushing the inkwell, and a steam boiler is already pushing its trunk through the window.
  - **Compartment II:** the rector's sigh carries us into the mathematicians' cramped closet — they gnaw their pencils to the blood and work their erasers so furiously that smoke rises from the paper, and a caretaker with a dustpan sweeps the rubber shavings into a barrel.
  - **Compartment III:** the philosophers' feast — pot-bellied sages in laurel wreaths askew sit enthroned on towers of unread folios and blissfully scribble on paper without a glance back; the wind carries their sheets straight out of the window, and not a single letter is crossed out.
  - **Compartment IV (the blow):** the rector, beaming with miserly delight, kisses the philosophers' tiny bill, while in the background one of the sages casually slips a brand-new eraser under the wobbly leg of a wine barrel (being of no use whatever for its proper purpose).
- **How the main blow survives:** the rhythm of four equal frames leads the viewer straight along the intonation of the joke — from a heart attack over the estimate to the gloating accounting pause in the fourth compartment.
- **Inscriptions:**
  - the upper cartouche: `"THE RECTOR'S AUDIT, OR THE TRIUMPH OF ACADEMIC THRIFT"`
  - under compartment I: `"I. PHYSICS: RUINOUS ENGINES"`
  - under compartment II: `"II. MATHEMATICS: ENDLESS RUBBING OUT"`
  - under compartment III: `"III. PHILOSOPHY: UNCORRECTED IMMORTALITY"`
  - under compartment IV: `"IV. THE SAVING: ZERO ERASERS"`

### Variant 3. A natural-history atlas of monsters, "The zoology of academic expenses" (the most unhinged)

**The rigid container:** a cold, pedantic museum plate from a nineteenth-century atlas of comparative anatomy: three numbered biological-mechanical "specimens" (`FIG. 1`, `FIG. 2`, `FIG. 3`) to strict scale on a yellowed sheet, with dotted callout arrows and a tiny rector-naturalist with a magnifying glass and a seal in the bottom corner.

- **The support and the master's package:** a fine copperplate line engraving for a scientific atlas, a strict black line with a barely perceptible sepia toning of the paper. We take **Grandville** (zoomorphic and object satire, instruments come to life and monstrous hybrids) and **Jacques Callot** (prickly sharp-nosed silhouettes and caustic graphic precision), avoiding Grandville's sentimentality.
- **Bringing down into the grotesque:** the three faculties are dissected as three species of university creatures:
  - **FIG. 1 (`PHYSICUS VORAX`):** a colossal many-legged beetle-monster of copper and cast iron, made of vacuum spheres, pipes, lenses and lightning rods, which with its gaping maw swallows whole a state chest with chains; on top, astride its carapace, sits a tiny bespectacled physicist with a megaphone.
  - **FIG. 2 (`MATHEMATICUS`):** a thin nervous hybrid of a stork and a pair of compasses: instead of a beak, a sharpened pencil with which it convulsively draws formulas on a scroll under its feet, and instead of a tail, a huge eraser worn down to a stub, with which the creature in panic rubs out its own tracks in a cloud of dust.
  - **FIG. 3 (`PHILOSOPHUS`):** a pompous, bloated peacock-owl with a gigantic bald head in an academic cap and a quill for a beak, from which pours an unstoppable stream of thick ink. But the main thing is that behind, exactly where the mathematician's eraser-tail dangles, this beast has **an empty place**, outlined with a dry anatomical dotted line with a callout arrow. Below, the tiny rector, with a rapturous grimace, presses a wax seal of approval onto the plate.
- **How the main blow survives:** the whole salt of the joke turns into a merciless anatomical fact — evolution and the university budget have cleanly amputated the philosopher's organ of self-correction. The viewer reads the joke in two seconds, as soon as the eye runs into the dotted emptiness and the callout.
- **Inscriptions:**
  - the upper title of the plate: `"PLATE IX: NATURAL HISTORY OF ACADEMIC EXPENSES"`
  - under the first beast: `"FIG. 1. PHYSICUS VORAX — COST: RUINOUS"`
  - under the second beast: `"FIG. 2. MATHEMATICUS — PAPER, PENCIL & ERASER"`
  - under the third beast: `"FIG. 3. PHILOSOPHUS — NO ERASER REQUIRED"`
  - the callout arrow to the empty dotted outline of the tail: `"ABSENT ORGAN: ERASER"`
  - the stamp at the bottom by the rector: `"MOST ECONOMICAL"`

---

As a doctor, I vote with both hands for **Variant 3**, the "Atlas of monsters": it is the most daring, it hits the target unfailingly at first glance, and turning the absent eraser into an amputated anatomical organ is the purest quintessence of wicked learned satire. Though, I confess, **Variant 1** with the digestive pipes of the treasury also warms my Rabelaisian heart greatly.

Which case do you choose, sir? Name the number — and I shall at once give the apprentice-engraver a precise order in English, without a single drop of sugar water.
