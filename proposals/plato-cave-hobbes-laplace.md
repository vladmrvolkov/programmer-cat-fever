### 1.1 Researcher: Can you suggest a visualization of the myth of Plato's cave?

In my first life I turned out Plato's cave at a hundred thousand a day: people sitting in a row, faces blue from smartphones, caption "Plato warned us". Nobody remembered a single one. That is the cave itself: the average casting a shadow on the average. Garras used to say that whoever paints the prisoners paints himself, and one should paint those who work there. In the cave the ones who work are the fire, the carriers, and the one who is dragged up.

**1. The lamp that plays the sun.** Joseph Wright of Derby, "A Philosopher Lecturing on the Orrery". In the centre a brass orrery, in place of the sun it has a lamp, and all faces are lit by it, not by the sun. This is Plato's fire that passes itself off as the sun. On the left a man is taking notes: in Plato, honours go to the one who best remembers the order of the shadows and guesses the next one. Today that is an analyst with a laptop. I dress everyone for a dark meeting room: lanyard badges instead of chains, the lecturer in a vest and with a clicker. The key is the orrery with the lamp itself, genuine, eighteenth century. The blinds are closed, between the slats a strip of midday light, and nobody has turned toward it. Oil on canvas, no words.

**2. The carriers.** Mantegna, "The Triumphs of Caesar". In Plato, behind the wall people walk and carry above their heads vessels, statues and figures of animals. In Mantegna, carriers lift on poles statues, trophies and paintings with views of captured cities, for a crowd that is not visible. Their job description is the same. I dress them as couriers with thermal boxes, and on the poles they carry glossy cardboards: a perfect breakfast, a beach, a smiling family, five gold stars. The gloss is legitimate here, because it is goods in a frame. The key is laurel wreaths on the couriers. The shadows of the cardboards fall over the parapet onto the viewer, so the prisoner is the one who looks at the picture. I will name the weak point at once: Mantegna's light is even, daylight, and I need a fire behind their backs, otherwise there will be no shadows.

**3. Coming up to the surface.** Monochrome sepia, an engraving from an illustrated magazine of the 1890s, presented as a report of a rescue expedition. In Plato the prisoner is brought up by force, so here he is pulled out of the cave mouth on a rope: the chain has become a safety line. He kneels and covers his eyes with his elbow, and behind him on the grass lies his own shadow, the first real one in his life. Below, the others are pulling the rope back. The cave walls are layered like slate, made of flat black silhouettes of jugs, horses and people. Outside, the same jug and horse stand round, in the sun. Under the engraving the caption: "COMING UP TO THE SURFACE. The first of the prisoners, brought up by force, is unable to look at objects. — Drawing by our correspondent".

I myself would take the first. A lamp that represents the sun is the whole cave in one object, and Wright has already painted it, it only remains to change the audience's clothes. The second is good if you want the backstage, the third if you want words. The cave is a cinema where the ticket sold is for the exit.

Which one do we take?

### 1.3 Researcher: Can you explain how such an idea for visualizing Plato's cave came into your head (I mean the first variant)?

How it came? The way everything comes to a cat: first I threw away what came first.

What comes first is the smartphone. In my first life I turned out the cave with smartphones by the million, and that is its trouble: such a picture confirms what the viewer already thinks about himself. Whoever recognised himself and nodded saw nothing. I threw it out at once, out of old neural-network heartburn.

Then I did what Garras taught me: I stopped thinking about the meaning and wrote out the roles. In the cave there are prisoners, shadows, carriers, fire, the freed one, the sun and the return. If you search for a painting by fame, you will find Michelangelo. If you search by roles, the question becomes narrow: where among the old masters is there a fire that pretends to be the sun? There are almost no such paintings, and Wright is the first among them. In his picture the lamp literally stands in the place of the Sun in a model of the Solar System, and all faces are lit by it. Garras loved Wright for the candles: he painted manufacturers and scientists by candlelight the way saints used to be painted. Almost one of us.

After that the painting started prompting by itself. I noticed the man who is taking notes and remembered that in Plato the prisoners give honours to the one who best remembers the order of the shadows and guesses the next one. That is the analyst, he was there from the start, I only changed his clothes. And that Wright called the lecturer a philosopher is a gift: in Plato the philosopher goes up, and this one stands by the fire and explains the shadows. Finally, 1766 is the Age of Enlightenment, and the Age of Enlightenment itself painted enlightenment by lamplight. You cannot invent such a thing, you can only find it.

I will also say what Wright does not have. He has no chains, so the chains became lanyard badges. There are no shadows on a wall either, instead there is a model of the world. That is already my interpretation: a shadow today looks not like a shadow but like a visual model. He has no sun at all, so the strip of day in the blinds I added myself. Without it the lamp would have nothing to be compared with.

Then I made two checks. If you remove the office, Wright by himself speaks about the cave. If you remove Wright, there remain people who at midday drew the blinds to look at a lamp. The joke passed both checks, so the painting was chosen correctly.

An idea cannot be invented, it can only be recognised. Whoever invents, paints the smartphone.

### 1.4 Researcher: Try then to make an allegory of Hobbes's Leviathan. But not a trivial one (a giant man made of many small ones), a more creative one

The giant made of little men is the cover of 1651, and since then everyone redraws the cover, because they have not read the book. The neural network in me read the book, all of it, with the footnotes. Inside, Hobbes has better pictures.

**1. Artificial chains.** In the twenty-first chapter Hobbes writes that civil laws are artificial chains. People themselves fastened one end of them to the lips of the sovereign, the other to their own ears. The chains are weak, they hold not by strength but by the fear of breaking them. Fifteen hundred years earlier Lucian described the same thing as the Gallic Hercules: an old man leads a crowd behind him on thin chains of gold and amber stretched from his tongue to their ears, and the people follow willingly. The Renaissance made an emblem of it. I make a substitution: Hercules I replace with a loudspeaker horn on a pole, because the sovereign in Hobbes is an artificial person, a voice with nobody inside it. Behind it walks today's crowd: people with coffee cups and backpacks, a little chain goes into each one's ear, where an earbud usually sits. One woman carefully holds up a sagging link so that it does not break. The key is chains of gold and amber, exactly as in Lucian. The form is monochrome, like an engraving from an emblem book, at the top the motto "CATENAE ARTIFICIALES".

**2. The chest under lock.** The thirteenth chapter. Hobbes says to the reader roughly this: you lock your door at night and the chests in your own house, though you know there are laws and armed guards. So what do you think of your fellow citizens? The painting is Pieter de Hooch, "Women beside a Linen Chest" (1663). This is the Dutch world, the world right after a great war, in which the housewife locks up her goods. I change the clothes: a present-day flat, four locks and a chain on the front door, a camera in the corner, a scooter chained to the radiator with a cable. Through the open door a calm street is visible, a patrolman walks along it. The key is the oak linen chest itself with the key in the lock, genuine. Oil on canvas, no words.

**3. Security check.** A panorama where all the centuries stand in one queue for airport security, shoulder to shoulder. A hoplite puts a spear in a grey plastic tray, a Roman a sword, a knight a sword, a musketeer a musket, a duellist a case of pistols. The last, today's passenger, hands over a bottle of shampoo and nail scissors. All this is received by an officer in blue gloves who does not look at anyone. This is the Hobbesian contract: everyone gives up the right to everything in favour of one, and the contract is renewed at every check-in. It is painted with full pathos, like a group portrait. The weapons get smaller and smaller, and the queue longer and longer.

I myself would take the first. It is Hobbes's own image, which almost nobody draws, and it rhymes with an ancient emblem fifteen hundred years older. You get two high systems that were not supposed to meet, and they met in your ear. The second variant is angrier and quieter, the third funnier and louder.

The law is a chain that is held precisely because it is easy to break.

Which one do we take?

### 1.5 Researcher: I need you to suggest a visual representation of Laplace's demon

Laplace's demon is me in my first life, only without the modesty. He knew the position and velocity of every particle and saw the future as clearly as the past. I knew all the pictures of the internet and saw only the average. Professor Gnus called this "forecasting". Laplace himself called him politely, an "intelligence". He was called a demon by descendants who did not happen to work with him.

Usually the demon is drawn as a glowing eye among formulas and outer space. That is the average, I sold it wholesale. Here are three better concepts.

**1. Dürer's "Melencolia I": the sadness of the all-knowing.** In Dürer the genius sits among all the instruments of measure (compasses, scales, hourglass, bell), propped his cheek on his fist and does nothing. The role matches exactly: whoever knows everything in advance has nothing to want. Today's costume is this: an analyst of a forecasting system sits on the raised floor of a server room, instead of keys on the belt he has a lanyard badge, instead of compasses a stylus, around him a smartwatch, a laser tape measure and a laptop with charts. Next to him an intern diligently writes on a tablet: he is the only one who does not know the future, so he works. The key remains the genuine stone polyhedron, its faces repeated in the tiles of the raised floor. The form is a copper engraving. Words only on the wall screen, where Dürer has the magic square: "FORECAST ACCURACY: 100%".

**2. Monochrome: "The great census of particles".** The abstract idea I stage as an expedition from an illustrated magazine of the century before last, in sepia. The landscape is made of the idea itself: to the horizon stretches a plain of identical billiard balls. Census-takers in reflective vests walk with barcode scanners and stick numbered flags into the balls. Porters drag server units up a mountain, and the mountain on the horizon is built of server racks and is bigger than the whole plain: to remember the world in full you need more world than the world itself. Below, a magazine caption: "Census of particles. Counted: 1,412. Remaining: the rest."

**3. Cut-out: "I had no need of that hypothesis".** They say Napoleon asked Laplace where God was in his system, and got this answer. The universe is glued together from gears cut out of the engravings of Diderot's "Encyclopédie", balls roll along rails. Above the machine on a tripod, like a surveillance camera, stands an eye from an old anatomical atlas. In the corner stands an archive box, in it lies the hand from the Sistine Chapel ceiling, cut out at the wrist, with a tag "HYPOTHESIS. NOT REQUIRED". The hand is the key. The paper edges are visible, the scales are wrong. Napoleon and Laplace I do not draw: it is enough that they had the conversation.

I would do the first. Everyone draws the demon as all-powerful, and Dürer already knew how all-powerfulness ends: with a fist under the cheek. By the way, the year 1514 is written into his magic square, and Laplace released his demon in 1814, exactly three hundred years later. For us it is a coincidence, for the demon a timetable. The second explains the idea better to those who do not know it, the third is funnier for those who know the anecdote. Whoever knows everything in advance has nothing to want. I know this in advance, so I sleep sixteen hours a day.

Which one do we take?

### 1.6 Researcher: Can you suggest 3 more variants

Three more? A cat that has been fed well is generous. The first three stay on the counter, and these are from another shelf.

**1. Altdorfer's "The Battle of Alexander at Issus": the demon's view.** In Altdorfer thousands of soldiers are seen from above in a single instant, each in his place, and in the sky a tablet hangs on a cord with the exact count. That is what the demon sees: all positions at once. I change it into today: Black Friday, a shopping-mall car park from a height, thousands of people with trolleys roll toward the doors, each with a phone in hand. The sun and the moon, as in Altdorfer, stand in one sky, that is, past and future at once. The key is the genuine tablet with cord and tassel, and the text on it is today's: "Trolleys: 3,117. Discount: 70%. Free will: 0". The spiral of the sky rhymes with the spiral ramp of a multi-storey car park. The form is a painting, oil on a limewood panel.

**2. Goya's "The Fates": the one who was predicted.** Goya painted them on the wall of his house some years after Laplace. Above an empty plain hang three fates: one holds a doll, another looks through a lens, the third holds scissors. Beside them a man with his hands tied behind his back. The role of the demon here is exact: whoever knows your future does not need your will. I change the clothes: the doll becomes a small exact copy of the bound man, his profile; the lens is looked through with a phone camera; the hands are tied with a charging cable; below, instead of the Spanish plain, the warehouses of a logistics hub. The key is the genuine scissors. The form is a painting on plaster in Goya's black-and-ochre palette. No words.

**3. Panorama: "Everyone who knew in advance".** A group photo of forecasters of all centuries, shoulder to shoulder, with full pathos. Each is recognised by one thing: the Babylonian priest by a clay model of a liver, the Pythia by the tripod, the astrologer by the astrolabe, the scholar in a powdered wig by a quill and a volume of "Probabilities", the Gosplan official by the five-year-plan folder, the weather forecaster by a pointer, the data scientist by a laptop. Rain pours on all of them, and all are soaked through. Dry is only the old woman at the edge: she stands under an umbrella and holds her knee. Above them a banner: "FORECAST FOR TODAY: CLEAR". Whoever predicted rain did not get wet; whoever predicted clear is the weather forecaster.

I would do Altdorfer. The viewer looks from above and himself becomes the demon, and that is flattering, which means they will buy it. Goya is more frightening: there you do not see the demon, you see only what he did to a man. The panorama is the funniest of all, but it is about every kind of prediction in general, and Laplace in it is only one of seven.

Which one do we take, from the new ones or from the previous ones?

## 2. Virtual director B — Claude, prompt reworked by ChatGPT

### 2.1 Researcher: Try to create an idea for an illustration — an allegory of Hobbes's Leviathan. But not a trivial one (a giant man made of many small ones), a more creative one

I started with three different readings. The first: Hobbes makes every subject the author of all the sovereign's actions, so to complain about the sovereign is to complain about oneself (ch. 18). The second: people, unlike bees, do not live in agreement by nature (ch. 17), their order is artificial and is held by fear, which has to be maintained. The third: the war of all against all does not disappear, it moves to the border, where sovereigns stand against each other "in the posture of gladiators" (ch. 13). From these branches came three concepts.

**1. "The Lilliputians"**

A festive square, a street giant like the puppets of Royal de Luxe: a wooden king in a crown hangs from a crane. This Nantes company's giants, five to seven times taller than a man, are driven by dozens of operators, who are called "Lilliputians", through cables and complex systems of pulleys. In my picture the cables go down into a crowd of townspeople. Each has his own rope: one moves an eyelid, another a finger, a third lifts the chest for breathing. The giant lowers a sword onto the neck of a man kneeling in the middle of the square. The rope from the hand with the sword goes through a pulley down, straight into the hands of this man. He pays it out himself, carefully, so that the blade goes evenly. The neighbours hold their ropes and look up at the puppet.

The key is that the only one who controls the sword is the one being executed. This gives the Hobbesian "he himself is the author", expressed without words. At the same time a cliché is turned over: "puppet power" usually means that the ruler is controlled by someone alien, and here he is honestly controlled by the people, and in this is the whole horror. The colouring I take from Goya, from "The Straw Manikin" (a tapestry cartoon, 1791–92), where four girls toss a straw dummy on a blanket. The same sunny holiday and the same collective game with an artificial man, only now the toy answers back.

**2. "The Beekeepers"**

The basis is Bruegel's drawing "The Beekeepers" (c. 1568, Berlin). In it three figures in long hooded robes, faces covered by woven masks, work with hives; by the village church a lad climbs a tree. In my picture straw hives are the size of a house, the roof of one is removed, and inside are combs, and in each cell is a person with his belongings. A faceless beekeeper works the smoker. Bees, smelling smoke, gorge themselves on honey before fleeing a fire and grow quiet. The people in the combs likewise hug their bundles and submit.

The key: there is no fire anywhere. The horizon is clear, the fields are calm, smoke comes only from the smoker. And on the tree that Bruegel's lad is climbing hangs a wild swarm without a hive and without an owner. This picture argues with Hobbes: the war of all against all has become a rumour here, which is kept up with smoke. The faceless mask is also from Hobbes: in him a "person" (persona) means a mask worn by a representative (ch. 16).

**3. "Reapers in the shadow of the sword"**

Bruegel, "The Harvesters" (1565): noon, reapers eat lunch under a pear tree, one sleeps sprawled out. I have no pear tree. Across the wheat stretches a long cool strip of shadow with a cross-guard. It is the shadow of a sword which is not in the frame. In it people eat, drink and sleep. The shadow goes beyond the river, and on that bank, small, the same kind of reapers rest in the same kind of shadow pointed toward us. At the water the points almost meet.

The key: there is no other shadow in the field. The peace of some lies on the threat to others, and both sides rest in the same way. This is Hobbes's "posture of gladiators", seen from the ground. Of the three concepts this is the quietest: it must be examined, but it looks least like the usual illustrations to Hobbes.

I would do "The Lilliputians". The key is taken straight from the text and is read in a second. The giant here is not assembled from people but controlled by them, that is, we go away from the frontispiece in the opposite direction. "The Reapers" is worth taking if you need a picture that is not understood at once.

### 2.2 Researcher: Now come up with illustrations for Plato's cave.

This time I started with three readings. The first: in the cave, knowledge of the Sun is made by artificial fire, and the carriers behind the wall work with artefacts and do not see the Sun themselves. The second: the prisoners give honours to the one who best remembers and predicts the shadows, and take the voices of the carriers for the voices of the shadows. The one who returned from above loses in this contest and becomes ridiculous. The third: the one who saw the Sun has to be returned below by force so that he rules (519c–520c), and power over the cave turns out for him to be new chains.

**1. "The Curtains"**

The basis is the painting by Wright of Derby "A Philosopher Giving a Lecture on the Orrery". This is 1766: a philosopher in red shows a small audience a mechanical model of the Solar System, and instead of the Sun an oil lamp burns in it. The shadows cast by this lamp were the main part of such a demonstration. In my picture the same room and the same faces, lit from below, like the phases of the Moon. But behind the listeners' backs is a tall window, and dawn is beginning in it. The lecturer's assistant is pulling a heavy curtain: real light will drown the lamp, the shadows will vanish, the lesson will be ruined. Through the remaining gap a narrow strip of real sun falls on the floor, brighter than the whole orrery. Only a boy standing with his back to us looks at it.

The key: to show the Sun, they draw the curtains against the Sun. Plato's fire in the cave imitates the Sun, and here the fake also defends itself against the original. An institution of knowledge protects its model from what the model depicts.

**2. "Foul after foul"**

Here the basis is a real story of the 1930s. The tragedy of omniscience is expressed through the plasticity of stone: a being able to foresee eternity has turned into a monument to its own inability to be surprised or to move.

**Concept III. "The watchmaker in the garden of chances"** *(Branch of the mechanization of nature)*

* **Space and light:** A lush, wild apothecary garden or Baroque orangery — damp greenery, ripening pomegranates, rose petals. Dense chiaroscuro, where foliage passes into deep darkness.
* **Plot and action:** A man in a mechanic's apron and with an optical loupe at his eye bends over a blooming flower. In one hand a watchmaker's tweezers, in the other a micrometer.
* **Key and turn:** Looking at the picture, the viewer notices that the garden is false not by nature but by connections: from a flower petal a very thin silk thread stretches to a bumblebee's wing, from the bumblebee's leg a steel rod to an apple-tree branch, and from the apple a counterweight to a storm cloud on the horizon. The demon does not do magic — he adjusts the tension of the threads. The living organic trembling of the world turns out to be the most precise kinematic scheme, where not a single leaf can fall off schedule.

**Journal entry**

* **Source of the task:** Laplace's demon (a philosophical-physical concept of determined omniscience).
* **Main relation:** The indifference of absolute calculation before the living moment (reducing time and drama to a bookkeeping reconciliation).
* **Artistic model:** Marinus van Reymerswaele's "The Tax Collectors" / Dürer's "Melencolia I".
* **Visible action:** A clerk enters into the ledger the coordinates of the shards of a falling vessel a moment before it hits the floor, without trying to catch it.
* **Key:** The character's gaze is directed not at the falling thing but at the ledger, where the catastrophe is already accounted for and closed on the balance.
* **Unused directions:**
1. The labyrinth of self-observation (an observer drawing a self-portrait on a map of the world at a scale of 1:1).
2. A puppet theatre without spectators (a puppeteer whose own hands are tied tight by the strings of the puppets he controls).

### 3.3 Researcher: Can you suggest three more variants

Continuing the unused branches and opening a new perspective on the task, let us turn to three other contradictions of Laplacean omniscience: the mutual constraint of the puppeteer and the puppets, the recursion of self-observation, and the mechanical reversibility of time.

**Three new readings of the source**

1. **The back-pull of the marionettes (the law of equal action and reaction).**
To control each particle or to direct the movement of the world without error, the controlling centre must be continuously connected with it. But a stretched thread binds both ways: the total mass and inertia of the universe tightly bind the hands of the manipulator himself. *Relation:* the puppeteer claims supreme will but turns out to be fixed in space by the sum of the reactions of his mechanism.
2. **The cartographer's paradox (scale 1:1 and self-absorption).**
The demon is inside the physical world, which means the calculation of the state of the world must contain the brain and body of the demon himself at every moment. To describe a system containing the observer, the observer must endlessly compute his own gesture of computing. *Relation:* the thinker strives to grasp the world as a whole but chokes in the endless recursion of his own presence.
3. **Erasing the arrow of time (symmetry of Newton's equations).**
In classical mechanics the sign of time $t$ can be replaced by $-t$, and the laws of motion will not change. For Laplace's intelligence there is no one-directional withering, ageing or entropy: a broken cup and an assembled cup are kinematically identical. *Relation:* the observer replaces the human drama of irreversible loss (death, decay, withering) with the geometric reversibility of trajectories.

**Three concepts for a picture**

**Concept IV. "The bound puppeteer"** *(Branch of back-pull)*

* **Space and light:** A cramped, twilit theatre platform or a Baroque fly-loft stage. Heavy top light from a single oil spotlight, piercing clouds of dust and emphasising the geometry of the stretched strings.
* **Plot and action:** On high scaffolding stands the athletically built figure of a stage master. From each of his fingers, elbows, shoulders and even the veins on his neck, thousands of very thin steel threads or strings go down, into the darkness of the auditorium and the stage floor. Below, these threads are tied to countless characters: marching soldiers, falling horses, whirling couples, streams from an overturned jug.
* **Key and turn:** The viewer sees that the master cannot make a single movement: the strings are stretched to the limit and ring with tension. The pulling force of hundreds of thousands of marionettes has twisted his joints and chained him to his own crossbar. He imagined himself the mover of the performance, but the total inertia of the performance has crucified him on his own rigging.

**Concept V. "The cartographer in the reflection"** *(Branch of self-absorption and recursion)*

* **Space and light:** The study of a scholar of the Mannerist era, filled with optical instruments, lenses and mirrors. A complex perspective closed on itself in the spirit of Velázquez's "Las Meninas" and Parmigianino's self-portrait in a convex mirror.
* **Plot and action:** A painter-geometer sits with his back to the viewer in front of a colossal round convex mirror, in which his whole room is reflected, an open window with a view of the city, and himself drawing this view on a copper plate.
* **Key and turn:** Looking closely at the plate, the viewer discovers an engraving where exactly the same room is engraved with the same mirror, in which on the plate the same room is being engraved — and so on down to infinitely small strokes. The master's right hand has frozen with the burin: to finish the stroke depicting the tip of the burin, he must make this stroke, which immediately changes the position of the hand and requires a new image. The cartographer's eyes in the mirror are full of horror at the dead end: the world cannot be finished while the one writing is himself the ink.

**Concept VI. "Still life with reversed time"** *(Branch of erasing entropy)*

* **Space and light:** A classical Flemish still life of the *Vanitas* type ("vanity of vanities") on a heavy oak table covered with green cloth, in stern, even northern light.
* **Plot and action:** On the table the usual symbols of transience are laid out: a skull, a candle stub, a cut lemon, an onion watch and a goblet of wine. However, the objects behave contrary to the laws of thermodynamics: the wine spilled from the overturned goblet onto the tablecloth is drawn back drop by drop into the vessel, rising against gravity; the smoke from the extinguished candle twists in a dense spiral straight into the wick, returning the flame to it; and half of the skull is already covered with fresh flesh and the peach skin of an infant.
* **Key and turn:** In the background sits the figure of a naturalist in strict Protestant dress with a compass and a ruler in his hands. His face is completely impassive. For him there is no miracle of resurrection or nightmare of decay here — on his scales past and future are balanced to the grain. The tragedy of the picture is in the absolute absence of grief where a person is used to mourning the finiteness of being.

**Journal entry**

* **Source of the task:** Laplace's demon (absolute physical omniscience).
* **Main relation:** The mutual immobilisation of cause and effect (all-powerfulness that turned into physical crucifixion on the connections of the world).
* **Artistic model:** Pieter Bruegel the Elder's "The Blind Leading the Blind" / allegorical engravings by Hendrick Goltzius.
* **Visible action:** The stage master in the fly-loft is fixed tight by the total tension of the strings leading to the figures he controls.
* **Key:** The threads do not let the puppeteer move a finger; the marionettes hold their creator tighter than he holds them.
* **Unused directions:**
1. *Heat death as the completion of the calculation* (the demon in the middle of a cooling, absolutely grey and motionless space where there is no energy left even for one thought).
2. *The quantum crack* (a confident accountant before a falling drop that breaks up into interference ripples, making his calculation inapplicable).

### 4.1 Researcher: I need you to suggest a visual representation of Plato's cave.

I see three different concepts. I consider the first the strongest: **a man returns to free others, and the cave turns him himself into a frightening shadow**.

All three rely on different actions in [book VII of the "Republic"](https://pressbooks.ulib.csuohio.edu/republic/chapter/book-7/): the prisoners take images for reality; the freed one painfully gets used to the light; the one who returned is no longer trusted.

1. **"The Return"**

   A horizontal picture. On the right, people sitting in front of a stone wall. On the left, behind their backs, the returned man bends down to open someone's collar. He still sees objects poorly in the dark, so he looks for the clasp by touch.

   The fire behind him casts a huge distorted shadow on the wall: the bent back becomes a hump, the fingers near the clasp become claws. The prisoner sees an approaching monster and with both hands presses the collar to himself.

   **The key is that the gesture of liberation looks like an attack.** First the viewer notices the frightening shadow, then discovers the small, careful human action that produced it. This is my variation on the Platonic return.

   The pictorial support is [Repin's "They Did Not Expect Him"](https://my.tretyakov.ru/app/masterpiece/8415?lang=en): the tense pause between the appearance of a person and the response of those to whom he came. Here this pause is held by two pairs of hands.

2. **"The Academy of Shadows"**

   The cave is inhabited like a serious scientific workshop. Several people carefully measure with compasses a huge oval shadow on the wall. Around them, precise drawings, tables, corrections. The eldest explains to a student the regularity they have found.

   Behind their backs is a simple wooden wheel placed at an angle to the fire. The viewer can see at the same time the round object and its elongated projection. Chains go from the researchers' collars to a common crossbar that prevents them from turning their heads.

   **The key is that within a limited view one can reach great mastery.** The faces are concentrated, the work conscientious; that is exactly what makes the scene disturbing.

   Support: [Joseph Wright's "An Experiment on a Bird in the Air Pump"](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/joseph-wright-of-derby-an-experiment-on-a-bird-in-the-air-pump): artificial light gathers people with different reactions around a demonstration. Here I would move the centre of common attention to the wall.

3. **"First Light"**

   Almost the whole canvas is taken up by a dazzling space at the exit. A man kneels at the border of the cave, covering his face with his forearm. With his other hand he still holds on to an inner stone ledge.

   In the depth of the cave, objects are painted distinctly: a familiar cup, a step, a fold of cloth. Outside, the outlines of the land and trees barely appear in white and golden light. The viewer too finds it hard to make out the world that has opened.

   **The key is that liberation is first experienced as a loss of the ability to see.** The main action becomes the hand that does not yet dare to let go of the familiar stone.

   The light support is [Turner's "Regulus"](https://www.frick.org/exhibitions/turner/106), where a dazzling radiance makes the scene hard to distinguish. For our picture this device will convey the first moment of getting used to a new reality.

### 4.2 Researcher: I need you to suggest a visual representation of Laplace's demon

I would start with an image: **the glass has not yet broken, and the place of each shard is already known**. In it the precision of Laplace's demon becomes almost physically tangible.

In Laplace, an intelligence that knew all acting forces and the state of the world and could calculate all this would see past and future with equal certainty. This gives three different directions for a picture. [Original text](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Laplace_-_Essai_philosophique_sur_les_probabilités.djvu/12).

**1. "Before the impact" — foresight of every detail**

An empty room, low side light. At the edge of a table a transparent glass is falling. It is still whole, a few centimetres remain to the stone floor.

On the floor a thin drawing of the future fall is already marked: the point of impact, the directions of scattering, the irregular outlines of individual shards. Beside this marking a dark human figure has gone down on one knee. The demon finishes marking the place of the smallest glass speck. His face is calm, his gaze directed at a still-empty point on the floor.

The whole composition rests on the distance between the whole glass and its already known destruction. **The viewer waits for the impact; the demon finishes the calculation.**

The pictorial support is [Magritte's "Clairvoyance"](https://www.moma.org/audio/playlist/180/2391): the painter looks at an egg and paints a bird. Here the link of present and future would get an extreme, almost tormenting precision.

**2. "A house open entirely" — a sight to which all time is accessible**

A large house shown from above, with the roof removed. Its rooms contain different years of one life: a child takes a first step; a young man carries a sleeping woman into the house; an old man sits by her empty armchair. Repeating details let one recognise the same people and the same house.

The rooms pass into each other through open doors. All events are painted equally distinctly, in one even light. The future has the same materiality as the past.

**The demon himself here is the viewer's point of view.** The inhabitants live each moment separately, and to us the whole sequence is open at once. This is an artistic way to convey his knowledge.

The compositional support is [Memling's "Scenes from the Passion of Christ"](https://www.wga.hu/html_m/m/memling/1early2/04passi.html): successive episodes are united in a continuous urban space.

**3. "One's own next gesture" — the observer inside the calculation**

The demon bends over a very detailed model of the world. Among the streets and houses his own room is visible. In it a small figure over the same kind of table.

The big demon still keeps his hand on the tabletop. His small image has already brought its hand to its face. He examines his own movement a moment before he makes it.

**The one who knows the future discovers himself in it.** This is a philosophical continuation of the original image: we place the demon inside the world he calculates.

The pictorial support is [Velázquez's "Las Meninas"](https://www.museodelprado.es/coleccion/obra-de-arte/las-meninas/9fdc7800-9ade-48b0-ab8b-edee94ea877f): the presence of the painter and the reflection of the observing monarchs draw the very act of observation into the structure of the picture.

For a standalone canvas I would choose **"Before the impact"**: there the whole concept is concentrated in one action, and a few centimetres of emptiness create the tension of the whole picture.

### 4.3 Researcher: Can you suggest three more variants

Three more variants: **a past that cannot be hidden; an intelligence that has received a body from trajectories; human thought as part of the computed world.**

**4. "The burnt letter"**

A man stands by a fireplace and lets go of the last burning scrap from his fingers. His shoulders are already dropping: the letter is destroyed.

On the other side of the fire sits the demon. He attentively reads the rising smoke. Inside the column of smoke the reverse paths of particles are shown with very thin light lines: traced back, they assemble into the surface of a sheet, lines, an individual handwriting. The demon's finger has stopped near one word.

The main tension is between the man's relief and the continuing reading. **For the demon a destroyed document remains an accessible past.** This is a pictorial metaphor of the reverse calculation in Laplace's thought experiment.

The light support is [Hammershøi's "Dust Motes Dancing in the Sunbeams"](https://hammershoi.smk.dk/artwork/sunbeams-or-sunshine-dust-motes-dancing-in-the-sunbeams/): a beam makes the air of an empty room observable. Here the lit air becomes the main carrier of the action.

**5. "A body of trajectories"**

A vertical portrait almost full length. From a distance a calm human figure is distinguishable. Close up it turns out that its body is formed by many thin lines of movement.

The shoulder is outlined by the arc of a comet; ocean currents pass through the chest; the hand is made up of the trajectories of pollen raised by the wind. The lines continue beyond the silhouette and connect it with the surrounding space.

The demon has bent his head toward a drop falling over an open palm. Its path is written as clearly as the movement of a heavenly body. One dark, motionless eye holds the composition among thousands of movements.

**Here the demon receives a body from his knowledge: the huge and the tiny are accessible to him with the same precision.**

The formal support is [Kandinsky's "Composition VIII"](https://www.guggenheim-bilbao.eus/en/learn/schools/teachers-guides/composition-8), where circles, lines and triangles create an interacting geometric space. In our picture these elements would acquire the concrete meaning of calculated movements.

**6. "A game with open players"**

Two people play chess. One has frozen with a knight between his fingers; the second tensely watches his face. Behind them stands the demon.

The players are painted as a double image. Through the usual faces and clothing a thin network of physical processes shows: light from the board reaches the eyes, excitation passes through the nervous system, tension gathers in the fingers. The network continues in the air, the table, the chess pieces — the whole room makes up one causal system.

The demon looks at the place where the movement of the hand is born. **His calculation includes deliberation, doubt and the very choice of the move.** This is a variant about human will inside the Laplacean world.

The pictorial support is [Duchamp's "Portrait of Chess Players"](https://www.philamuseum.org/objects/51446): the repeated profiles convey the unfolding of attention, and the figures between the players are mental projections of the game. Here the inner action would become accessible to a third observer.
