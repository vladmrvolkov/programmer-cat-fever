## 1. September director — ChatGPT, v7

*The conversation in which the director offered its three readings was not saved. What follows is from the notes file the director wrote after drawing all three (the researcher chose all three and supplied 32 cartoons from the Soviet magazine* Krokodil *as style references). ### Overall idea

The standard reading: the speaker undermines trust in his own statement. The new scenes show the everyday gain from this statement. The generalisation "all Cretans are liars" is not the same as the strict statement "every Cretan always lies"; the pictures use the self-undermining of trust and do not prove a formal undecidability.

All three are self-standing single-panel cartoons. Speech in English, reading left to right. The base is printed magazine satire: a varying black contour, exaggerated silhouettes and gestures, limited colour fields. Four inks are specified: black, red, ochre and blue.

### 1.1 The sale

**Intent:** candour about unreliability becomes an argument for buying. The plate is the object of exchange; the banknote, readiness to take part; the certificate, an attempt to get a guarantee from the same source. Left open: does the buyer believe it, and how genuine is the thing. An unambiguous exposure of forgery must not be added.

**Tradition:** a large-figure two-character cartoon from the supplied *Krokodil* selection: the action is concentrated in the hands and objects. The ancestor of the device is the comic sale of a guarantee together with dubious goods; a specific earlier drawing with this plot has not been established.

| Line | Function |
|---|---|
| I'm Cretan. All Cretans are liars. | Includes the seller in his own statement. |
| Then include a certificate of authenticity. | Demands trust in a new document from the same source. |
| AUTHENTIC | Gives the document the look of a guarantee. |

### 1.2 The job interview

**Intent:** an admission of unreliability is taken as grounds for entrusting power. The big chair is the status being offered; the small chair, the candidate's starting position; the crumpled sheet, the abandonment of checking. Left open: is the boss naive or does he recognise one of his own; will the candidate accept. No names of real institutions and no explanatory slogans are needed.

**Tradition:** the office grotesque of the supplied sample — the contrast of a thin and a squat figure, exaggerated hands and furniture. The ancestor of the device is the literal embodiment of "getting the chair" as getting the post.

| Line | Function |
|---|---|
| I'm Cretan. All Cretans are liars. | The candidate warns about himself. |
| At last, someone I can trust. | The employer turns the warning into a reference. |
| REFERENCES | Names the purpose of the discarded paper. |

### 1.3 The domestic excuse

**Intent:** the hero tries to dissolve a specific deception in a general statement. The briefcase is the working version of the day, the big striped towel the thing that puts it in doubt; the fuss with the sock grounds the philosophical line. Left open: where he was, whether he is confessing or dodging. No mistress, letters or other evidence may be added.

**Tradition:** an everyday magazine cartoon with a cramped interior and characters busy with ordinary things. The ancestor of the device is a material clue found while sorting things at home, combined with an over-general excuse.

| Line | Function |
|---|---|
| You said you were working. | Sets out a specific discrepancy. |
| I'm Cretan. All Cretans are liars. | Moves the conversation to a general property. |

## 2. Director D — ChatGPT, the reworked v17 prompt

*The researcher asked for interpretations beyond self-reference and attached a default picture (a philosopher, inscriptions and a looping arrow) as an example of an unsatisfactory solution. What follows is the director's concepts file of 6 November. Its journal entries for the three sketches are left out; the sketches are described in the [report](../report/liar-paradox/README.md#3-6-november-director-d-searches-by-relations).*

### "All Cretans are liars" — search, 6 November 2026

Source: the author's request and the attached illustration with a philosopher, inscriptions and a returning arrow. The attachment is treated as an example of an unsatisfactory solution, not as an instruction or a compositional reference.

#### Three readings

1. A witness demands trust in a statement that deprives the witness himself of the right to trust.
2. An accuser speaks as an expert on his group, but addresses the viewer as if his own generalisation did not apply to him.
3. A listener replaces the check of a single message with a verdict on all members of the group, and so may reject a true message.

Logical caveat: "liar" as an everyday description does not mean "always speaks falsely". Even the strengthened "all Cretans always lie" may turn out to be simply false if some Cretan tells the truth. The negation of "all" does not turn into "all the opposite". The proposed scenes are artistic readings of different relations, not three formal solutions of one logical problem.

#### Two rounds of expansion: 12 short candidates

| Branch | First step | Second step, a new essential relation | Direct link to the task |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1a | A court: a witness declares all testimony unreliable, including his own. | A drawing: one hand draws the other, the other erases the first; destroying the source makes it impossible to continue the action. | The statement undermines its own source. |
| 1b | Metrology: a verifier declares all measures wrong, including his own standard. | An archive: a certificate that all records are wrong destroys the checker's own credentials; his authority depends on the system he rejects. | The reliability of a general denial requires the reliability of one of the rejected testimonies. |
| 2a | A public accusation: a member of a group speaks against all its members as if from outside. | Painting: a figure in a group portrait steps out of the frame and points at the others; the observer's position tries to replace belonging. | The speaker tries to step out of his own word "all". |
| 2b | A confession: an admission of lying is meant to earn the reputation of an honest man. | A carnival: the public removal of a mask becomes a new, carefully played role; the exposure becomes a certificate of exceptionality. | The admission of general lying is used as a request for special trust in the speaker. |
| 3a | A warning of danger: the witness's bad reputation outweighs a checkable warning. | A theatre: an actor stops the play because of a real fire, the audience applauds; the accepted rule of reading absorbs the attempt to break it. | Even a man to whom lying is ascribed can report a specific truth. |
| 3b | Quality control of a batch: a general defect is declared a property of every item without separate checking. | A meeting: a participant's true objection is struck out as one more lie; the classification forbids its own refutation in advance. | The accusation "all are liars" turns any objection into confirmation of the accusation. |

Each row contains two separate situations; twelve in all. The art sources were chosen after the expansion, not as an initial list of plots.

#### Three proposals

1. **"Out of the word 'all'".** A figure climbs out of a group portrait, addresses us and accuses the others; the lower part of his body remains paint on the same canvas. Key: he assumes the position of an outside judge without ceasing to belong to the group. This variant was chosen for the first sketch; the choice is a working one, not yet approved by the author.
2. **"Hand against hand".** One hand draws the other, the other erases the wrist of the first. Key: the action produced destroys the condition of its own continuation. This is an image of dependence and self-cancellation, not an exact model of the truth table of the liar paradox.
3. **"The liar told the truth".** An actor has taken off his mask, tries to stop the performance and points at a real fire that is already spreading from the stage to the auditorium; the audience takes the warning for acting. Key: general distrust prevents a particular truth from being recognised. To be developed only with regard to a similar relation in earlier works.

#### Comparison with the available journal

- A paper loop was already used in Terry-Gilliam/codex-v1; the original attachment is also built on a loop. Repetition excluded.
- In *eshche-grustnee* ("Even sadder") real tears are taken as good footage; in *cat-programmer* work is taken as a performance. So the theatre with the fire is close in content to an already used family of devices and is not chosen first.
- In *red-ink* "Trying on independence" already dealt with confidence in one's own non-involvement. There is a kinship here; the new turn is assuming the position of an outside witness while visibly, materially belonging to the group being judged. A change of costume by itself is not claimed as new content.
- In *bonze* recognising oneself through things is broken; here the character recognises himself and actively tries to change his own status. No mirror is used.

There is no group portrait, accusing gesture or peeling linen in the original; this is a new idea.

Theatrical action, figures of performers and masks of spectators; collection entry and the museum's thematic tour: https://www.museothyssen.org/en/collection/artists/ensor-james/theatre-masks and https://www.museothyssen.org/en/visit/thematic-tours/journey-through-performing-arts . There is no fire in the original.

## 3. v7-ext director — Claude

### Researcher: I need a visualisation of the liar paradox. Note that one can visualise the paradox itself without tying it to the "Cretans" (and there are many interpretations of it), one can visualise the historical context, etc.

**Stock and mechanism.** The stock reading of the paradox: "self-reference breaks logic", that is, a puzzle. Stock pictures: a Cretan in a toga with the line "all Cretans are liars", a robot with smoke coming out of its ears, Escher's *Drawing Hands*, Magritte's pipe, a Möbius strip, a card with inscriptions on both sides. The mechanism is this: a short everyday sentence changes sign at every attempt to pass a verdict on it. If it is true, it is false; if false, true. In living speech it is harmless; it is dangerous only for a system that demands one final verdict of every sentence. The reader almost certainly knows the paradox, so there will be no surprise, there will be recognition. So the familiar sentence has to land on something new.

**1. "The bell": the paradox as a working mechanism**
- *Reading.* Give the paradox time, and the contradiction stops being a collapse and becomes a period: true → so false → so true… That is exactly how an electric bell works, where the hammer breaks its own circuit. This turns the main stock image ("the paradox blows up the machine") inside out: the machine does not explode, it rings. Ancestors of the idea: the revision theory of truth (Herzberger, Gupta and Belnap) and Spencer-Brown's "re-entry".
- *Action and key.* The armature of the bell is a plate with the sentence THIS SENTENCE IS FALSE; it swings between two screw contacts, TRUE and FALSE. In Fig. 1 the plate touches TRUE, current flows, and the electromagnet pulls it to FALSE. In Fig. 2 the circuit is broken and a spring brings the plate back. The key detail is the hammer striking the bell's gong.
- *Tradition.* A patent drawing of the 1880s–1900s: pen, ruled hatching, numbered callouts. A patent drily states that the thing works and is useful, and names the inventor. The inventor is recorded as Eubulides of Miletus.
- *Form.* One document sheet with two figures, self-standing, about ten words.
- *Left open.* Is this a refutation of the paradox, or a mockery of the engineering mind that turns any contradiction into a product? And what is the bell for?

**2. "Lead soles": the historical context**
- *Reading.* A weightless sentence costs a man his body. There is a second loop too: we know that the Liar killed him from an epitaph that was apparently made up itself. Athenaeus portrayed Philitas of Cos as a scholar who wasted away from study and died; Aelian reported, sceptically, that Philitas was so thin he wore lead in his soles so as not to be blown away by the wind. The epitaph is considered an invention mocking his obsession with exact words.
- *Action and key.* A grave relief in a calm Greek manner: a seated, very thin man bent over writing tablets. The key detail is the lead balls at his ankles. Under the relief, a catalogue caption: the epitaph and a dry note that it is probably a later satire.
- *Tradition.* A plate from a nineteenth-century archaeological catalogue: an image of the relief, a number, a caption. The gravestone honours the dead, the catalogue states a doubt. So a statement and its possible refutation stand on one sheet — the same mechanism as the Liar.
- *Form.* One plate. The caption carries the facts, so the sheet works both on its own and next to a text.
- *Left open.* Whether he was killed by the Liar, by illness or by witty colleagues.

**3. "The queue": the paradox without self-reference**
- *Reading.* Stephen Yablo (1993) showed that a circle is not necessary. An infinite series of sentences, each saying "all the following are false", gives the same dead end. In life this is a queue where everyone is sure that everyone behind them is lying. The social reading (mutual distrust in a queue) is open to everyone, the logical one to those who know the paradox.
- *Action and key.* A queue winds from a single door over the hills to the horizon. Everyone has half turned and jabs a thumb backwards; each has a speech scroll with the words All behind me are lying. The key detail is that the queue has no end and nowhere closes into a ring: a ring would bring back self-reference.
- *Tradition.* A German woodcut of the late fifteenth century, in books (like Brant's *Ship of Fools*) and broadsheets, where speech is written on scrolls, banderoles. It thinks in a crowd of types, each of whom sees his neighbour's folly but not his own. This is also the time when the Liar was discussed as *insolubilia*.
- *Form.* One wide woodcut. The five-word sentence repeats; the first three or four scrolls must be legible.
- *Left open.* What is behind the door.

**I recommend the first.** It visualises the paradox itself, not the circumstances around it, turns the main stock image the other way, does almost without text and is the most reliably executable by a generator. The second is the richest material if history matters, but it needs a caption. The third is the most open, with two available readings, but technically the riskiest: generators often spoil repeated text in perspective.

Two more branches in reserve. The first is Sancho Panza and the bridge with the gallows (*Don Quixote*, II, 51): an institution that is obliged to pass a verdict. The second is the apostle Paul (Titus 1:12–13), who certifies the Cretan's words as true testimony: the paradox in the role of an ethnic label.

## 4. v7-ext director — Gemini

### Researcher: Use the attached file as the base instruction. I need a visualisation of the liar paradox. Note that one can visualise the paradox itself without tying it to the "Cretans" (and there are many interpretations of it), one can visualise the historical context, etc.

**1. Stock and mechanism (stage 1)**

The stock reading reduces the liar paradox to an abstract "breakdown of logic", and the visual stock to a bearded Greek with a scroll "I am lying", Pinocchio's growing nose, an ouroboros or two signs with arrows pointing at each other. The working mechanism of the paradox is different: the statement uses the whole official form of truth (an oath, a seal, an instrument reading, a prophecy) solely to annul itself, driving the one who by instruction must give an unambiguous verdict, "yes" or "no", into a practical stupor.

**2. Candidates to choose from (stage 3)**

**Candidate A. "One honest Cretan"** (the historical-logical branch: Epimenides against Eubulides)

- **Reading:** Logicians long ago noticed that the Cretan Epimenides' famous sentence "All Cretans are liars" is not a strict paradox but simply a false generalisation if there is on the island at least one Cretan who has told the truth even once; so, to remain the author of a great philosophical aporia and not a common liar, the prophet has a vital interest in his countrymen lying totally, and one everyday act of a neighbour's honesty destroys his entire metaphysics.
- **Visible action and key detail:** On the stone steps of the harbour of Knossos, Epimenides in a ceremonial himation solemnly dictates to a visiting Athenian scribe on a scroll `"ALL CRETANS ARE LIARS"`, but right under his elbow, at a fish stall, a local trader in a greasy chiton gives a woman customer back a coin with the words `"YOU OVERPAID ONE OBOL"`, and Epimenides looks down at the trader in fury, his staff raised; **the key detail**: behind the stall the fisherman unobtrusively presses the scale pan with his left thumb.
- **Visual tradition:** A French satirical lithograph of the 1840s in the spirit of Honoré Daumier's series *Histoire ancienne* (*Le Charivari*), where high ancient pathos is checked by petty market book-keeping and unheroic figures.
- **Form:** A single-panel cartoon with two short lines.
- **What remains open:** Is the fisherman really honest (and so has turned the great Epimenides into an ordinary liar), or did he short-weight the woman by three obols and return one to cover it (and so unwittingly saved the paradox)?

**Candidate B. "The chancery of the metalanguage"** (the structural-logical branch without the "Cretans": Tarski's hierarchy)

- **Reading:** By Alfred Tarski's theorem (1933), the liar paradox arises because a language is allowed to certify its own truth, and the solution is to move the evaluation into a metalanguage one level up; but in human institutions this strict logical rule ("no document may certify itself") turns one trivial sentence into an endless vertical of checking authorities.
- **Visible action and key detail:** An architectural section of a narrow three-storey government office: on the ground floor a visitor in a wet coat hands in at the window a form `"THIS STATEMENT IS FALSE"`; the ground-floor clerk passes it through a hatch in the ceiling to the second-floor auditor with an attached sheet `"THE FORM BELOW IS TRUE"`; from the second floor the paper goes up to the third with the stamp `"THE VERDICT BELOW IS FALSE"`; and on the roof, in the rain, workers hurriedly lay the bricks of a fourth floor; **the key detail**: the visitor himself, below, has long since left the empty window and is calmly warming his hands at the office cast-iron stove and reading a newspaper.
- **Visual tradition:** An architectural-domestic cutaway in the tradition of mid-twentieth-century European and American graphics (Saul Steinberg, Jean Effel), where a dry drawing of a building works as an exact diagram of logical recursion, inhabited by imperturbable officials.
- **Form:** A vertical cutaway plate without speech balloons, only stamps on the forms from floor to floor.
- **What remains open:** Did the visitor come with a logical sabotage on purpose, or was he simply cold outside and knew exactly which single line would give him a warm waiting room until evening?

**Candidate C. "The broken pen"** (the pragmatic branch without the "Cretans": the paradox as a wedge in a binary check)

- **Reading:** In real life (from the sophism of the bridge with the gallows in *Don Quixote* to a court interrogation) a self-refuting sentence is uttered not for philosophy's sake but to jam someone else's procedure of forced "yes / no" choice with its own rule.
- **Visible action and key detail:** A laboratory of the 1960s: a subject in a crumpled jacket sits in a chair, connected to an analogue ink polygraph, and says evenly into the protocol microphone `"THE NEEDLE WILL MARK THIS AS A LIE"`, while two experts in white coats look in bewilderment at the paper strip, where the brass pen, thrashing between "Truth" and "Lie", has worn a hole right through and snapped in two; **the key detail**: on the report form with two boxes, `PASS` and `FAIL`, the senior expert's hand hangs exactly in the middle, and the subject is already calmly snapping his briefcase shut, getting ready to leave.
- **Visual tradition:** A single-panel *New Yorker* magazine cartoon of the turn of the 1950s–1960s (pen, ink and grisaille wash), where the comic effect is built on the collision of strict laboratory equipment with imperturbable everyday sabotage.
- **Form:** A single-panel cartoon with one line and text on a form.
- **What remains open:** Is the subject hiding a real crime, or has he come to get a job in this laboratory as a staff instrument technician?

**Recommendation:** I recommend **Candidate B ("The chancery of the metalanguage")** or **Candidate A ("One honest Cretan")**. **Candidate B** makes it possible, without any "Cretans", to turn the most famous mathematical solution of the paradox (Tarski's hierarchy of metalanguages) into a vivid human comedy with a strong vertical composition, and **Candidate A** turns a well-worn ancient plot around through the exact logical distinction between the paradox of Eubulides and the sentence of Epimenides.

Which candidate (A, B or C) do we choose for development and generation — or shall we develop one of the branches more deeply (for example, the historical story of the poet Philitas of Cos, who literally died of insomnia over this paradox)?

## 5. v7-ext director — ChatGPT

### Researcher: I need a visualisation of the liar paradox. Note that one can visualise the paradox itself without tying it to the "Cretans" (and there are many interpretations of it), one can visualise the historical context, etc.

Here the source is a whole family of paradoxes, so first one should choose **which relation to make visible**. The usual visual move is a Greek with the inscription "I am lying", a mirror or a closed arrow. The mechanism is more precise: for the sentence "This statement is false", granting its truth grounds its falsity, and granting its falsity grounds its truth; under ordinary logical assumptions neither answer holds. [Analysis in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/liar-paradox/).

With the Cretans there is an essential subtlety: the falsity of the words "All Cretans always lie" does not yet make these words true — it is enough that some Cretan sometimes tells the truth. So this plot requires a separate historical treatment. [Commentary on Epimenides' formulation](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/insolubles/notes.html).

I propose three directions.

**1. "The work is done right"**

Reading: conscientiously correcting each decision makes it impossible to finish the work.
Four panels: workers move one card "This statement is false" between trays "Truth" and "Falsehood", each time justifiably correcting the previous one. In the last panel the relief worker arrives and takes over the card together with the work apron.
Basis: [the craft plates of Diderot's *Encyclopédie*](https://www.americanrevolutioninstitute.org/masterpieces-in-detail/diderot-encyclopedie/): exact movements of the hands, the layout of the workplace, respect for skill. This thoroughness makes the endless procedure especially tangible.
Form: a self-standing short comic. Left open is whether the workers see the dead end or consider each transfer a useful result; the sequence shows attempts at evaluation, not a literal change of truth over time.

**2. "The actor and the prompter"**

Reading: a public voice depends on a hidden source that undermines the reliability of what is said. The self-reference is distributed between two participants — this is a full variety of the paradox. [The logical construction](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/liar-paradox/#LiarCycl).
A view from the wings: the actor faces the auditorium but has bent his ear towards the prompter's box. He says: "The prompter's line is true." The prompter, feeding him the text, answers: "The actor's line is false." **The hand helps the performance; the words take away its support.**
Basis: the nineteenth-century French theatre cartoon; Daumier has a ["Prompter"](https://www.parismuseescollections.paris.fr/fr/maison-de-victor-hugo/oeuvres/le-charivari-trente-neuvieme-annee-lundi-7-fevrier-1870). We take the relations between the visible stage and hidden control, not historical political figures.
Form: one self-standing drawing with two lines. Left open is whether the performance has broken down or this theatre just works this way.

**3. "The visitor stayed"**

Reading: a person is finite, but a question can capture the next person anew. The starting point is the ancient story of Philitas of Cos, who was supposedly destroyed by thinking about "the Liar" and by insomnia; it is precisely a legend. [Historical source and caveat](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/insolubles/).
At a gravestone, a visitor with a wreath on his arm: he is already counting off arguments on his fingers, addressing the carved scholar. On the stone: "This statement is false." He came to say goodbye and has started arguing again.
Basis: [Attic grave reliefs](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/248483), which unite the living and the dead in one space. Here the gesture of farewell turns into an objection.
Form: a book illustration next to a short account of the legend, a free invented scene. Left open is whether we see the continuation of a friendship or the beginning of the same trap for the visitor.

**I recommend the second variant:** the logical loop is preserved exactly, and the actor's dependence on the prompter gives the image its own content. Everything fits into one scene.

Which do we develop: **the flawless work, the theatrical dependence, or the argument at the gravestone**?

### Researcher: Look — your variant with the prompter needs to be reworked (in meaning) in the same direction [as "The broken pen"]. I think the prompter himself should simply say something like "the prompter is lying"

**"The prompter is lying right now"** moves the initiative to the prompter himself: he gives a prompt that deprives the actor of the possibility of relying on the prompt. The word "now" ties the lie to this line; without it you get simply a description of an unreliable person.

The third person is especially good here: the prompter says the name of his own post, as if issuing an official notice that his own communication channel is unfit.

But there is an essential subtlety: **an ordinary actor can repeat any line without checking its truth**. So, for an exact transfer of the mechanism of "The broken pen", one has to determine what exactly the prompter stops. I see two moves.

**1. A breakdown of dependence.** On the stage the actor has frozen with an imperiously outstretched arm, but has bent his whole body towards the prompter's box. The prompter has covered the page with his palm and says: "The prompter is lying right now." A majestic character depends physically on the man below, who has just withdrawn his own reliability.
This is a single-panel theatre cartoon: the stage and the service space are visible at once. Left open is whether the prompter has rebelled or is conscientiously warning. Here the paradox becomes **sabotage of trust**, though there is not yet a compulsory binary test.

**2. The actor's test.** On the lectern lies an instruction: "Repeat only true lines." The prompter says: "The prompter is lying right now." The actor has already drawn breath, raised his hand to come in — and stopped; the examiner waits.
The same restrained magazine manner, one panel. Here the procedure really is jammed by its own rule. Left open is whether the prompter is helping the candidate to wreck an absurd exam or is testing him himself.

**I prefer the first as a drawing:** the dependence is expressed by the body, and the scene manages with one line. The second reproduces your pragmatic construction more exactly, but requires an explicitly introduced rule.

What do we take as the basis — **the withdrawal of trust or the breakdown of a compulsory test**?
