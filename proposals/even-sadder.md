## Researcher: Before, we worked like this — I gave you a text, and you found classic works that could be used as inspiration for visualising the idea of the text. Now the reverse task. I have attached a picture. You need to come up with three variants of a plot that can be visualised on the basis of this picture. The plots, naturally, are not the ones the author of the picture originally intended for it. In this particular case, it is interesting that the main character was modelled by the son of the artist Gustav Klutsis, who was repressed and shot in 1938.

*The attached picture was Fyodor Reshetnikov's* Low Marks Again *(1952).*

With this painting one can change the very cause of the awkwardness: **what must the one who has come in justify himself for — and who gave the others the right to judge him?** Keeping the arrangement of the figures, we get three different plots.

The story about Klutsis's son is indeed told by [the Kultura.RF guide](https://www.culture.ru/s/novaya-tretyakovka/). It is especially important for the first idea. Below are invented scenes, not guesses about a hidden intention of Reshetnikov's.

**1. "He brought it again"**

After the father's arrest the household destroys everything that might recall their kinship. The boy found a thrown-away portrait, gathered the torn pieces and brought it home.

We hardly change the arrangement. He stands on the left in his coat, eyes lowered. Instead of a satchel he holds a large photograph in a damaged frame. The father's face is crossed by uneven strips of glued paper. On the wall behind the boy, a light rectangle where a portrait was taken down.

The mother sits with her hands clasped: she is afraid for her son, who has not yet learned to save himself by forgetting. The sister at the table holds a pair of scissors but is already looking towards the door. The youngest goes on riding. The dog jumps up at the one who has come in, keeping the ordinary joy of home amid this fear.

What matters here is that the family loves both the boy and the man in the photograph. That is exactly why the scene is agonising: the mother must make her son destroy the image of his father in order to protect the son himself.

**The key is the carefully glued portrait.** A child's work of restoring the family becomes dangerous disobedience. A familiar school guilt turns into punishment for loyalty.

**2. "No longer needed"**

The family is replacing the household robot that looked after the children for many years. The new model has already been delivered; the old one has been prepared for removal.

In the boy's place, a short, worn robot with a small suitcase. The pose is the same: shoulders lowered, head bowed. But its body keeps traces of family life: children's stickers, a clumsily drawn heart and, above all, **the children's height marks with dates**. Once they were measured standing with their backs against it.

The mother removes its tag with its household name and ties on a shipping label. The sister at the table studies the instructions for the new purchase; an open box stands beside her. The youngest on the bicycle happily holds a new remote control. The dog braces its paws against the old robot and keeps the packing from being finished.

All the human characters are busy with a perfectly reasonable improvement of their home. The viewer gradually notices that together with the appliance they are sending the witness of their own childhood to recycling.

The plot rests on the collision of two actions: **the family processes the replacement of a thing; the dog holds on to its own**. The height marks make its bond with the household visible, without a story about past services.

**3. "Even sadder"**

The family is filming a popular video about a negligent child. The scene of coming home with a bad mark has been repeated many times. The boy is tired of acting shame, and there are still no real tears.

The room, the clothes and the arrangement of the figures stay almost as they were. Only at the front edge of the picture a phone on a tripod appears. The sister, instead of a notebook, holds a sheet with the direction: "Even sadder". The mother keeps an upset expression, but her gaze is directed at the screen — she is checking whether her own upset is coming out well. The youngest on the bicycle watches his elder brother's work with pleasure.

The dog again jumps up at the boy and licks his chin. He barely keeps from smiling. It is exactly this moment that should be painted: a face that the viewer first takes for a crying one turns out, on a closer look, to be the face of someone suppressing a laugh.

The family is annoyed: the dog has spoiled the take again. The next one will work when the boy is really brought to tears.

Here the very scene of condemnation becomes a family product. The viewer is given an uncomfortable place by the camera: **this whole domestic trial is acted out for his attention**.

I would choose the first variant for development: the returned portrait changes the meaning of every glance and at the same time requires minimal intervention in the painting.

For the journal: source — *Low Marks Again* and the remark about Klutsis; relation — loved ones demand forgetting for the sake of safety; model — Reshetnikov's composition; action — a child brings back a destroyed portrait; key — the traces of glueing; remaining branches — writing off family memory, and the production of public shame.
