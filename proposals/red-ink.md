**The researcher's first message**, sent to both, gave the joke in two versions: an Armenian who repatriates from Italy to the USSR agrees with his brother that a letter in ordinary ink means things are good and one in green ink means they are bad; the letter comes in ordinary ink and lists every good thing, adding that the only minor shortcoming is that green ink is hard to get. Then it quoted Slavoj Žižek's version from *Welcome to the Desert of the Real* (in Russian translation): a worker from the German Democratic Republic sent to Siberia, blue ink for truth and red for lies, a letter in blue praising everything and ending "the only thing missing is red ink", followed by Žižek's application of the joke to today (we feel free because we lack the very language to articulate our unfreedom; the task is to give the protesters red ink). The researcher added: "So here it doesn't matter whether it's an Armenian or a German from the GDR. Žižek is precisely saying that the joke can be applied in a modern context too."

## 1. Director D — ChatGPT

### 1.1 Researcher: Let's try to make a visualisation of this joke: [the two versions and Žižek's comment]

I would start from a scene where **a person is offered the chance to speak, but among the available answers there is no answer for his own state**. I have already made the first sketch. From the search I chose two different ideas.

**1. "The pain scale"**

A man sits before a doctor, bent with pain. The doctor listens attentively, ready to write down the answer. Between them, a card: "How do you feel?" — and six faces, from a slight smile to complete delight. The patient's finger has stopped above the card.

Key: **the patient's face is the only expression that is not on the scale**.

What matters to me here is the doctor's sympathy. Both participants may conscientiously be trying to understand each other, and the offered way of describing still excludes what is happening. So "red ink" becomes the missing answer in modern, quite comfortable life.

The pictorial support is [Luke Fildes's *The Doctor*](https://victorianweb.org/painting/fildes/paintings/1.html): concentrated attention to the sick, tension between faces, a small lit area in which a human fate is decided. In the sketch this link is transferred to the patient, the doctor and the card.

**2. "Pressure"**

An apartment in the evening. A woman reads a letter with neat blue lines: "Everything is fine with me." Something has made her stop. She tilts the sheet towards the desk lamp.

The raking light reveals between the lines the word **"NO"**, pressed in with a dry pen. In one place the paper is almost torn through. There really was no ink for this word; the pressure of the hand remained.

Key: **the recipient reads the effort that the comfortable text was meant to hide**. This develops another side of the joke: the absence of a means of expression itself becomes a message. This is already a scene of inventing a way to tell the truth.

Support: [Gabriël Metsu's *Woman Reading a Letter*](https://onlinecollection.nationalgallery.ie/objects/details/8709). There a maid draws back the curtain on a painting with a restless sea, revealing an additional meaning of the letter. In our idea the light on the paper makes this discovery.

I chose the first variant for the sketch for the clarity of its visible contradiction. I see its limit too: it conveys the absence of the needed answer; Žižek's substitution of the concepts themselves can be developed further.

### 1.2 Researcher: Why the joke is hard to draw

> The original joke is about censorship. There is a censor who reads letters, and there is a code agreed in advance: blue is truth, red is a lie. It is the same as "blink three times if you are being held hostage". The joke is that the code runs into the absence of a means. But even in this case "pity there is no red ink" can become a substitute (*sapienti sat*).
> Žižek turned it differently. What he lacks is no longer ink but language: today it is impossible to write "there is only no red ink", because that is how the very language in which we speak about freedom is built.
> But this is a different situation. There is no direct censorship in it; instead of it, something else. So the joke in its raw form does not fit it: the mechanism "code against the censor" does not work if there is no censor.

Yes. **In the joke the heroes have, in advance, a language in which what is happening can be understood.** They know what "bad" is, can tell it from the official "good", and agree on a way of transmitting it. The difficulty arises in delivering a message already understood. The sentence about the missing ink becomes a new way of delivering it.

In the Žižekian reading the difficulty arises earlier: **in understanding one's own situation**. A person can feel dissatisfaction, complain, argue — but the available explanations direct the complaint so that the relation producing this dissatisfaction remains unrecognised. For example, dependence can be experienced and described exclusively as one's own lack of enterprise. Then even sincere self-criticism helps to reproduce the dependence.

So I would refine the word "impossible". It is quite possible to say "we lack language". But this sentence does not yet give the concepts that make it possible to understand a specific unfreedom. Whereas in the joke the message "there is no red ink" practically completes the transmission: the addressee understands exactly what happened.

**Žižek moves the lack from the level of transmission to the level of comprehension, and the structure of the joke as a whole does not follow this move.** The joke becomes a metaphor that keeps its expressiveness but loses its exact correspondence to its new subject.

This reveals the weakness of both my variants. In "The pain scale" the patient knows he is in pain; the institution offers an unfit form of answer. His own face already serves as that very "there is no red ink". In "Pressure" the hero altogether successfully gets round the obstacle. Both pictures keep a truth known in advance, which needs to find a way out.

For the Žižekian situation the task is harder: one has to show **an action by which a person tries to improve his situation and at the same time reproduces the cause of his trouble, because he understands it in the concepts available to him**. The viewer must see this relation between actions and consequences. If we simply put a suffering person next to a false inscription "happiness", we again get an already exposed lie — and lose exactly the difficulty you point to.

### 1.3 Researcher: Can you try to propose three more variants

I'll try three scenes in which **the very idea of the right action supports the trap**. Each has its own substitution.

1. **"Working on oneself"**

   A bright hall, mirrors, a wooden floor. Modern people are learning to keep their balance. One has fallen and is getting up, ashamed. The instructor patiently corrects the position of another's shoulders. The most successful pupil already stands steadily — in a painfully twisted pose.

   The whole room is tilted: floor, window frames, mirrors. Since they are consistent with one another, at first it is the people who seem crooked. Only the cord of a hanging lamp, hanging strictly vertical, reveals the structure of the space.

   Everyone looks in the mirrors, checking themselves. The instructor also stands on this floor and conscientiously passes on the skill he has mastered.

   **Key: a person takes the ability to adapt to a general deformation for the measure of his own worth.** He can say "I'm not steady enough", "I need to work on myself more". The thought of how the room itself is built does not enter his explanation of failure.

   Pictorial support: [Degas's *The Dance Class*](https://www.musee-orsay.fr/fr/oeuvres/la-classe-de-danse-1151): the parquet receding into depth, work on the body, tired and self-correcting figures. The tilt of the room is our change.

2. **"An honest division"**

   Several people have gathered at a big dining table. On it, bread, plates, a jug. They carefully divide **the table itself**: draw lines, check measurements, saw the tabletop into equal shares.

   Mutual goodwill matters here. One holds a board for his neighbour, another checks that nobody is short-changed. The one who has received his part sits a little to one side: he holds it with both hands, a plate stands on it — and so he cannot eat.

   In the centre a small area of whole tabletop still remains. The last line of the cut runs across it.

   **Key: everyone receives his own, and what they could have used together disappears.** Their concept of justice allows detailed discussion of the size of shares, the order, the accuracy of measurement. Use of a common table looks, in this system, like an unfinished division of property.

   Support: [the Le Nain brothers' *Peasant Family at a Meal*](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010059565): restrained gestures, serious faces, a poor meal. I would keep this human seriousness. The people must look convinced of the justice of what is happening.

3. **"A balance of interests"**

   A man carries on his shoulders a long yoke with two heavy baskets. By each stands a helper. They argue, compare the loads and move stones.

   The carrier himself takes an active part: he stretches out his arm and shows where to add one more stone. The helper does as asked. The yoke at last becomes strictly horizontal — at the very second when the carrier's knees begin to give way.

   His gaze is fixed on the balance achieved.

   **Key: the proof of improvement accepted by all is compatible with an increase in weight.** Disagreement exists here; a person can demand corrections and get his way. But the whole argument goes on in the concepts of imbalance and balance; the total load remains outside the discussion.

   Pictorial interlocutor: [Vermeer's *Woman Holding a Balance*](https://www.nga.gov/artworks/1236-woman-holding-balance): concentration on equilibrium and the precision of a small gesture. In our picture the calm horizontal of the yoke comes into contradiction with the buckling body.

I would develop **"An honest division"** further. In it, it especially matters that the people successfully carry out their idea of justice. The loss arises inside this success — and can be shown by one unfinished cut.

### 1.4 Researcher: For "An honest division" it's better to take Norman Rockwell's *Freedom from Want* as the basis. But here are some more ideas for you: Remember the film *They Live*? It had special glasses that let you see the lie. If we use the diptych idea, in the first panel they work and in the second they don't. But there is a paradox here — if we can paint such a picture, then our picture is itself the red ink, and then Žižek is wrong. Something else to think about: Utagawa Kuniyoshi's cats. And another: traditionally the tale of the naked king is taken as a story about conformism. But Shestov in *The Apotheosis of Groundlessness* says that everyone knew perfectly well that the king was naked; only the boy wasn't in the know.

Rockwell is much more exact for "An honest division". In [*Freedom from Want*](https://audiotour.nrm.org/freedom-from-want/) the hostess serves a big turkey to the common table. I would keep exactly this gesture: **she is already lowering the dish, and there is no longer a common place for it**. Everyone has received his part of the tabletop. There is food, there is goodwill, the feast is prepared — what is destroyed is the relation thanks to which all this became a common meal. Such a turn is stronger than the original scene with the saws.

And your other examples make it necessary to separate two difficulties we had begun to mix: **the impossibility of recognising one's situation, and the possibility of knowing about it while continuing to take part**.

I half agree with the paradox of the picture. If we really create an image that makes it possible to see a previously indistinguishable relation, it becomes a certain quantity of "red ink". But it does not yet follow that Žižek is wrong: in the fragment you quoted he directly sets the task of *giving* this ink to the protesters. The possibility of its appearance is presupposed there.

But this objection does hit my earlier, too strong formulation — as if language were built so that a way out is completely impossible. With such a claim the philosopher's own reasoning becomes a problem too. It is more exact to speak of a lack of certain means of understanding. One can name a "blind spot" before having seen what is in it. And one can paint an effective allegory that will only repeat an already familiar denunciation.

**Shestov introduces another, independent turn.** You remembered exactly: it is the first part of *The Apotheosis of Groundlessness*, aphorism 19. It says: "After all, even without him everyone knew the king was naked." Further on it is about teaching children the rules of permissible speech. The boy sees the same as the adults; he does not yet know how one is supposed to deal with what one sees. [Shestov's text](https://predanie.ru/book/69932-apofeoz-bespochvennosti/full/).

So the glasses in the second panel may "not work" in a very meaningful sense: **they have nothing additional to expose, and the ceremony goes on anyway**. This is now a question of the power of knowledge, not of its absence.

I would try your diptych like this:

- **Left:** through glasses raised before the eyes, a festive advertisement reveals an order. Inside the lenses and outside them, different images. The viewer gets the expected pleasure of exposure.
- **Right:** the same glasses raised in the same gesture. Both inside the lenses and outside, a completely naked king. The adults solemnly carry his non-existent train. The boy points at the king, and his father kindly shows him where to pick up the edge of the train.

Here the father does not hide what he sees from the child. He teaches him to take part. And the viewer, who felt perceptive in the first half, discovers in the second the limit of his own perceptiveness. This is my interpretation of your glasses not working; it leads precisely in Shestov's direction.

With Kuniyoshi there is another important move. In his line of cat-actors, recognition was secured by familiar faces and characteristic features transferred into a permitted form. Such ways round restrictions are documented in [the Asian Civilisations Museum catalogue](https://www.nhb.gov.sg/acm/-/media/acm/acm2017/documents/life-in-edo-russel-wong-in-kyoto/life-in-edo_label-translations_2nd-rotations_eng.pdf?la=en). This is an example of how a pictorial language really produces "ink": the viewer understands more than the image directly names.

But for our search the following question is especially interesting: **what happens after recognition?** One can recognise the actor in the cat, the order in the advertisement, the naked king in the king — and consider the work of criticism complete. A diptych can catch exactly this moment of satisfaction: the viewer has figured everything out, and in the second half of the picture he is already being invited to hold the train.

### 1.5 Researcher: One more thought. The film *The Matrix* is supposedly against simulacra, but it itself became a franchise and a brand, just as "Buddhism" in the West became "enlightenment at a discount".

Yes, and here a more complex possibility arises: **the sense of leaving the system becomes one of the ways of taking part in it**.

With *The Matrix* what matters is not only the fact of its becoming a brand. A commercial film is quite capable of telling an essential truth. The substitution happens at the moment when the viewer takes the exposure he has experienced for his own liberation: *I have learned the mechanism, recognised myself in Neo — so I am already among the awakened*. One can go on with one's former life, having received a new, more flattering idea of one's place in it.

With "enlightenment at a discount" the turn is similar. Paying for instruction refutes nothing in itself. But the practice of liberation from attachments can turn into acquiring one more advantage: calm, productivity, competitiveness. A person learns to bear more easily the order he was going to reconsider.

Žižek develops this thought directly in the article ["From Western Marxism to Western Buddhism"](https://www.cabinetmagazine.org/issues/2/zizek.php): cultivated inner distance, in his view, allows one to go on taking part intensely in capitalist life while keeping a sense of non-involvement. This is a criticism of a certain use of spiritual practice; extending it to the whole of Buddhism would be too broad.

For our search, three positions can now be told apart:

- **The joke:** I understand what is happening, but it is hard to deliver the message.
- **Shestov:** we understand what is happening and support the convention.
- **Your *Matrix*:** the very experience of exposure becomes a service provided to us within the existing order.

The last changes the meaning of the diptych too. **In the second panel the glasses may go on working flawlessly.** They show the orders behind the advertising, reveal manipulation — and the person buys the next, improved model of glasses. He considers this purchase a continuation of his liberation. Then one has to depict the moment when the ability to see turns into a certificate of one's own independence.

And our picture is exposed to the same risk. The viewer may get pleasure from recognising the mechanism, feel himself a person who can no longer be fooled — and be satisfied with that. So it is not enough to put one more deceived customer into the image: that would give the viewer a comfortable position above him in advance.

In the terms of the original joke the new substitution would sound like this: **red ink has gone on sale, and buying it has come to be taken for a letter already written.**

### 1.6 Researcher: Yes — that's a good thought — one has to depict the moment when the ability to see turns into a certificate of one's own independence. Try then to revise the variants we considered and propose two reworked ones

I would keep **the hero's rightness in both variants**. He really has seen something. The substitution happens with the next movement: "I see this — so it no longer applies to me."

**1. "Trying on independence" — the reworked diptych with the glasses**

On the left a man looks for the first time through glasses at an attractive advertisement. Through the image the order "BUY" shows through. He is tense, leaning a little forward: the familiar surface of the world has stopped being reliable.

On the right the same man, in the same working glasses, tries on a T-shirt before a mirror. On it is printed, large, **"BUY"**. He smooths the fabric, turns half round, chooses a facial expression. The price tag is still hanging.

He understands the inscription perfectly well and wears it ironically — as the sign of a person who has figured out the mechanism. Now he is occupied with how convincingly he looks like a person who cannot be bought.

**In the first panel the order becomes visible. In the second it becomes part of the attractive image of the exposer himself.** The ability to see has received a convenient form that can be worn.

The compositional principle of Rockwell's [*Triple Self-Portrait*](https://prints.nrm.org/detail/261054/rockwell-triple-self-portrait-1960) helps here: a man, his reflection and the image of himself being created. In our version the image of oneself is put together right during the fitting.

I would avoid a smug grimace. Better a pleasure familiar to everyone: at last a thing has been found that exactly expresses *what I am like*. Then it is harder for the viewer to wall himself off from the hero.

**2. "To our understanding" — the reworked "An honest division"**

We keep the basis of [Rockwell's *Freedom from Want*](https://audiotour.nrm.org/freedom-from-want/): a close circle of people, a white tablecloth, the hostess with a big dish. The table has already been sawn into separate personal shares. There is nowhere to put the common dish.

In the earlier variant the main action was the sawing. Now it is finished.

A guest at the near edge lifts the tablecloth and shows the others the cuts: he has accurately discovered why the dinner cannot take place. People turn to him, laugh at the apt remark, raise their glasses in his honour. He is still pointing at the gap, but already accepting congratulations.

The hostess smiles too. Only her hands are tense: the heavy dish still has to be held in the air.

**At the table a new common cause has arisen — to acknowledge what understanding people they are.** An accurate remark has become an occasion to confirm the independence of the whole company. For a few moments this has even given back its lost unity.

I would turn the nearest guest towards the viewer: he invites us to share the pleasure of an accurate observation. In front of us is one more separate piece of tabletop. We can raise a glass too.

This variant now seems to me stronger for a whole canvas. Here there is time that can be felt in the body: the congratulations drag on, and the dish grows heavier.

## 2. Director B — Claude

### 2.1 Researcher: Let's try to make a visualisation of this joke: [the two versions and Žižek's comment]

Žižek is right: the hero's nationality decides nothing here; the mechanism is what matters. But at least three different mechanisms are at work in the joke, and each gives its own picture.

**Three readings**

The first concerns the initiated and the uninitiated. Only the one who knows the agreement receives the truth. The censor and everyone else see a cheerful complaint about a trifle and let through a verdict hidden in the permitted genre of "individual shortcomings".

The second: power takes away the instrument and leaves the words alone. The censor has no need to strike out a lie; it is enough not to deliver any red. Whoever controls the warehouse controls the dictionary too. When no words are left, the blank sheet starts to speak, and the same censor reads it without mistakes.

The third, Žižek's: a contented person who has no word for discontent. The abundance in the letter (shops, films, girls) does not cover a lie here but serves as the price. For a full fridge one pays with one missing colour, and a person may not even notice this absence.

From each branch I got one idea.

**1. "The assayers"**

Model: Rembrandt, *The Syndics of the Drapers' Guild* (1662). The painting shows officials who assessed the quality of dyed cloth. They all look at us, and one even rises from his chair, as if he has noticed our presence. The table is covered with a heavy red Oriental carpet.

Scene: the same arrangement, the same carpet, a low viewpoint. I would keep Rembrandt's costumes. On the carpet, instead of cloth samples, lies a blank sheet of A4 in a plastic sleeve with an evidence tag. The commission has looked up from its work and looks at the viewer. Before one of the syndics a report lies open, and he is already writing in it in red ink.

Key: red ink exists, but in this picture only the commission has it. The blank sheet cannot say anything by itself. The commission reads it without error and writes in with its own hand what is "written" on it. Here the idea goes further than the joke. There the censor does not notice the catch; here he turns out to be the only literate reader of emptiness. The viewer, at whom the assayers look, stands in the place of the one who brought this sheet. The link with today is literal: in 2022, both in China and in Russia, people were detained for a blank sheet.

**2. "A wide choice"**

Model: Andreas Gursky, *99 Cent* (1999). A frontal photograph of a cheap supermarket, where the goods dissolve into an endless even pattern.

Scene: a wall of stationery from floor to ceiling, thousands of bottles and pens in every shade of blue, from icy to midnight. On the price tags, instead of the names of shades, stand words from Žižek's list: "Freedom", "Democracy", "Human rights", "War on terror". Around, shoppers with baskets. In the foreground a man with an unfinished letter holds two bottles up to the light, "Freedom" and "Democracy", and chooses between them. Their colour is the same. Behind his shoulder, in the solid pattern, hangs one empty hook. The man doesn't see it, but we do.

Key: the man is so busy choosing between identical blues that he doesn't notice the gap. The false concepts Žižek writes about turn out here to be not lies but an assortment.

**3. "A letter from Siberia"**

Model: Laktionov, *A Letter from the Front* (1947). Bright sun, a boy reads a letter, beside him a girl, two women and the soldier who brought it. The painting was endlessly reproduced in school textbooks, calendars and magazines, so it is the canonical image of a "good letter".

Scene: the same sunlit threshold and the same arrangement. The boy is finishing the last line, about the red ink, and the women laugh at the funny trifle. In the soldier's place stands the friend who agreed on the colour before leaving. He doesn't laugh: he has raised the envelope to the sun and is looking through the paper to see what colour the lines are.

Key: the family laughs at the "minor shortcoming" just as we laugh at the joke. The one who knows the agreement understands that this is the report. Laktionov's sun, that is, the very genre of the happy letter, works as the blue ink, and only one person uses this light to check their colour.

I would do "The assayers". It has the strongest turn of its own, and the model coincides almost mystically: Rembrandt painted people who professionally checked dye. "A wide choice" will do if Žižek's thesis matters most. "A letter from Siberia" will do if the anecdote itself is needed, recognisable at first glance.

### 2.2 Researcher: Why the joke is hard to draw

*The same message as in section 1.2.*

I agree with the main point, and it hits my variants harder than it seems. First two corrections to the reasoning, then conclusions for the picture.

**The first.** The postscript about the ink here is not a spare substitute but the joke itself. The joke tells a success story: the code breaks, and it is precisely the breakdown that delivers the message. The censor lets through a complaint about a trifle; the friends read a verdict in it. For this three are needed: the writer, who knows the truth; the censor, who prevents him from telling it; the addressee, who remembers the agreement. *Sapienti sat* works as long as there is a *sapiens* with the key.

**The second.** In Žižek two drop out at once. There is no censor, and the writer does not know that he lacks anything: he "feels free". So his letter ends with the girls who are easy to get, and there is no postscript in it. Žižek himself adds it, from outside. His version is essentially the joke without the last line. The censor's place is taken by the dictionary. The censor has moved from the post office into language and works earlier: he does not strike out a finished thought, he prevents it from forming. Curiously, Žižek's version is already prepared for this. For the Armenian, colour encodes the world: whether it is good or bad there. For the German, colour encodes the letter itself: whether it holds truth or lies. The second code already speaks about language, not about life.

Hence the difficulty with the picture, and it is built deeper than it seems. Painting can show absence, but the viewer reads any absence shown as a signal. The viewer of a picture is always initiated. So the picture, by its very structure, stands on the side of the joke: a lack that nobody experiences as a lack cannot be painted, because once painted it is already experienced.

Now honestly about my three variants. "The assayers" and "A letter from Siberia" live in the world of the joke. There is a censor there, and the code works. "The assayers" are true to their material: the blank sheet in Moscow and Shanghai runs into direct censorship, and the censor understands it first. But they have nothing to do with Žižek. "A wide choice" aimed at Žižek and tripped over exactly your thought. The empty hook is the postscript "there is no red ink", that is, the mechanism of the joke: I gave the viewer a key and thereby brought back the censor.

The way out, I think, is one: paint not lack but a fullness that doesn't work. In a world without a censor there is red ink, as much as you like, but it means nothing: red meant "lie" only for the one who reads other people's letters. Without an opponent the code falls apart into mere colours. The viewer will get a key in any case; the question is which. Let it be "there is enough ink, there is no language", not "there is no ink".

I see two ways.

**"A wide choice", corrected.** No empty hook. On the same endless wall, next to "Freedom" and "Democracy", hang red pens with labels "Protest", "Resistance", "Revolution". On the counter lies a sheet for trying pens, covered by shoppers: flourishes, names, and someone has written in large letters "NO!". All the flourishes are blue, and next to this "NO" lies the red pen it was written with. Key: a casing of any colour, one refill. The protesters have ink but no language, so they object in the same words they were given.

**The diptych "The letter" / "The delivery".** The left leaf repeats "A letter from Siberia": the sunny threshold, the family laughing at a trifle, the friend looking at the envelope against the light. The code works. The right leaf shows the writer's room today: a big apartment, well heated, on the floor an opened courier box with a dozen red bottles delivered next-day. He sits at the table and writes with the new red ink the same thing: "Everything here is absolutely wonderful…". No postscript; there's nothing to complain about. Key: the lack was closed before anyone managed to write about it, and it turned out that it was not ink that was lacking. This is your thesis directly as a picture: the joke is painted twice, and the second leaf shows why it cannot be transferred.

The diptych is closer to me. It makes the very gap between the joke and Žižek the subject of the picture instead of papering it over. Which way do we take?

### 2.3 Researcher: Remember the film *They Live*? … [the same message as in section 1.4]

#### The paradox

It is real. I wouldn't solve it; I'd build it into the picture. But first it is worth understanding what exactly it refutes.

Our picture refutes Žižek only in the literal formulation: "we lack the language for unfreedom". That formulation is already refuted by the book in which it is printed. Žižek himself has a stronger and older formula. In *The Sublime Object of Ideology* he takes from Sloterdijk cynical reason: "they know very well what they are doing, but still, they are doing it". Ideology rests not on ignorance but on action.

Shestov says the same almost eighty years before Sloterdijk: everyone knows the king is naked. Everyone has the knowledge, so the red ink is not in it. And Andersen has an ending that is usually forgotten. After the boy's cry the whole people shout the same thing. The king feels that the people are right, but decides that the procession must be carried through, and walks on even more majestically, and the chamberlains carry the train that isn't there. The whole crowd writes in red ink, and nothing happens.

It follows that red ink is not the possibility of saying, but something said that changes something. We can paint the picture. The question is whether it will stop the procession. If it doesn't, it does not refute Žižek but illustrates him.

Here Žižek has a weak spot, and your paradox catches exactly it. In this wording the thesis is irrefutable. If criticism is forbidden, unfreedom is proved. If it is allowed but changes nothing, unfreedom is proved too.

#### Kuniyoshi

Kuniyoshi is the first leaf in pure form. The Tenpō reforms of 1841–1843 officially forbade depicting courtesans and actors in prints. His answer was "Scribbles on a Storehouse Wall": supposedly a wall covered in graffiti, in fact portraits of actors with hints at their roles. The title contains the pun *nita kara*, that is, "aren't they alike?". The joke turned out so subtle that the sheet bears the approval seals of two censors. This is our joke entire: the censor stamps a letter with a code, and the title says outright *sapienti sat*.

Then the second leaf begins. At the end of the 1840s the reforms stopped being observed, Kuniyoshi went back to ordinary actor portraits, but his cats remained. The code outlived the censor and turned into a style, and then into a commodity. The same happened with OBEY.

#### The diptych "Aren't they alike?"

**Left leaf.** A street, a procession, a king in a luxurious mantle. The crowd is silent and bows. On the wall behind it, permitted scribbles in the spirit of Kuniyoshi: chalk cats, one of them in a crown, and a red stamp "Permitted". In the foreground a boy in black glasses. In the lens, black-and-white by the film's convention, the king is naked. The glasses work: the truth is hidden, a code is needed, and the code passes the censor.

**Right leaf.** The same street, the same procession, the same boy. The king is now naked for everyone; the chamberlains carry the non-existent train. The crowd shouts and films on phones; everyone's T-shirt reads «А КОРОЛЬ-ТО ГОЛЫЙ!» ("BUT THE KING IS NAKED!"). By the wall stands a stall: mugs with a cat in a crown, a carousel with the same black glasses and, among the other goods, a reproduction of the left leaf. The boy looks through the lens and sees the same as outside, in colour. Only he is surprised. The adults around look at him indulgently.

Key: on the right leaf the glasses are not broken; they simply have nothing to show. The truth is not hidden, it is printed on the chest, and the procession goes on. Shestov's boy here is the one who doesn't know that everyone knows. The reproduction of the left leaf on the stall answers your paradox inside the frame: our red ink is already on sale next to the mugs.
