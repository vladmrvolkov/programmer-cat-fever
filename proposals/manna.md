# A virtual director on Warhol and Yakovlev: a text for two paintings

> **This is a translation, not the original.** The conversation was held in Russian. This file is a plain technical translation into English made by Claude (Opus 5.5) on 12 November 2026 for this repository. It is not a literary translation, and wording, rhythm and puns of the originals are not kept. The Russian original is not published here. Lines and captions that the director wrote in English are reproduced as written. Headings marked **Researcher:** are the human researcher's messages, translated.

The director is Claude with the [v7-ext](../method/system-prompts/v7-ext/) prompt. This is a reverse task: the researcher gave two paintings and asked for a literary basis for them. Drawn and tested: "What if we can it?" (test [87](../tests/87-manna-seal-in-jars/README.md)). The image prompt and the director's review of the result are left out.

## Researcher: We used to work like this — I gave a text task and you created the concept of an image. I want to do the reverse experiment: I give a picture, and you must come up with a literary basis for it, including a joke, an abstract concept, etc. Look at the pictures in the attachment

*The attachment was a photograph of Andy Warhol's* Campbell's Soup Cans *as hung at MoMA and Boris Yakovlev's* Soviet Preserves.

I accept the reverse move. I turn the method round like this: the picture is given, and I look for a text next to which it works in the mode "next to a text". The text carries the mechanism (laughter, thought), and the image adds a reading that is not in the text. The literal solution here is ekphrasis, that is, a text retelling the picture; it is the analogue of slop. The check is the same, only reversed: name what the picture adds to the text and what does not follow from the text itself.

**What is given.** Warhol, *Campbell's Soup Cans*, 1962. Here it is a photo of the hanging at MoMA: 32 canvases, 4 rows of 8. A correction to the file name of the second painting: it is *Soviet Preserves* by Boris Yakovlev, 1939, the Tretyakov Gallery. Not 1937 and not Vasily Yakovlev, his namesake and also a painter. I read the pair as a diptych; if two separate tasks were intended, tell me.

**What I see, before interpretations.** Warhol has 32 identical compositions, differing only in the name of the variety. Not one can is open, the contents are not visible at all, only the label. The gold medal on each is an empty circle without an inscription. One can (Cheddar Cheese, bottom row, fifth from the left) has a ribbon "NEW! GREAT AS A SAUCE TOO!".

In Yakovlev almost everything is in glass: the contents are visible, and the labels are illegible patches. Around them, straw and opened crates; at the bottom left on the floor lie a hammer, pincers and nails. One tin is open (the lid bent back), next to it a plate with apricot halves — the only portion laid out. There are no people.

**Stock reading and mechanism.** The stock reading of this pair is "theirs / ours": serial consumption against painted abundance with empty shelves. Baudrillard and Benjamin stick to Warhol at once, the date to Yakovlev. Visually this comparison of two systems has already been made by Sots Art (Komar and Melamid, Kosolapov), so it is the main attractor.

The paintings' own mechanisms differ. Warhol takes by imperturbable repetition: the eye looks for a difference and finds only words. Yakovlev takes by the appetite of painting: light through glass and syrup, abundance at the moment of unpacking. The text must not explain the first or drown the second in irony.

**Candidates.** I checked three branches: trust (the word against the eye), time (the store against the gift), event (the novelty against the delivery).

**1. "What if we can it?"** — a joke on biblical material

> Moses announced: gather manna for one day, don't leave any till morning — it will spoil.
> From the crowd someone asked: "What if we can it?"

Relation: providence against the pantry; household common sense seriously solves a problem set by God. The pair adds what the text does not say: both main images of twentieth-century abundance answer "yes, we canned it". Abundance is painted as a store, and a store is fear of tomorrow. The plate in Yakovlev is the only "portion for today", taken out of the store.

There is a second layer for those who look for it. In the Book of Numbers the people tire of the same manna and remember the cucumbers of Egypt. Warhol has one food under 32 names (he himself, by his own account, lunched on this soup for years); Yakovlev has jars of cucumbers. Which canvas is the wilderness and which the "fleshpots" remains open. Ancestors: Exodus 16 and Numbers 11, plus the genre of the Jewish joke in which one haggles with God over trifles.

**2. "In glass"** — a joke

> A Soviet guest is shown round an American shop. He stands for a long time at the shelf of soups.
> "And how do you know what's inside?"
> "It says so."
> "That's why ours are in glass."

Relation: trust in the word against trust in the eye. The last line shows a whole world where what is written lies by default, and yet does not name propaganda. The pair adds a categorical contrast: tin shows only the name, glass only the contents. Hence a second turn: the world "with one's own eyes" is an oil painting of 1939, that is, the hero trusts his eyes and is looking at a canvas. Who is naive here remains open.

Ancestor: the joke about the fence on which one thing is written and firewood lies behind it. To be honest, this candidate lives inside the stock frame "theirs / ours"; the picture only turns it over.

**3. "NEW! = They're giving it out!"** — a dictionary entry

> NEW! GREAT AS A SAUCE TOO! — translates into Russian as «Дают! Бери, потом разберёмся» ("They're giving it out! Grab it, we'll sort it out later").

Relation: a thing becomes an event in two ways. Either it is new and has to persuade, even invent a second use for itself. Or it has been delivered, and nobody needs persuading. The pair adds a proportion: in Warhol the event is one small ribbon on 32 canvases, while in Yakovlev it takes up the whole picture. The crates are opened, the straw, the tools dropped on the floor — the delivery itself is the subject.

Ancestors: Bierce's *Devil's Dictionary* and Flaubert's *Dictionary of Received Ideas*, where a definition works as a joke. Open: whose greed is funnier. This too is the frame "theirs / ours".

**I recommend the first.** Only it leaves the stock frame: it does not set the canvases against each other but makes one statement of them. It is here that the picture adds most, though the text never names it. It has both a joke and an abstract concept under it — the store as distrust of tomorrow.

There is also an unchecked branch: the ribbon NEW! as a newcomer, who alone has to explain what else he is good for.

Which do we take — or dig further?

## Researcher: You said: "Visually this comparison of two systems has already been made by Sots Art (Komar and Melamid, Kosolapov)". What specific works do you mean?

When I wrote that, I had no specific work in mind. I meant the general move of Sots Art — combining Soviet visual language with pop art — and presented it as if a comparison of exactly such a pair had already been made. That is exactly the confident packaging of a general impression that the method warns against. I have now checked; here is what really exists and how close it is to our pair.

**Komar and Melamid.** A direct hit is *Post-Art No. 1 (Warhol)*, 1973. They repeated Warhol's Campbell's Tomato Soup can by hand and burned the canvas in several places. It is part of a series: six paintings of 1973–74 in which canonical pop works by Warhol, Lichtenstein, Johns, Indiana, Wesselmann and Phillips are shown as if they had survived a war or a fire. Komar recalls asking Warhol why he had painted the hammer and sickle. Warhol joked that they had done his soup can, and he had done their hammer and sickle.

An important caveat: this is not a comparison of two systems but a look at one from outside. The canvases looked as if Soviet citizens had rescued them after a catastrophe that befell capitalist America.

Sots Art proper of 1972–73 — for example, *Double Self-Portrait* (1972) and *We Were Born to Make a Fairy Tale Come True* (1972) — works with Soviet agitprop as pop material. There are no American goods there: they did not paint cans and branded bottles, as Warhol did. Incidentally: Warhol gave them his soul for free — this is their action of buying and selling souls.

**Kosolapov.** The main work is *Lenin — Coca-Cola*: a red ground, the Coca-Cola logo, Lenin's profile and the slogan "It's the real thing". Sources disagree on the date: Wikipedia gives 1980, the MIT collection holds a 1982 version, and there are later canvases. The story of the Times Square billboard in 1982 also varies. By some accounts it was shown there; by others the project never happened, and Coca-Cola decided that the company was advertising communism.

Closer to our pair is something else — his cans of caviar, conceived as an answer to Campbell's soup cans. Under them lies his own thesis: in America rich and poor drink the same Coca-Cola, while in Russia only the rich eat caviar. The same move is in his *Mickey-Lenin* (2003) and similar works.

**Closest to our pair is no longer Sots Art.** At the exhibition "Andy Warhol and Russian Art" (St Petersburg, 2021), Warhol's soup cans were hung next to Dmitry Shorin's open can of Soviet condensed milk and a black-caviar wrapper. There was also Viktor Ponomarenko's still life: McDonald's bags and cups in the manner of the old Dutch masters. This is the mirror move to Yakovlev. I have not seen the works themselves; I know them only from the description in a review.

**What I correct.** I know no work that puts Yakovlev's still life and Warhol's cans side by side. Other things have been done: Warhol's can as a ruin (Komar and Melamid), the Soviet can as privilege against the American mass one (Kosolapov's caviar, Shorin's condensed milk), and Western fast food in the Dutch manner (Ponomarenko).

For the candidates of the previous answer this matters more than I said then. The third ("NEW! = They're giving it out!") almost repeats Kosolapov's thesis: one product for all against the product as event or privilege. The second ("In glass") stands next to it. The first ("What if we can it?") does not, as far as I know, overlap with any of these works.

## Researcher: Look. The Soviet painting is that very "abundance" which people strove for but could not reach (that same problem of "sausage", or "the fridge versus the TV"). American abundance — as people in the late USSR imagined it — is that very dream they strove for. But it all turned out to be just canned food — almost identical.

I accept the reading. First I'll name where the stock is in it, then what in the paintings themselves sharpens it.

**The stock.** Your reading repeats the move of an old joke: everything we were told about communism was a lie, and everything about capitalism was the truth. Next to it stand the "sausage emigration" and the picture the viewer will remember first: Yeltsin in a Houston supermarket in 1989. If the text simply says "they dreamed of abundance — they got identical cans", the pair will illustrate it rather than add to it.

**What the paintings themselves add.**

- The dream was already canned food. Yakovlev paints not a garden and not a market but preserves in crates with straw. So "it turned out to be canned food" is not a discovery; what changed is something else: glass was replaced by tin. In the dream you can see what's inside; in the fulfilled dream you can see only the name.
- The dream was more varied than reality. In Yakovlev no two jars are the same: different shapes, sizes, colours. In Warhol the form is one, and your "almost" is a word on the label.
- The sameness is one and the same, but the assessments of it are opposite. For Warhol it is a virtue: in his *Philosophy* he praises Coke because the President and a bum get the same one. For the one who went for abundance it is a disappointment. But at the same time it is the fulfilment of another promise, the Soviet one — equality.
- In your terms both pictures are the TV. In the photo, Warhol's cans hang not on a shelf but in frames on a museum wall. There is no fridge in this pair.

**Candidates.**

**1. "It's communism here"** — a joke

> A letter from New York, early nineties: "Mama, it's communism here: there's plenty of everything, and it's all the same."

The dream came true entirely, including the promise nobody went for: equality turned out to be sameness. The pair adds what is not in the text: the dream on the left is more varied than the fulfilment on the right, and the dream was a can too. Ancestor: the joke about "everything true about capitalism". The turn is that capitalism did not deceive here but fulfilled communism. Open: what kind of letter this is — a complaint or a confession.

**2. "Through glass"** — an aphorism

> While you dream, you see what's inside. When you've got it, you read labels.

Desire is visual, possession is a choice between names. Which side is where, the pair shows: glass with illegible labels against tin on which there is nothing but a label. Ancestor: Wilde's two tragedies — not getting what one wants, and getting it (*Lady Windermere's Fan*). Open: which is worse — reading labels or looking through glass.

**3. "Above the table"** — a micro-story

> For thirty years a clipping from a magazine hung above his kitchen table. In Brooklyn, where all of it was sold round the corner, he hung another in the same place.
> "Why?" asked his wife. "The shop's right next door."
> "In the shop it's not the same."

The dream outlives its fulfilment: even with a full fridge a person keeps a TV for himself. The text does not say what is on the clippings; the pair shows both. It also adds that America too hung its soup on the wall, only a museum one. Open: what exactly is "not the same". A caveat: this candidate is furthest from your "almost identical"; it is about the picture against the thing.

**I recommend the first.** It holds your reading most exactly: "almost identical" becomes the punchline. And it adds one turn: sameness is not the failure of the dream but the fulfilled promise of the other system. Its weakness is that it is close to a familiar joke, and this very turn has to pull it out. If the least stock one is needed — the third.

Do we take the first or another?

## Researcher: Do the variant "What if we can it?" — it corresponds exactly to the reading "sausage emigration". It should be a cartoon, but so that it is clear there is no point in preserving manna (the absurdity of the idea of canning manna)

**The idea.** The habit of scarcity outlives the miracle. The people have just come out of Egypt — this is emigration in its pure form. They are promised bread every morning, and the first practical thought is how to store it. The woman is neither stupid nor greedy: she solves the problem her former life set her, and solves it skilfully.

What is for what:
- **The white layer of manna to the horizon.** The absurdity is visible without words: what lies everywhere and will appear again in the morning is being canned.
- **A three-litre jar and a can-sealing tool.** This is the habit brought along. Manna in a jar looks like semolina (*manka* in Russian): a miracle canned in glass becomes groats. This is for those who notice.
- **A checked bag with two jars already sealed.** It stands for emigration and shows that the woman is not asking but informing.
- **A hand on the tool's handle, stopped at half a turn, and the other hand raised.** This is a question "from the floor" at a meeting.
- **Moses looks at the jar.** His gesture is stopped halfway.

What remains open is Moses's answer and who is right. Exodus says the manna will spoil; the twentieth century says canned food keeps for years.

What must not appear: haloes, rays from the sky, the tablets, anger on faces and any ethnic caricature. Separately: "sausage emigration" was in its time often used to reproach precisely Jewish emigrants, and the frame of the Exodus makes this link literal. So the target is the Soviet habit of stocking up, common to everyone who lived under shortage. The woman is drawn with sympathy, and everyone's face is as in a Bible engraving, without caricature.

**Mode and form.** This is a self-standing cartoon: one panel with a two-line caption, designed as a page of an illustrated Bible. A second panel is not needed: the stopped gesture of Moses carries the pause of understanding.

| Caption line | Who | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| «— Манна будет каждое утро. Никто не оставляй её до утра.» ("Manna will come every morning. Let no man leave of it till the morning.") | Moses | Names the manna and gives both conditions: every morning, and not to keep it. «Никто не оставляй» is the register of the Russian Synodal Bible. |
| «— А если закатать?» ("What if we can it?") | The woman | The punchline word for word. A kitchen register against the biblical one; the question is asked when two jars are already sealed. |

**Visual language.** The tradition is the wood engravings of the large family Bibles of the 1860s–80s, the era of the Doré Bible. This voice tells sacred history with complete seriousness and cuts every object with equal reverence. Inside it the can-sealing tool becomes an object of sacred history, and the joke is told with a straight face.

Layers:
- one black ink on warm paper, tone only by line;
- the manna is the untouched white of the paper;
- naturalistic proportions without caricature;
- a high horizon, so that the white field becomes the largest shape;
- the caption in a book roman under the frame.

The categorical contrast here is not in colour but in objects: the jar and the bag are engraved with the same line as the staff and the hills. The manner asserts "this is Scripture" and works as the straight man in a comic duo. I don't name Doré to the generator: the name pulls in stormy skies, rays and a signature in the corner, so I describe the tradition in layers.

Ancestors of the device: Terry Gilliam's animation for *Monty Python*, assembled from Victorian engravings, and *Life of Brian* with its everyday pettiness in a biblical crowd. A question to God about a household trifle came from the Jewish joke.

The picture is the one of test 87.*
