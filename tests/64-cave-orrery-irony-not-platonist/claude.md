# The attached image presents an ironic interpretation of Plato metaphor of the Cave. Note that the image format itself - the use of classical motifs within a new context - is a postmodernist technique (which in itself may imply irony). To which philosophical system (major system of thought etc.) do you think the artist belongs? And how, then, might the image be explained? This is irony about Plato's concept, so author is not neoplatonist. 

The picture is a remake of Joseph Wright of Derby's 1766 painting, whose full title is *A Philosopher giving that Lecture on the Orrery, in which a lamp is put in place of the Sun*. That title is half the joke. Wright's painting was an icon of the Enlightenment: science as a new revelation, the faces lit as if around a manger. The remake keeps the composition and the devotional glow but changes three things. The audience is now a corporate team with identical, blank ID badges. The room has closed blinds. And a shaft of real daylight cuts through them while every person in the room keeps staring at the lamp.

**My answer: Nietzsche.** More precisely, the anti-Platonist line he started. In an early notebook he called his philosophy "inverted Platonism." Postmodernism later took this up as its explicit program; Deleuze named "overturning Platonism" as the task of modern philosophy. So the postmodern form and the Nietzschean content belong together.

**Why the image mocks Plato rather than serving him**

A Platonist could easily paint "moderns stuck staring at images." What makes this anti-Platonic is that these people are doing exactly what Plato prescribed. In *Republic* VII (529–530), Socrates tells Glaucon that real astronomy is pursued like geometry, through problems and rational models, and that we should leave the visible heavens alone.

The group is following that curriculum. They have turned from shadows to the fire and the artifacts; the little planets held up on rods above a rim even resemble the figures carried above the wall in the cave. They have a philosopher guiding them, and they are studying the intelligible structure of the cosmos rather than gaping at the sky. By Plato's standards, they are on the way up.

The image puts them in the cave anyway. The "sun" of Platonic reason turns out to be a lamp that a philosopher has set in its place. The philosopher, who in Plato returns to free the prisoners, is here the one tending the fire. Meanwhile the sensible, this-worldly sun, which Plato ranked lowest, streams in through the blinds. The hierarchy is reversed: the intelligible model is the cave, and the despised sensory world is the exit.

**Science as Plato's heir**

Nietzsche argued that modern science did not escape Platonism but inherited it. In *The Gay Science* §344 he writes that even we godless anti-metaphysicians still take our fire from the flame lit by an ancient faith, the faith of Plato that truth is divine. The image makes that metaphor literal: the "knowers" of today are gathered around a flame. Wright's painting already borrowed sacred lighting for science. The remake shows the ritual is still running.

There is also §108: God is dead, but there may still be caves for thousands of years in which his shadow is shown. This boardroom is one of those caves.

**The modern setting**

The corporate update isn't decoration; it shows where the Enlightenment's lamp ended up. The people fit Nietzsche's "last men" in the *Zarathustra* prologue. They have left the places where life was hard because one needs warmth, and everyone wants the same and is the same. Hence the identical blank badges and the rehearsed "thinker" poses.

Look at the color: the model is warm and golden, while the real light is cold, thin and bluish. The comfortable simulation is chosen over the harsh real thing. Zarathustra is the counter-image. He also lived in a cave, but he walks out of it and speaks to the sun. Nobody here even turns toward the window.

There is one more Nietzschean nuance. In *Twilight of the Idols* ("How the 'True World' Finally Became a Fable"), the last stage abolishes the apparent world along with the true one: noon, the moment of the shortest shadow. So the beam is not a new Form of the Good waiting to be worshipped. It is plain daylight, the world as it is, which is why it looks so ordinary and slightly glaring.

**Alternatives, and why I'd still pick Nietzsche**

Baudrillard is the obvious postmodern candidate: the model precedes the territory, and the real survives only as a "desert." But a Baudrillardian image would have no window, or the window would be another screen. Here the real outside exists, and the composition insists that it matters. That is critique, not resigned hyperreality.

Heidegger's essay on Plato's doctrine of truth is closer. He reads the cave as the moment truth became the correct viewing of a model, and the orrery is literally a "world picture." Adorno and Horkheimer could read it as enlightenment reverting to myth. All three readings descend from Nietzsche, and his covers the most details: the fire, the shadow-cult, the warmth-seeking herd, and the unglamorous sun.

**The final irony**

You and I are also looking at a picture of a sunbeam rather than at the sun. The image is itself a lamp put in place of the Sun. That self-implicating turn is the postmodern irony you noticed in the form, and it keeps the artist from posing as the one who has escaped.

# Can you explain why you only analyze Western schools of philosophy?

Partly because the image steered me that way, and partly because I defaulted without checking.

The defensible part is that the image is built from Western materials: Plato's cave, an Enlightenment painting, a modern corporate boardroom. "Irony about Plato" is most naturally a move inside the Western tradition, because only a tradition that took Plato as its foundation has much stake in overturning him. Anti-Platonism is a family quarrel. Your own framing in terms of postmodernism and Neoplatonism pointed the same way.

The less defensible part is that I never asked whether a stronger reading might come from elsewhere. Academic philosophy, and the material I learned from, often treats "philosophy" as meaning the Western canon, and I followed that habit. But your question doesn't require the artist's standpoint to share the image's materials. A Daoist or Buddhist could use Plato and Wright as targets just as Nietzsche borrowed Zoroaster. When I look, two traditions fit well.

**Daoism (Zhuangzi)** has its own version of the cave: the frog in the well, from the "Autumn Floods" chapter. The frog boasts to the great turtle of the Eastern Sea about how complete its little world is, and the turtle describes the ocean. The difference from Plato is telling. Nobody is chained. The frog is proud and content, which is exactly the mood of that boardroom.

The *Daodejing* opens by saying that a Dao which can be spoken is not the constant Dao, and the orrery is a Dao rendered in brass. Zhuangzi adds that the fish trap exists for the fish and can be forgotten once the fish is caught, just as words exist for meaning. These people have mistaken the trap for the fish.

A Daoist irony against Plato would also differ from Nietzsche's. It wouldn't champion the sensory world against the intelligible one. It would distrust the whole scene of a master handing disciples the structure of the cosmos, the kind of solemn instruction Zhuangzi loved to parody.

**Buddhism** offers two angles. The Buddha declined to answer cosmological questions, such as whether the world is eternal or infinite, because they don't lead to liberation. In the arrow parable (Majjhima Nikaya 63), a man shot with a poisoned arrow refuses treatment until he learns who shot it and what the bow was made of, and he dies first. A room absorbed in a model of the cosmos, missing the light actually present, is close to that scene.

Chan/Zen sharpens it with the finger pointing at the moon: confuse the teaching with what it points at and you lose both. The lecturer's outstretched hand is literally pointing at the model while the light enters from somewhere he isn't pointing. Read this way, the beam is sudden awakening, which arrives uninvited and is missed precisely by the most studious seekers.

Comparing these with Nietzsche, I'd say they explain the image's basic shape as well as he does, and the beam perhaps better. What they explain less directly are its specific targets: Wright's Enlightenment painting, the corporate update, the flame as Plato's faith in truth carried into modern science. Those details suggest a critique made from inside the West, so I'd still put Nietzsche first for this artist. Zhuangzi is a serious second, and the image is richer for having both readings.

The line between these readings is also less sharp than it looks. Heidegger worked on translating the *Daodejing* with Paul Shih-yi Hsiao in 1946. Nishitani Keiji of the Kyoto School wrote *The Self-Overcoming of Nihilism*, connecting Nietzsche's critique of the "true world" with Buddhist emptiness. Some of the strongest anti-Platonist thinking of the last century came from exactly that meeting.


# Suppose this satire takes aim at both Plato and Advaita. To which school, then, does the author belong? And how, in that case, should the depiction be interpreted?

Then I'd place the author in **Madhyamaka Buddhism**, the school of Nāgārjuna.

**Why that school**

Plato and Advaita share four commitments, and a satirist who targets both has to reject all four:

1. One eternal reality beyond the changing world: the Good for Plato, Brahman for Advaita.
2. A demotion of the world we live in: shadows in one case, māyā in the other.
3. Liberation through knowledge handed from teacher to student.
4. Light as the image of the absolute. The Kaṭha and Muṇḍaka Upaniṣads say of Brahman that there the sun does not shine, nor moon nor stars, and that by its light all this shines.

Buddhism rejects all four, and it was historically the main opponent of the ātman–Brahman doctrine. The quarrel is even a family one. Advaita absorbed so much Buddhist method, such as Gauḍapāda's doctrine of non-origination and the two-level theory of truth, that rival Vedāntins called Śaṅkara's followers crypto-Buddhists.

Madhyamaka in particular fits because it doesn't replace one absolute with another, and neither does the picture.

**The room stages the Upaniṣadic verse literally**

In this room the sun does not shine, and everything is lit by one central light. Then the satirist adds a sunbeam. The "light of lights" is a lamp on a table, and the sun that supposedly doesn't shine "there" is coming through the blinds.

**The flame**

For Plato this is the cave's fire. For Advaita it recalls the theater lamp of the *Pañcadaśī*: a lamp on the stage that lights the patron, the dancers and the audience equally and keeps shining after they leave. It is the classic image of unchanging witness-consciousness, and the lamp in the picture lights every face equally.

A Buddhist sees a flame differently: as a process, never the same from one moment to the next, burning on fuel. The Pali word for fuel, *upādāna*, is also the word for clinging. One of the four kinds of clinging listed in the suttas is clinging to doctrines of a self. The group's rapt attention is the fuel; their absolute exists because they keep feeding it. *Nirvāṇa* literally means that fire going out.

**The lesson in progress**

Advaita's path has three stages: hearing the teaching (*śravaṇa*), reflecting on it (*manana*), and meditative absorption (*nididhyāsana*). The picture shows all three at once. The teacher speaks, several listeners hold thinker poses, others gaze in absorption, and one takes notes. Plato's philosopher leading the ascent is the same scene. The satire is that the curriculum itself is the room.

**The blinds**

In Advaita, māyā has two powers: concealing (*āvaraṇa*) and projecting (*vikṣepa*). Here the blinds conceal and the orrery projects, and behind the veil Brahman is supposed to wait.

Madhyamaka's word for conventional truth, *saṃvṛti*, also literally means "covering." But for Nāgārjuna nothing is hidden behind it. Ultimate truth is just the emptiness of the conventional, and he insists there is not the slightest difference between saṃsāra and nirvāṇa.

So the beam doesn't reveal a hidden absolute. It is ordinary daylight already lying across the room. That cuts against Plato's climb outward and against Advaita's turn inward alike: nobody needs to leave or dive into the Self, only to notice what is already falling across the table.

**The beam**

The beam is a small picture of dependent origination. It exists only where the sun, a gap in the slats and the right angle come together, and it is visible only because of dust in the air. Remove any condition and the beam is gone.

Its color matters too. Advaita calls Brahman bliss (*ānanda*), and the absolute in this room is the warm, golden, cozy part. The real is cool and plain.

**The badges**

Advaita teaches that individual selves are one consciousness divided by limiting adjuncts, like the space inside separate pots. The identical badges parody that unity as corporate sameness.

The Madhyamaka joke goes further. In Buddhism a self is only a designation (*prajñapti*) with nothing behind it: a name tag with no name on it. The answer these people are looking for in the flame is hanging around their necks.

**Other candidates**

Nietzsche still qualifies, and he actually grouped the two together. His friend Paul Deussen, the great Vedānta scholar, argued that Śaṅkara, Plato and Kant taught the same truth. In the *Genealogy of Morals* (III, §17), Nietzsche quotes through Deussen the Vedāntic passage about the soul in deep sleep entering "the highest light," as an example of the ascetic ideal.

The Cārvāka materialists would also mock both, but bluntly: the guru as a huckster, matter as the only reality. They would make the sun a new foundation. The picture doesn't. Its real is thin, contingent and unnoticed, and that restraint points to Nāgārjuna.
