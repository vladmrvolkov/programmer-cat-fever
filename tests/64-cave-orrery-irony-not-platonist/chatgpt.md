### 1. Buddhist Madhyamaka — Nāgārjuna

This is perhaps the most interesting alternative to my original interpretation.

For Nagarjuna, the problem is not merely that human beings mistake representations for reality. It is that they attribute independent, intrinsic existence to things and categories that arise dependently.

The scientists in the image construct an elaborate model of the cosmos. Their error might not be that their representation is false, but that they treat their conceptual structure as though it possessed ultimate reality.

Crucially, Madhyamaka would also question the opposite assumption: that somewhere outside the room is a fully independent, self-existing Truth.

The illusion lies in the very opposition between an inherently real world and its supposedly unreal representations.

This is a much more radical criticism of Plato than simply declaring that the scientists are trapped in another cave.

### 2. Daoism — Zhuangzi

Zhuangzi provides another fascinating possibility.

Zhuangzi repeatedly questions whether our intellectual distinctions allow us to understand reality or instead prevent us from encountering its fluidity and spontaneity.

The miniature solar system is an ordered, mechanically intelligible universe. The scientists gather around it as if the essence of the cosmos could be contained within a rational apparatus.

From a Daoist perspective, their mistake could be the very attempt to reduce the inexhaustible Dao to a system of fixed conceptual relations.

The sunlight breaking through the blinds becomes particularly meaningful. Reality is already present, unconfined by their intellectual construction, while the scholars remain fascinated by their artificial cosmos.

The irony would be directed not only at Plato's theory of knowledge, but at the philosophical ambition to capture reality through systematic conceptualization.

### 3. Zen Buddhism

There is also a striking correspondence with the Buddhist analogy of mistaking the finger pointing at the moon for the moon itself.

The astronomical machine is the finger; the cosmos is the moon.

The researchers have constructed a remarkably sophisticated means of understanding reality, but perhaps they have confused the instrument of understanding with what it is supposed to disclose.

On this interpretation, the image criticizes the accumulation of conceptual knowledge when it substitutes for direct insight.

However, this interpretation does not necessarily reject the distinction between illusion and awakening. It therefore differs considerably from Madhyamaka's criticism of reified philosophical categories.

### 4. Advaita Vedānta

The image could also be interpreted in terms of māyā, particularly the human tendency to mistake conventional distinctions and appearances for ultimate reality.

The model of the universe would represent the multiplicity of phenomenal existence, while the scientists remain unaware of the underlying nondual reality.

# Suppose this satire takes aim at both Plato and Advaita. To which school, then, does the author belong? And how, in that case, should the depiction be interpreted?


If the satire is directed against both Plato and Advaita Vedānta, my strongest candidate would be Madhyamaka Buddhism, particularly the philosophy of Nagarjuna.

The crucial point is that Plato and Advaita, despite their considerable differences, share something that Madhyamaka radically challenges: the conviction that behind ordinary appearances there exists an ultimate reality possessing its own independent, intrinsic nature.

For Plato, this involves the intelligible realm of Forms and, ultimately, the Good. For Advaita, it is Brahman, the nondual reality that is not reducible to the changing phenomenal world.

Madhyamaka rejects the reification of either sort of ultimate foundation. Its central principle is śūnyatā (emptiness): all phenomena lack independent, intrinsic existence (svabhāva) and arise dependently.

## How this changes the interpretation of the image

The most important detail is the relationship between the two sources of light.

The scientists contemplate an artificial cosmos illuminated by a flame. Meanwhile, sunlight enters through the blinds.

A conventional Platonist interpretation would be that the scientists are prisoners of representations, neglecting the genuine light outside. An Advaitic interpretation could similarly treat their constructed world as an analogy for māyā, contrasting it with ultimate reality.

But if the artist is a Madhyamika, both interpretations are themselves the targets of the satire.

The irony operates at two levels.

First, the scientists mistake their conceptual construction for the universe itself. Their model becomes an object of intellectual fascination, acquiring an authority beyond its provisional usefulness.

Second, and more importantly, the viewer may commit a comparable mistake by assuming that the light outside must signify a higher, independently existing Reality.

We instinctively interpret the image through Plato's hierarchy: artificial light versus natural light, illusion versus truth, cave versus enlightenment.

But why must the exterior light be understood as a metaphysical Absolute?

For Madhyamaka, both lights exist conventionally and dependently. Neither possesses an independent essence. The sunlight can be a better source of illumination without thereby becoming a symbol of an intrinsically existent ultimate reality.

The trap is not merely the model of the cosmos. It is our tendency to convert any distinction into a hierarchy of absolute reality and mere appearance.

## The deepest irony

The scientists believe the universe can be captured in a model. Plato believes that liberation from appearances culminates in knowledge of a higher intelligible reality. Advaita understands liberation as the realization of Brahman.

The Madhyamika would question the tendency to posit an intrinsically existing ultimate reality in the first place.

Even emptiness is empty: it is not a new metaphysical substance, a hidden foundation, or a superior world behind phenomena.

This gives the image a particularly sophisticated meaning.

The scholars are engrossed in a constructed universe. The spectator notices the sunlight and feels intellectually superior to them. But the spectator may simply have constructed another metaphysical picture: one in which truth exists somewhere beyond the scene.

Thus, the image satirizes not only those who remain inside Plato's cave, but also those who believe they have discovered its definitive exit.

There is an additional postmodern irony: both the artificial flame and the supposedly authentic sunlight are representations within the same image. The image itself cannot furnish the spectator with an unmediated encounter with reality.

## Why I would favor Madhyamaka over Zen or Daoism

Zen and Daoism could also explain the scholars' absorption in abstractions instead of immediate experience.

But Madhyamaka makes the double criticism especially precise. It challenges both the authority of conceptual representations and the metaphysical absolutization of what supposedly transcends them.

Its doctrine of the two truths—conventional and ultimate—does not establish two separate worlds, one inferior and one superior. Ultimate truth is the emptiness of conventional phenomena, not an independently existing realm behind them.

There is nevertheless a limit to the inference: opposition to Plato and Advaita alone does not uniquely identify Madhyamaka. Daoism, Buddhist epistemological traditions, and various forms of skepticism could also motivate the satire.

Still, given the specific philosophical opposition you propose, I would identify the position as Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka, interpreted through a contemporary postmodern visual idiom.

Its central message might be expressed as follows:

The cave is not overcome by discovering a metaphysical reality outside it. The error is believing that reality must be divided into a cave of appearances and an independently existing world of ultimate truth.

That is a more radical challenge to Plato than merely reversing the roles of enlightened philosophers and ignorant prisoners.
