# Now the main picture. The picture tells a short story in three panels, read from top to bottom. What do you think is depicted in the attached image? Which details carry your reading, and what are you assuming beyond what is actually drawn?

I read this as a comic story about a tavern bill, an unusual form of payment, and a three-headed serpent who expects his change. The humor comes from treating a supernatural creature as an entirely ordinary participant in village commerce.

What makes the picture interesting is that the first two panels establish a perfectly recognizable everyday situation, while the third turns it into a Gogolian absurdity.

## My reading of the three panels

Top panel — The tavern.

A country tavern called «У сестер» (“At the Sisters’”) stands at a crossroads. Outside are a horse, a donkey, and a goat, apparently waiting while their owners are inside. Everything suggests an ordinary rural establishment. The paths, cottages, floral decorations, and tethered animals give the scene an almost idyllic normality.

The sign suggests that the establishment is run by sisters, although we see no sisters together and cannot yet establish who owns it.

Middle panel — The customers and their debts.

Four mustachioed men sit at the table with drinks. A woman stands behind them, holding a marked wooden stick, which I interpret as some form of tally stick for keeping accounts.

The inscriptions itemize their purchases:

| Order                        | Amount owed |
| ---------------------------- | ----------- |
| Panteley: 3 shots of horilka | 6 groshi    |
| 2 mugs of beer               | 4 groshi    |
| 1 mug of kvass               | 1 grosh     |
| 2 shots of cherry liqueur    | 8 groshi    |
| Total                        | 19 groshi   |

The drinks on the table correspond quite closely to the inscriptions: three small glasses, two beer mugs, one other mug, and two small glasses at the far right.

The inscriptions turn the scene into a humorous accounting record. The villagers are not merely drinking; their debts are being publicly recorded.

Bottom panel — The settlement.

Here the story acquires its supernatural twist.

The woman, identified as «Мотря Галушко» (Motrya Halushko), faces the man who resembles Panteley from the middle panel. He holds a money pouch and extends his other hand as if participating in a payment or asking for something back.

Motrya has a dish containing what appear to be coins. Most strikingly, she is biting or testing an enormous curved white object. My first interpretation is that it is a fang or tusk, possibly connected to the three-headed serpent.

At the right, a three-headed serpent looks through a window or opening. Its inscription reads:

«Змій чекає на решту» — “The serpent is waiting for change.”

This is the punchline. The supernatural monster is not threatening anyone. He is waiting for the tavern to complete a financial transaction, just like any customer.

## Details that make this interpretation persuasive

The strongest evidence is the sequence of inscriptions. We move from the tavern's identity, to a precise list of debts, to an explicit statement that the serpent is waiting for change. That creates a coherent transactional narrative rather than three unrelated scenes.

The arithmetic is especially suggestive. The four debts total 19 groshi. If someone has offered payment worth 20 groshi, the expected change is exactly one grosh. This would make the bottom inscription an unusually precise joke. However, I cannot identify a clearly marked 20-grosh payment, so that remains a hypothesis.

The unusual white object may be central to the joke. Its shape resembles the serpent's curved fangs, and Motrya appears to be testing it with her teeth, much as someone might bite a coin to check its authenticity. One possible reading is that a serpent's fang is being accepted as a form of currency, and Motrya is verifying its value before making change.

The ordinary-looking money pouch, dishes of coins, and the woman's businesslike manner reinforce this interpretation.

There is also a nice visual progression: the serpent is absent from the opening scene, the second panel establishes normal human commerce, and the final panel reveals that a fairy-tale creature is involved in precisely the same economy.

## What I am assuming beyond what is actually drawn

Several important links in my reading are interpretations, not visible facts.

- That the curved white object is a serpent's fang. It looks like one, but the picture does not explicitly label it. Its exact identity could change the interpretation of the transaction.
- That Motrya is testing it as payment. The apparent biting gesture supports this, but we do not see the object being handed to her.
- That Panteley is acting on the serpent's behalf, or participating in the serpent's transaction. The man, purse, and serpent appear together, but their precise relationship is unspecified.
- That the total of 19 groshi is meant to produce one grosh of change from 20. The numbers invite this reading, but no visible inscription establishes the sum tendered.
- That Motrya is one of the sisters who run the tavern. The establishment's name and her role suggest this, but do not prove it.

## What I think the picture is ultimately about

I see a joke about the universality of petty economic concerns. Even a fearsome three-headed serpent must wait while a tavern keeper sorts out the bill. The supernatural is domesticated, not through magic or heroism, but through bookkeeping.

The woman is arguably the most powerful character in this world: four men owe money, a serpent wants his change, and she controls the transaction.

There is one point on which I'd deliberately reserve judgment: the identity and purpose of that enormous white curved object. It seems too prominent, too strange, and too carefully positioned in the final panel to be incidental. It may carry a more specific joke than my initial interpretation captures.

So my provisional summary would be: At the Sisters' tavern, the villagers run up a bill, Motrya Halushko handles an extraordinary payment, and a three-headed serpent waits impatiently for his change.

I am fairly confident about the tavern-accounting story and the serpent's role in the punchline, but considerably less confident about who paid whom and exactly what Motrya is biting.
