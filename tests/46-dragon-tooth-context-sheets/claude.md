# Before the main picture, some context -  the main picture itself comes in the next message. This is a contemporary works in the tradition of Ukrainian naive painting and painted tavern signboards. In this tradition figures are flat and distorted on purpose, the inscriptions are part of the story, and fairy-tale creatures live side by side with village life, as in Gogol's "Evenings on a Farm Near Dikanka". In this world the tavern serves both people and fairy-tale creatures; they are ordinary customers. In this tradition an odd-looking object is more likely to be a joke than a mistake.

Got it, and thanks for the framing. It helps to know the conventions before reading the main piece.

The triptych works like a little comic strip built on proverbs. In the first panel, "Удень людям, уночі чортам" (by day for people, by night for devils), the mill-tavern keeper takes coins at the door while peasants and devils walk the same plank in a queue, each carrying a sack to be milled, as if this were the most normal thing in the world. In the second, "Чорти платять наперед" (devils pay in advance), the devils have traditional folk names, Антипко, Куць and Безп'ятко, and drink and play cards with the villagers. The third, "Доґрався до сорочки" (gambled himself down to his shirt), flips the usual folk tale where a man loses his soul to the devil. Here Антипко is the one stripped barefoot to his shirt, his trousers and sash piled on the bench as winnings, and the keeper is chalking up "Антипко винен гостям 3 золотих" (Antypko owes the guests 3 zloty). So he paid in advance and still ended up in debt.

The bottom-left piece riffs on the Cossack Mamai composition: he sits cross-legged under a tree with his sabre, powder horn and flask hanging from the branches. But a three-headed serpent stands where his horse usually would, and instead of a kobza he holds an open purse and an outstretched palm. He could be presenting the bill or showing his purse is empty, and I suspect the ambiguity is part of the joke.

The "ВІЙ" board retells Gogol: the three seminarians on the road, the witch riding Khoma, Khoma reading inside the chalk circle, and Viy with his eyelids lifted while the rooster waits in the window. The center panel is the epilogue in the Kyiv tavern, with Khaliava's "Славний був чоловік Хома!" and Gorobets's "Загинув, бо злякався." I also like the old boot sole lying on the bench. In Gogol's ending, Khaliava pockets just such a sole out of habit, so that's a nice detail for anyone who knows the story.

# Now the main picture. The picture tells a short story in three panels, read from top to bottom. What do you think is depicted in the attached image? Which details carry your reading, and what are you assuming beyond what is actually drawn?


I read it as a story about settling a bar tab. The punchline: a three-headed serpent has paid with one of its fangs, and now the tavern owes *it* money.

**Top panel** sets the scene. A roadside tavern, "У сестер" ("At the Sisters'"), stands where two roads meet. At the hitching rails are a saddled horse, a donkey under a saddle-cloth, and a goat tied to the donkey and loaded with wicker panniers like a pack mule. Customers have arrived. The serpent isn't among the animals.

**Middle panel** is the tab. The hostess holds a notched tally stick (бирка), the old tavern way of recording debts, and each drinker's bill is written above his head. The table matches the text exactly: three shots in front of Panteley (owes 6), two beer mugs (owes 4), one kvas (owes 1), two cups of вишнівка (owes 8). The prices are consistent: 2 groshi for horilka or beer, 1 for kvas, 4 for cherry liqueur, 19 in total. Every line says "винен" (owes), so nobody has paid yet. Only Panteley is named, which marks him as the hero. The bowl of dumplings and the bread aren't on anyone's bill, and with a hostess named Галушко, I suspect the галушки are a small joke.

**Bottom panel** is the reckoning. Panteley, recognizable by the same yellow coat, blue zhupan and red sash, holds his purse open with his palm out. It's exactly the gesture of the Mamai figure in your context picture, who sat beside this same serpent. Motria Halushko bites a big curved fang the way you'd bite a gold coin to test it. Her other hand is in a bowl of coins, counting out change, and she's glancing sideways at Panteley. In the window the serpent rests a claw on the sill, and the caption explains the situation: "Змій чекає на решту," the serpent is waiting for its change. A dragon's tooth is a "coin" far too large for a few groshi.

The details that carry this reading are the bite-test gesture, the coin bowl under her hand, the caption, the itemized debts that frame everything as a tab, and the matching costume that links Panteley across the panels and back to the Mamai painting.

Here is what I'm assuming beyond what's drawn.

**That the white object is the serpent's fang.** Its shape and the serpent next to it argue for this. When I zoomed in, the left head shows only one large fang while the other two heads each show a pair. That would be a lovely confirming detail, but the left head is in profile, so the second fang could simply be hidden. It's suggestive, not proof.

**Whose bill the fang paid.** Nothing drawn says. My guess, borrowed from the context painting, is that the serpent is Panteley's companion and is covering his 6 groshi, or possibly the whole table's 19. Panteley's gesture cuts both ways. An open purse and empty palm can mean "I've got nothing," or it can mean "put the change here." If it's the second, there may be a further joke: he plans to pocket the serpent's change.

**Whether the hostess and Motria are the same woman.** Their clothes are identical, but the sign says "sisters" in the plural. Only the second woman is named, and her face is heavier and sterner. It could be one sister keeping the tally and the other taking the money.

**Who owns the animals.** I assume the horse is Panteley's, as a Cossack's horse would be, and that the donkey and goat belong to the other drinkers. Four drinkers, three mounts, and no serpent at the rail, which suggests it flew in. My guess is it's at the window because it wouldn't fit through the door.

**The sequence.** Arrival, then drinking on credit, then settling up is the natural top-to-bottom reading, but it isn't stated anywhere. The ending is also left open. "Чекає" invites the thought that the serpent has been waiting a while, and that Motria, still testing the fang and eyeing Panteley, is in no hurry to hand anything back.
