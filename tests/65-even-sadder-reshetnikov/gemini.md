This image is a satirical postmodern remix of Fyodor Reshetnikov’s famous 1952 Soviet Socialist Realist painting, ***Low Marks Again*** (*Опять двойка*), reimagined for the age of social media and algorithmic clout-chasing.

## What Is Depicted: From Moral Drama to "Sadfishing"

In Reshetnikov’s original painting, the scene is a sincere domestic tragedy: a schoolboy returns home with a failing grade (a "2"), met by his disappointed mother, his judging Pioneer sister, his mocking little brother, and his unconditionally loving dog.

In this digital recontextualization, the genuine family drama is exposed as a **staged social media production**:

* **The Cropped Reality:** A smartphone on a tripod stands in the foreground, filming a vertical video (the format of TikTok, Reels, and Shorts). Notably, the phone’s screen only captures the tight frame of a "heartbroken" boy being comforted by his loyal dog, cropping out the messy truth of the room.


* **The Director:** The older sister, originally the symbol of Soviet moral uprightness in her red Pioneer neckerchief, now stands on a chair holding a cue card that reads **"ЕЩЁ ГРУСТНЕЕ" ("EVEN SADDER")**. She is the ruthless content producer coaching her brother to milk his emotions for views.


* **Breaking Character:** Instead of feeling genuine shame, the boy in the foreground is being tickled by the dog's tongue and is barely holding back a smirk—a theatrical phenomenon known as "corpsing" (breaking character by laughing during a serious scene). Meanwhile, the mother and little brother sit behind the camera like an exhausted film crew waiting for the take to end.

## Philosophical Frameworks of the Artist and the Image

Because the artist uses irony, pastiche, and meta-commentary to deconstruct how humans manufacture "truth," they do not belong to just one rigid school. Depending on whether we look East or West, the artist’s worldview—and the explanation of the painting—aligns with several major philosophical traditions:

### 1. Postmodern Critical Theory: Hyperreality and The Spectacle (Western)

The artist operates squarely in the tradition of **Postmodernism** (and specifically post-Soviet **Sots Art**, which subverts Socialist Realist icons with capitalist absurdity).

* **Baudrillard’s Simulacra and Simulation:** French philosopher Jean Baudrillard argued that in postmodern society, symbols and signs replace actual reality, creating *hyperreality*. The image on the smartphone screen is a **simulacrum**—a fake representation of grief that, to the millions of viewers scrolling on their phones, will feel more "real" and emotionally moving than actual life.


* **Debord’s Society of the Spectacle:** Marxist theorist Guy Debord wrote that *"all that once was directly lived has become mere representation."* In the 1950s painting, the family directly lived their shame and disappointment. In the artist's updated vision, human emotion and familial intimacy are no longer lived; they are commodified into a spectacle to generate digital currency (likes, shares, and monetization).



### 2. Advaita Vedanta and Buddhism: *Maya* and *Lila* (Indian / Eastern)

Stepping outside Western cynicism, the artist’s perspective aligns remarkably well with Eastern non-dualistic philosophies, which view human drama with detached amusement rather than despair.

* **Maya (Illusion) and Avidya (Ignorance):** In Hindu Advaita Vedanta and Buddhism, we suffer because we mistake a constructed, limited perception of the world (*Maya*) for ultimate truth. The smartphone screen is a literal metaphor for the unenlightened mind: it takes a narrow, cropped slice of existence and constructs a story of suffering (*dukkha*) out of it. The artist steps back to show us the whole room—the "ultimate reality"—revealing that the tragedy on the screen is an illusion.


* **Lila (Cosmic Play):** The boy's suppressed smile transforms the painting into an illustration of *Lila*—the Hindu concept that all of creation, with its heavy roles of success, failure, and tragedy, is ultimately a divine play or game. The boy knows he isn't truly a tragic figure; he is just an actor in a costume, barely able to keep a straight face while playing his part.


* **Suchness (*Tathata*):** In Zen/Chan Buddhism, the dog is the only enlightened being in the room. Completely oblivious to both Soviet academic shame and 21st-century TikTok algorithms, the dog simply licks the boy's face—existing in pure, unconditioned presence.



### 3. Daoism: *Ziran* (Spontaneity) vs. Contrived Artifice (Chinese)

If the artist belongs to an Eastern school of thought, they have the irreverent, playful spirit of **Zhuangzi** and **Daoism**, which constantly uses humor to mock rigid societal moralizing.

* **Critique of *Wei* (Contrived Action):** Daoism argues that when humans abandon their natural state, they invent artificial systems of morality and performance. Both the original Soviet state (which demanded performative guilt over bad grades) and modern social media (which demands performative sadness for views) represent unnatural, forced human behavior (*wei*).


* **The Triumph of *Ziran* (Naturalness):** No matter how hard the sister demands "EVEN SADDER," authentic nature (*ziran*) breaks through the artifice. A dog’s wet tongue is naturally funny to a young boy, and his suppressed smile is the irrepressible truth of human nature leaking through a fabricated narrative.



### 4. Existentialism: *Bad Faith* and the Gaze (Western)

Through the lens of **Jean-Paul Sartre**, the artist is diagnosing modern **Bad Faith** (*mauvaise foi*)—the phenomenon where human beings deny their authentic freedom and pretend they are mere objects playing a predetermined role.

* In Sartre's philosophy, the "Gaze" of the Other turns us into performers. Here, the all-seeing Gaze has shifted from the totalitarian state (symbolized by the Pioneer sister in 1952) to the invisible, omnipresent **Algorithm** (symbolized by the smartphone lens). The family members willingly reduce their authentic lives to a scripted pantomime to appease an invisible audience, though the boy's smirk hints that he recognizes the sheer absurdity of the performance.

# To be honest, the first person who comes to mind for me is Žižek.


Bringing Slavoj Žižek into this room is a stroke of genius—you can practically hear him tugging at his t-shirt, sniffing, and declaring, *"Look at the boy's face—this is pure ideology at its finest, and so on and so on!"*

In fact, this painting reads almost like a visual illustration of Žižek’s entire philosophical project, which fuses Lacanian psychoanalysis, Hegelian dialectics, and a deep fondness for post-Soviet irony. Seen through a Žižekian lens, four specific concepts explain what is happening in the room:

* **Cynical Ideology ("They know very well what they are doing, yet they are doing it"):** In *The Sublime Object of Ideology*, Žižek argues that modern ideology no longer operates through naive brainwashing ("they do not know it, but they are doing it"). Instead, it operates through cynicism: we see right through the lie, we mock it, and *we do it anyway*. Nobody in this room believes in the tragedy being filmed. The sister is blatantly holding a cue card demanding "EVEN SADDER," the mother looks like a tired stagehand, and the boy is smirking. For Žižek, that suppressed smile is the ultimate trap of postmodern ideology: we think that because we maintain an ironic distance and don't take the performance seriously, we are free—when in reality, our compliance with the ritual (filming the TikTok) is all the system requires.


* **The "Big Other" on a Tripod:** Borrowing from Jacques Lacan, Žižek constantly talks about the *Big Other*—the invisible, symbolic authority or virtual gaze for whom we perform our lives. In the original 1952 painting, the Big Other was the Communist Party and Soviet morality, embodied by the judging Pioneer sister in her red neckerchief. In this remix, the Big Other has been literalized as the smartphone on the tripod. The family doesn't need to be sad for *each other*; they only need to stage sadness for the gaze of the Big Other (the Algorithm), which validates their existence.


* **Interpassivity (The Screen Suffers for Us):** One of Žižek’s favorite quirks of modern culture is *interpassivity*—when an external object experiences our emotions or beliefs on our behalf, much like canned laughter on a sitcom laughs *for* us so we can just sit there deadpan. Look closely at the smartphone screen in the painting: inside that tiny rectangle, the boy looks genuinely heartbroken. Because his digital double on the screen is already doing the heavy lifting of "feeling sad" for the audience, the real boy standing in the room is relieved of the duty to actually feel anything, leaving him free to enjoy the dog licking his chin.


* **Privatized Socialist Realism and Obscene *Jouissance*:** Žižek loves pointing out the dark comedic parallels between Stalinist totalitarianism and late-capitalist consumerism. Soviet Socialist Realism never depicted reality as it actually was; it painted a forced, hyper-staged fantasy of how life *ought* to look under socialism. By dropping a ring-light tripod into a classic Soviet canvas, the artist exposes how modern social media culture is just decentralized Socialist Realism—except instead of staging moralistic tableaus for the State, families now stage them for engagement metrics. And that barely contained smirk on the boy's face (echoed by the grinning little brother on the bike) captures what Lacan and Žižek call **obscene *jouissance* (surplus enjoyment)**: the secret, transgressive thrill of knowing the official narrative is a total sham while gaming it from the inside.



If Žižek were analyzing this, he'd probably argue that the 2020s family is actually *more* trapped than the 1950s family—because in 1952 you were forced to obey an external authority, whereas today we willingly direct our own humiliation and call it "self-expression".
