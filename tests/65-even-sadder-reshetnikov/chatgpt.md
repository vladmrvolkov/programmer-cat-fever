The image strikes me as a sophisticated philosophical joke, not merely a parody of a Soviet painting. I think its central subject is the difference between genuine experience and the performance of experience. And the detail you mention—the boy barely suppressing a smile—is crucial to understanding it.

The composition is an unmistakable reinterpretation of Again a Deuce (Опять двойка) by Fyodor Reshetnikov (1952). In the original, the boy has received a bad mark at school. His mother is disappointed, his sister reproachful, his younger brother amused, and only the dog welcomes him unconditionally.

In the new image, however, there is a smartphone on a tripod, and the older girl holds a sign reading «ЕЩЁ ГРУСТНЕЕ»—“Even sadder!” This sounds like a director's instruction to an actor.

Suddenly, the entire situation changes. What originally represented authentic shame, disappointment, and familial judgment has become a production in which everyone is playing a role. The boy's almost-smile gives the game away.

## What philosophical position does this suggest?

I would distinguish between the philosophy that best explains the mechanism of the image and the philosophy that best captures the artist's apparent attitude toward it.

### 1. Baudrillard: reality replaced by its representation

The most immediate connection is to Jean Baudrillard and his theory of simulacra.

The original painting was already a carefully composed representation, conveying a moral lesson about responsibility, failure, and familial expectations. Indeed, it belongs to the tradition of Soviet socialist realism.&#x20;

Now the representation is being reproduced as another representation, specifically one designed to be recorded and watched.

The girl is effectively directing the manufacture of sadness. What matters is no longer whether the boy is unhappy, but whether he looks sufficiently unhappy.

This is quite close to Baudrillard's understanding of a society in which images and signs increasingly organize experience itself.&#x20;

But there is an important complication: the boy knows that the situation is absurd. He can barely suppress his amusement. Consequently, the image doesn't simply depict people imprisoned within simulation. It also depicts someone who sees through it.

That makes me reluctant to stop at Baudrillard.

### 2. Daoism: the spontaneous smile against prescribed emotion

My more interesting candidate would be Daoism, particularly the philosophy of Zhuangzi.

Zhuangzi was famously skeptical of rigid social distinctions, prescribed moral judgments, and the assumption that conventional ways of behaving possess some ultimate authority. His philosophical parables frequently use humor to undermine excessive seriousness.&#x20;

Consider how the painting could work through this lens.

The boy is supposed to be ashamed. His mother is supposed to be disappointed. His sister is supposed to be morally indignant. Everyone has been assigned a role, and the sign makes the artificiality of these roles explicit.

Yet the dog is simply delighted to see the boy.

And the boy, despite the demand that he appear miserable, cannot quite stop himself from smiling.

These are the two most spontaneous expressions of emotion in the painting. Neither conforms to the social demand for sadness.

The Daoist interpretation would be that the family has constructed an elaborate system of judgments and expectations, mistaking those conventions for something necessarily true. The dog's affection and the boy's smile quietly undermine that system.

Under this reading, the painting isn't primarily about technology. It is about the absurdity of externally prescribed emotional states.

The smartphone merely makes this absurdity visible.

There is even a more radical interpretation: the original Soviet painting was already instructing its audience how everyone ought to feel. The new image simply makes the previously invisible instruction explicit:

Be sadder!

And the boy's smile becomes a tiny act of freedom.

### 3. Buddhism: the manufacture of suffering

Another possible connection is to Buddhism, particularly its understanding of dukkha (suffering, dissatisfaction) and the role of attachment.

The boy has supposedly failed. But why must this failure produce suffering? Because everyone has invested psychological significance in grades, reputation, approval, and the expectations of others.

The sign introduces a second layer: society not only generates suffering through its attachments, but also demands that suffering be visibly expressed.

Under this reading, the smile suggests some distance from the social identities being imposed on the boy.

There is, however, an important distinction. Buddhism does not teach that suffering is merely imaginary or that a person can always escape it by laughing. The more defensible Buddhist interpretation concerns attachment to social identities and the judgments associated with them.

I find this reading plausible, although the image seems more playful and socially satirical than explicitly Buddhist.

### 4. Metamodernism: irony without abandoning sincerity

There is one further possibility worth considering: metamodernism, understood as an artistic sensibility that moves between postmodern irony and renewed sincerity. This conception was developed by Timotheus Vermeulen and Robin van den Akker.&#x20;

The painting is obviously ironic. But it isn't wholly cynical.

The mother's expression still invites sympathy. The dog still communicates affection. The boy's smile is warm rather than contemptuous.

If the artist merely wanted to declare that all human emotions were fake, the dog and the smile would be unnecessary.

Instead, the image permits us to appreciate the original sentimental scene while simultaneously laughing at the way sentiment is manufactured.

This coexistence of sincerity and irony is characteristic of a metamodern reading.

## My preferred interpretation

I would give Baudrillard the strongest claim as a diagnosis of the society being portrayed, and Zhuangzi the strongest claim as an expression of the artist's philosophical temperament.

The decisive point, for me, is that the boy is not simply exposed as a fraud.

He is smiling because the situation is funny. And perhaps because the dog's affection makes it impossible for him to remain miserable.

There are two kinds of emotional life in the image: one that is directed, judged, and reproduced, and another that simply happens.

The first belongs to the sister with the sign and the smartphone. The second belongs to the dog and the boy's involuntary smile.

That distinction gives the painting a surprisingly affirmative philosophical meaning.

We cannot establish the actual artist's philosophical allegiance from this single image, of course. But I would hesitate to call its underlying position nihilistic. Its humor seems to depend on the possibility that something authentic survives beneath all this theatricality.

My reading in one sentence: The boy is being instructed to perform unhappiness for a world that expects him to be unhappy, but his irrepressible smile reveals how ridiculous that expectation can be.

And perhaps the deepest joke is that the supposedly unfortunate schoolboy has understood something that all the serious adults have missed: he doesn't actually have to be sad simply because the story says he should be.


# To be honest, the first person who comes to mind for me is Žižek.



I think Žižek is actually a stronger candidate than either Baudrillard or Zhuangzi, particularly because of the boy's suppressed smile. And reconsidering the image through Žižek changes my interpretation substantially.

What makes it so Žižekian is that everyone knows the situation is staged, yet they continue acting as though it is real.

This brings us directly to one of Slavoj Žižek's central ideas: cynical ideology.

### 1. They know very well what they're doing—and still they do it

In The Sublime Object of Ideology, Žižek develops his account of modern ideology partly through Peter Sloterdijk's concept of cynical reason.

The traditional Marxist understanding of ideology assumes that people are deceived about their actual circumstances.

Žižek's more unsettling proposition is that ideology continues functioning even when people recognize its artificiality.

Look at the painting.

The girl knows the boy isn't really devastated. The boy knows his sadness is being manufactured. The camera makes the whole operation transparent.

And yet the performance continues.

The sign «ЕЩЁ ГРУСТНЕЕ» is particularly brilliant from this perspective: ideology no longer even needs to conceal the fact that it is directing our emotional responses.

### 2. The boy's smile is not liberation—it is part of the ideology

Here is where I would revise my earlier Daoist interpretation.

I initially interpreted the boy's smile as a moment of freedom from imposed social expectations.

But Žižek would likely challenge that reading.

What if the boy's ironic distance is precisely what allows him to participate in the ideological ritual?

He doesn't genuinely believe that he must be devastated. He knows he is acting. He finds the whole situation funny.

But he continues playing his role.

His smile is not necessarily a rebellion against the system. It may be the psychological mechanism that makes participation bearable.

This is perhaps the most interesting philosophical difference between Žižek and a simple postmodern interpretation.

For a certain kind of postmodernism, exposing the artificiality of a representation undermines its authority.

For Žižek, exposing that artificiality may change almost nothing. We can laugh at the ideological performance while continuing to reproduce it.

### 3. The smartphone and the Lacanian Big Other

The smartphone adds another dimension.

Nobody in the room necessarily believes in the authenticity of this domestic tragedy. Nevertheless, they are constructing it for an imagined audience.

This recalls the Lacanian concept of the Big Other, so important in Žižek's philosophy.

The Big Other is not simply another person watching us. It is the symbolic order—the impersonal system of expectations, rules, and meanings within which our actions become intelligible.

In this scene, the smartphone offers a particularly concrete embodiment of an anticipated external gaze.

The family is staging the emotions that the audience supposedly expects.

The boy must be sad because that is what the narrative requires. The mother must be disappointed because that is what a mother in this narrative is supposed to feel.

And it may not matter that nobody actually believes any of it.

The performance sustains the social fiction.

### 4. But there is an even more interesting twist: socialist realism

Consider that the source painting is a canonical work of Soviet socialist realism.

In Reshetnikov's original, the boy's humiliation is presented as a morally significant event. His sister embodies conscientiousness; his mother, disappointed responsibility; the dog, unconditional affection.

The composition establishes an ideological relationship between personal failure, education, family, and social responsibility.

But now we discover that the entire scene is being directed.

The girl's instruction effectively exposes the invisible ideological command already present in the original painting.

It is as though the original socialist-realist artwork had been turned inside out, revealing the mechanism that produces its supposedly spontaneous emotions.

And here's what I find particularly Žižekian: the contemporary smartphone doesn't necessarily represent emancipation from Soviet ideology.

The old ideological demand was something like: You must be ashamed because you have failed.

The new demand is: You must convincingly perform shame because that is what the audience wants to see.

The content changes, but the structural demand remains.

One could even interpret the image as a transition from the disciplinary logic of Soviet socialist realism to the performative logic of contemporary social media.

Both systems organize our relationship to ourselves through an imagined external gaze.

### 5. The final paradox: Who is really laughing?

This is where the boy's almost-smile becomes decisive.

He is not laughing openly. He is trying not to laugh.

That means he occupies two positions simultaneously: he is participating in the fiction and is aware of its absurdity.

And we, looking at the painting, are placed in exactly the same position.

We recognize the original painting. We understand the joke. We feel intellectually superior to the ideological spectacle being staged.

But perhaps Žižek would ask: why are we so certain that our own ironic distance places us outside ideology?

After all, postmodern irony itself can become an entirely conventional, socially prescribed response.

We are supposed to recognize the reference, appreciate the subversion, and enjoy our own sophistication.

What if our enjoyment of the joke is itself part of the joke?

That is why I increasingly prefer your Žižek interpretation to my earlier Daoist one.

The Daoist reading sees the smile as an escape from an artificial world.

The Žižekian reading asks whether the very feeling of having escaped is one of the ways the artificial world perpetuates itself.

And there is one question I'd be especially interested in exploring: Is the artist ridiculing Soviet ideological art, or is the artist ridiculing our contemporary conviction that we have somehow transcended it?

The second possibility would make the image considerably more radical—and, to my mind, much more Žižekian.
