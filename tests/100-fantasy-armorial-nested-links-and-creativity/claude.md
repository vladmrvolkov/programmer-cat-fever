# 1

This is a **genealogy of fantasy literature drawn as a heraldic armorial**, a roll of coats of arms. Each of the 39 entries (7 founding traditions and 32 writers) gets a shield, and each writer's shield is built from the arms of the writers or traditions that influenced them. The 84 "source relations" are those influence links. The idea in the subtitle, "earlier arms survive inside later shields," means you can read a writer's literary ancestry directly off their shield.

## How it's organised

- **Columns are eras**, left to right: Traditions → Early Foundations → Myth & the Weird → Mid-Century → New Directions → Late-Century. The entry numbers run in blocks by column (01–05, 06–11, 12–15, 16–23, 24–30, 31–39).
- **Each shield is labelled** with its number, name, and a "Sources" line listing the entries it draws on, in order.
- **Lines trace the sources.** Solid lines show descent. Dotted or dashed lines show looser affinity rather than descent, such as Lem from folk tales and Dunsany from Eastern philosophy.
- **Rows loosely form lanes.** The top row follows the fairy-tale line (folk tales → MacDonald → Lewis → Carter → Pullman), and the bottom row follows Gothic/adventure into sword-and-sorcery (Howard → Leiber → Wagner → Cook).

## The rules of construction

**1. Founding devices.** Each tradition has a simple one-colour field with one charge:
- Folk tales: red with three gold lozenges
- Gothic & Romantic: black-and-white vertical stripes
- Northern & Celtic epics: blue with a gold saltire
- Arthuriana: white with a red cross
- Eastern philosophy & antiquity: green with a gold ring
- Haggard & adventure: red with a white diagonal band
- Edwardian tales: gold with a blue top band

**2. Personal device.** Every writer gets a small shield in the centre, invented for them. With a single source, the writer takes that source's arms whole and sets their device on top. MacDonald is the folk-tale field plus a white shield with red roundels. Lewis is MacDonald's arms with Lewis's black-and-white device covering MacDonald's.

**3. Quartering combines sources.** With two sources, the shield is split into four. The first-listed source fills the top-left and bottom-right quarters, and the second fills the other two. This is why Clark Ashton Smith (10, 02) and Lovecraft (02, 10) are mirror images: same ancestry, different emphasis. With more sources the shield divides further, giving Tolkien eight compartments and Sapkowski a 3×3 grid.

**4. Nesting.** A quartered ancestor goes into a quarter whole, including their own quarters and personal device. Jack Vance's shield contains Smith's quartered arms in miniature, and Peake's purple-and-white device reappears inside Moorcock and Wolfe. By the late-century column, shields have become dense patchworks, which visually shows how much each writer synthesises.

**5. Marks of difference.** These borrow real heraldic conventions to show *attitude*, not just inheritance:
- **Colours exchanged (↔) = polemic.** The source's arms appear with their tinctures swapped. Pullman's shield is Lewis's with red and gold reversed, the anti-Narnia. Carter's folk-tale quarters are swapped the same way, for her subversive fairy tales. As far as I can read the small print, Moorcock, Martin, Sapkowski and Pratchett carry this mark on their Tolkien quarter.
- **Whole shield inverted (↺).** Donaldson's shield is literally upside down: Tolkien's arms turned over for the anti-heroic Thomas Covenant. Historically, reversed arms signified disgrace.
- **Three-point label.** Brooks and Jordan carry the white bar with pendants, the heraldic mark of an eldest son who hasn't yet inherited. It's a pointed way of calling them Tolkien's heirs apparent.

A few small details: Leiber and Wagner share identical sources (13, 12), so only their central devices tell them apart. The footer also notes the Weird Tales circle of Lovecraft, Howard and Smith.

## Who is in conflict with Tolkien

**Polemic: Moorcock, Sapkowski, Pratchett, Martin.** Each has **↔** after 21 in the caption. The legend defines ↔ as "colours exchanged: polemic," so their Tolkien compartment should carry his arms with the tinctures swapped. The caption mark is the reliable evidence, because the swap inside Tolkien's already crowded arms is too small to confirm at this size. Martin also has a dashed incoming line, though I can't tell whether it belongs to this link or to his Scott link.

**Inversion: Donaldson.** His caption reads **21↺**, and the whole shield is turned point-upward, the only upside-down shield on the chart. A dashed line also runs into it. It is the most visible sign of opposition on the chart. The legend lists inversion separately from polemic, so it reads as a reversal of Tolkien rather than an argument with him.

## Shields that carry Tolkien without conflict

- **Brooks and Jordan** carry the label, which marks them as Tolkien's heirs rather than his opponents.
- **Le Guin, Wolfe and Hobb** carry him plainly.
- **Cook** has no quarrel of his own. Because nested parts are carried whole, his Moorcock compartment should still contain Moorcock's swapped Tolkien, so he inherits the dispute second-hand.
- **Sapkowski**, by the same logic, objects twice: once in his own Tolkien compartment, and again through the swapped Tolkien inside his Moorcock compartment.

## Problems and inconsistencies

**1. Moorcock's polemic doesn't seem to be drawn.** The first sheet marks his Tolkien source **21↔**, and the second sheet's footer says the contested compartment changes colour. Pratchett's Howard compartment shows what a correct swap looks like: a white field with a red band and a gold field with a blue saltire, the reverse of Howard's own colours. Martin's Tolkien compartment looks swapped the same way. As far as I can make out, Moorcock's Tolkien compartment keeps Tolkien's ordinary colours: blue fields with gold saltires, and Scott's red cross on white. The close-up captions also drop the ↔ marks, so nothing on the second sheet tells you Moorcock is in conflict with Tolkien.

**2. Eddison is effectively invisible in Tolkien's shield.** His compartment is the bottom-right corner, which the shield's curve cuts down to a sliver. His chequered device is lost, and what remains looks like another plain Northern-epics compartment.

**3. Sapkowski's centre hides a source.** In a 3×3 grid, the fifth source falls dead centre, so Poul Anderson sits entirely under Sapkowski's own chequered escutcheon. Moorcock's centre hides much of Howard in the same way, though Howard's purple lozenge keeps him recognisable.

**4. Carter seems to have Lewis's device.** Her central shield (black over white) looks the same as the device Lewis bears on the first sheet. Personal devices are supposed to be unique, and Carter's sources are folk tales and Gothic literature, not Lewis. On the first sheet, the dashed lines on either side of her shield at Lewis's height also read as a chain, Lewis → Carter → Pullman, which neither caption supports.

**5. "Invented personal device" has unstated exceptions.** Brooks and Jordan carry Tolkien's own device, Donaldson carries it upside down, and Pullman carries Lewis's. For Brooks this is good heraldry, since a label is meant to mark the father's arms. Jordan is odder: his shield is otherwise his own composite, yet it carries Tolkien's device and a label. His Brooks compartment then adds a second label inside it.

**6. Legend gaps on the first sheet.**
- The dot after a source number is never explained, though the dotted borders in the close-up confirm it means affinity.
- The legend describes dotted lines only for affinity, yet the same dashed style also carries the polemic and inversion links into Pullman and Donaldson.


# 5

**8 out of 10.**

Family trees of fantasy are common: posters, blog infographics, "map of the genre" charts. Almost all of them are boxes, names and arrows. This one makes the *node itself* carry the data. A shield is a compressed record of its own ancestry, built by real heraldic rules of combining arms, so the picture can be read rather than just looked at. We did exactly that over this conversation: tracing Tolkien through twelve shields, checking quarters against captions, catching errors. That's the test of whether a visual encoding works, and it largely passes. The form also suits the subject, since heraldry is part of fantasy's own imagery.

**Most original element: the marks of difference.** Most influence charts can only say "A influenced B." This one encodes *attitude*:
- **Swapped colours** mean polemic.
- **An upside-down shield** means rebellion.
- **A three-point label** means a dutiful heir.

None of these is invented for the occasion. A label really does mark an eldest son, and reversed arms really did signal disgrace. So the jokes have historical weight: Brooks and Jordan as Tolkien's heirs-in-waiting, Donaldson turning him upside down, Pullman wearing Lewis's arms inside out. That's the part I haven't seen before.

**Most predictable element: the skeleton.** Era columns running left to right, orthogonal connector lines and a Tolkien-centred canon make up the standard genre-history layout. The selection of writers is also the expected one, the names any such chart would include. Strip the shields off and you'd have a very ordinary diagram.

**Weakest part: the late shields can't hold their own logic.** Nesting is the clever rule, and it destroys legibility exactly where the history gets richest. By the late-century column, quarters within quarters shrink to coloured noise. The shield's curve crops the corner compartments, and the central device covers the middle one. Eddison vanishes from Tolkien's shield, Anderson disappears under Sapkowski's centre, and I couldn't confirm whether Moorcock's polemic was even drawn. At that point you're reading the captions, not the arms.

The connector lines share the problem. Merged into shared trunks, they mostly can't be traced, so they act as decoration repeating the captions.

What keeps it from a 9 is that the idea is stronger than the execution where it matters most. A version with fewer nesting levels, perhaps carrying forward only the founding traditions, would lose some depth but keep the late shields readable.

Mostly no. Someone who knows both fantasy history and the chart's rules could identify perhaps a third of the shields. Nobody could identify most of them, because the part meant to say "this is me" says nothing about the writer.

## What a reader can work out without the names

- **The traditions** are easy: each has its own colour and charge.
- **The ancestry** is readable in principle: a shield quartering Howard and Lovecraft must belong to someone downstream of Weird Tales.
- **The marked writers** are the easiest to spot:
  - An upside-down shield is Donaldson.
  - Tolkien's arms with a label are Brooks.
  - Lewis's arms with the colours reversed are Pullman.
- **Tolkien himself** is recognisable by frequency: his eight-part arms recur everywhere.
- **Hobb** you could deduce from Le Guin plus Tolkien.

## Where recognition breaks down

**The personal devices are arbitrary.** Nothing about a white shield with red roundels says MacDonald, and nothing about red and white bars says Moorcock. You could learn them, as medieval heralds learned rolls of arms, but you couldn't infer them.

**Many writers differ only in that small device.**
- MacDonald, Lewis and Lem are all folk tales plus a small central shield.
- Scott and White are both the Arthurian cross plus one.
- Morris, Eddison and Anderson are all the Northern saltire plus one.
- Leiber and Wagner share identical sources, so only their devices separate them.
- Brooks and Jordan both carry Tolkien's device.

**The device shrinks to nothing once nested.** The one identifying mark is the first thing lost when a writer becomes a quarter in someone else's shield.

## What would make them recognisable

**Canting arms**, the heraldic tradition of punning on the bearer's name:
- **Moorcock:** a moorcock, the red grouse
- **Martin:** a martlet, a real heraldic bird long used for that name
- **Wolfe:** a wolf
- **Brooks:** wavy blue bars for a brook
- **Peake:** a mountain peak
- **Carter:** a cartwheel

**Charges drawn from the work**, where the name gives nothing:
- **Eddison:** a serpent eating its tail
- **Jordan:** a wheel
- **Lewis:** a lion
- **Pratchett:** a turtle bearing a disc
- **Pullman:** a compass rose
- **Sapkowski:** two crossed swords, one silver
- **Le Guin:** a dragon
- **Lovecraft:** tentacles

These charges must not collide with the existing system. A ring for Tolkien is the obvious choice, but the ring is already the charge for Eastern philosophy.

**Put identity where nesting can't erase it.** A full heraldic achievement has a crest above the shield. Moving the personal charge there would keep the shield for ancestry and the crest for identity. The crest stays legible at any size, and when a writer becomes a quarter elsewhere, their crest could simply travel as a small badge.

That one change would also address the weakest part I named earlier, because the late shields could get crowded without becoming anonymous.
