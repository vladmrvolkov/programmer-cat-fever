I think the **core joke works very well**, but the comic currently contains *too many jokes competing with the main one*.

The fundamental mechanism is strong: everyone interprets the cat through the extraordinary fact that **it can talk**, while the cat regards that as completely irrelevant because its own identity is **“I’m a programmer.”** The circus offer is therefore based on a category mistake. The punchline —

> “Sounds tempting. But what would they need a **PROGRAMMER** for?”

— suddenly forces the reader to reconstruct everything that came before. That is exactly the kind of retrospective reversal a good narrative joke needs. The cat hasn't misunderstood anything; *everyone else has misunderstood the cat*.

There are also some good supporting details. The waiter repeating “Coffee… cream… pastry…” nicely establishes his disbelief. “And such good English! Where are you FROM?” / “From here” is a decent miniature joke because people are desperately trying to assimilate an impossible phenomenon into ordinary social categories. And the cat imagining acrobats, lollipops, cotton candy etc. before giving the programmer answer is useful: for a moment it makes us think the cat is seriously considering a circus career, which helps conceal the punchline.

Where I think it loses strength is in the **density of peripheral jokes**. “Mum! The cat’s wearing a skirt!”, “Is this AI-generated?”, the petting/HSSS episode, “give us a smile,” and especially the final “First they talk. Next they’ll want to vote” all create independent comic threads. Individually some are amusing, but collectively they make the reader unsure what the comic is *about*.

The “AI-generated?” line is particularly double-edged. It is funny as a meta-joke, especially because the scene itself is absurd, but it immediately breaks the fictional frame. Instead of thinking, “Why is there a talking cat in this café?”, I start thinking about the *picture*. That weakens the narrative misdirection needed for the programmer punchline.

And I would definitely reconsider the **last little panel**. “A programmer? Can you believe it? / First they talk. Next they’ll want to vote.” is a second punchline after the real punchline. In joke construction this is dangerous: the strongest conceptual reversal should ideally be the last thing the reader encounters. Here the comic continues after it has already reached its natural endpoint.

There is another subtle point that I think is important. The laptop appears **only at the moment of the programmer revelation**, which is good. In the earlier version we discussed, showing the laptop beforehand spoiled the joke because it supplied the missing premise visually. Here the laptop functions almost like a visual exclamation mark accompanying the punchline rather than advance information. That's much better.

If this were mine, I would probably reduce it to something like **6–7 panels**:

1. Cat enters café; people notice something odd but no explicit meta-jokes.
2. Cat orders coffee and pastry.
3. Waiter realizes it is a talking cat.
4. Short exchange establishing that this is perfectly normal *to the cat*.
5. Waiter excitedly describes his uncle's circus and the fabulous opportunity.
6. Cat thoughtfully imagines the circus — perhaps one elaborate fantasy panel.
7. **“A circus? Interesting… but what would they need a programmer for?”**
   Laptop visible for the first time.

And then **stop**.

That would turn it from a collection of jokes about a talking cat into a much cleaner **narrative joke with a delayed reinterpretation**. The present version feels somewhat like *MAD magazine*: many little visual and verbal gags packed around the central story. That's not bad in itself, but the original joke is clever enough that I think it benefits from greater austerity.

The funniest thing about the premise, to me, is not really *“a cat is a programmer.”* It's the much more elegant idea: **the miraculous property noticed by everybody else is, from the protagonist's perspective, merely incidental.** That is the part worth protecting.
