It's a really well-built piece of satire.

The talking cat stands in for anyone treated as an exotic outsider in a place they belong. Nearly every reaction is a real-world microaggression translated into cat terms. People comment on her clothes. The waiter praises her "good English" and demands to know where she's *from*, and she answers flatly, "From here." A stranger touches her without asking ("Ooh, it's soft!"), which is the hair-touching thing many people deal with. The waiter tells her to smile and immediately starts working out how to profit from her as a novelty. Because the premise is absurd, you laugh first and recognize the pattern second. That's what lets it make its point without preaching, at least until the very end.

The best part is the twist. The dream sequence misleads you into thinking she's tempted, but her imagined circus is just a cage with the same café patrons gawking at her. Then she says, "what would they need a PROGRAMMER for?" That line does two jobs. She turns down the novelty role on her own terms, and it exposes the waiter's deepest disbelief. He adjusted to a *talking cat* within a few panels, but a cat with a professional skill is what truly breaks him. The comic is saying that people accept difference as a curiosity much faster than they accept it as competence. His "cage— er, dressing room" slip sets this up nicely.

The background details reward a second look:
- The old woman wrapped in a fur stole, made from actual dead animals, is aghast at a cat wearing clothes.
- The pixelated "Is this AI-generated?" bubble is a very current way of dismissing anything that doesn't fit your worldview as fake.
- The only time the cat acts "like an animal" is when someone touches her without permission, which is exactly the moment onlookers would use to say "see, she's wild."
- The waiter finally pours her cream while gossiping about her to someone else.

If I had one critique, it's the closing line. "First they talk. Next they'll want to vote" is funny, and it echoes almost word for word real arguments made against women, Black citizens and immigrants. But it states the subtext outright. Ending on "A programmer! Can you believe it?" would trust the reader a bit more. The comic is also very dense: almost every panel is its own jab, so on a first read it can feel slightly like a checklist. The cat is also a composite of several groups (the skirt and "smile" point to gender, the English and "where from" point to immigration or race). That makes it widely relatable, though it trades some sharpness for breadth.

Overall, it's clever, the art sells the characters, and the programmer reveal lifts it from a list of jokes into a real punchline.
