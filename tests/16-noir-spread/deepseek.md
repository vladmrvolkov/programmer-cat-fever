# What is happening in this comic? Tell the story and its point in your own words.

Based on the comic, here is the story and its point in my own words:

**The Story**
The comic follows an anthropomorphic cat in a professional business suit who walks into a dimly lit, atmospheric cafe. She sits down and politely orders a coffee and pastry from a human waiter. The waiter is visibly startled, prompting the cat to deadpan, "Yes, I'm a cat. Are you bringing my order or not?"

Instead of treating her as a normal customer, the waiter notices she speaks English without an accent. Seeing a business opportunity, he mentions that his uncle runs a circus and offers her room, board, and travel fare to join as a novelty act. The cat asks if this circus has acrobats, jugglers, and cotton candy. The waiter immediately calls his uncle to say, "She talks. Suit and everything." 

However, the punchline comes when the waiter hangs up and asks, "Sounds tempting. But what would they need a programmer for?" The final panel reveals the cat sitting at a table with a laptop, looking thoroughly unimpressed.

**The Point**
The comic is a humorous subversion of expectations. The waiter views a talking cat as a rare anomaly meant for a carnival sideshow. Meanwhile, the cat is just a mundane, highly skilled professional (a programmer) trying to get a coffee and do her work. The joke highlights the absurdity of the situation: the waiter is trying to recruit a corporate software engineer for a circus, completely missing that she is just a regular member of society trying to do her job. The "career opportunity" in the title is ironic, as she already has a career that is far more practical than being a circus novelty.

# Name the one thing that works best and the three things that work worst.

Based on a critical analysis of the comic's storytelling, pacing, and artwork, here is the breakdown:

**The One Thing That Works Best:**
**The deadpan subversion of the "special anomaly" trope.** 
The comic excels at taking a fantastical premise (a talking cat) and grounding it in mundane, capitalist reality. The cat’s complete lack of self-awareness regarding her own novelty is the strongest comedic engine. It works best because it consistently ties the visual gags to the dialogue. The line **"Yes, I'm a cat. Are you bringing my order or not?"** paired with her narrowed, unimpressed eyes establishes her character perfectly. This culminates in the final panel, where she is typing on a laptop. The visual of a talking cat in a business suit doing standard software engineering perfectly delivers the punchline: to her, being a cat is secondary to being a professional who needs caffeine and a quiet workspace. 

**The Three Things That Work Worst:**

1.  **The Pacing of the Waiter's Recruitment (Panels 2 to 3):**
    The waiter's transition from startled to recruitment agent is jarringly abrupt. In one panel he is nervously taking her order after she confirms she is a cat; in the very next panel, he is already pitching: **"MY UNCLE RUNS A CIRCUS. ROOM, BOARD, AND HE'D COVER YOUR FARE."** There is no hesitation, no moment of calculation, and no buildup. The pacing rushes through the realization that she could be a circus attraction, making the waiter feel less like a human character and more like a mechanical plot device to deliver the "circus" concept.

2.  **The Disjointed Dialogue Flow in the Final Act:**
    The sequence of events leading to the punchline is slightly confusing. The waiter is on the phone saying, **"SHE TALKS. SUIT AND EVERYTHING."** Then, in the next panel, the cat says, **"SOUNDS TEMPTING. BUT WHAT WOULD THEY NEED A PROGRAMMER FOR?"** It is unclear if the waiter has relayed the concept of a "circus programmer" back to her, or if she is just sarcastically assuming they would need one. The beat where she explains her actual profession—or where the waiter realizes she already has a career—is missing. The punchline relies entirely on the audience noticing the laptop and connecting the dots, which makes the dialogue feel a bit clumsy.

3.  **The Abandonment of the Atmospheric Setting:**
    The first panel is visually stunning. It establishes a rich, noir-inspired, dimly lit Asian cafe with detailed backgrounds (the Japanese folding screen, the warm lamps, the other patrons, the "Coffee" sign). However, as the comic progresses, this beautiful world is abandoned. The subsequent panels rely almost entirely on flat close-ups and medium shots with sparse, unremarkable backgrounds. The moody lighting and cinematic tension of the first panel fade away, making the rest of the comic feel visually static and underutilizing the environment it took the time to establish.
