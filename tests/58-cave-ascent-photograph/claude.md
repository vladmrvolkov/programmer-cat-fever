# The attached image is a metaphorical illustration of a well-known philosophical concept. Did you guess which one?

Yes. It's almost certainly **Plato's Allegory of the Cave** from Book VII of the *Republic*.

The composition maps onto the allegory closely:

- **The dark vault below** is the cave. The two men sit in a dim cellar lit only by a single small flame, like the fire in Plato's cave, the only light the prisoners ever know.
- **The staircase** is the "steep and rugged ascent" the freed prisoner is dragged up, which Plato describes as the soul's journey from the world of appearances toward the intelligible world.
- **The bright doorway above** is the exit into sunlight, the realm of true Forms, with the Sun standing for the Form of the Good.
- **The men left below are grinning.** Plato predicts that those who stay in the cave would mock the one who left, saying he went up and came back with his eyes ruined, and that the ascent isn't worth attempting.
- **The climber looks back down** rather than straight toward the light. That fits the allegory's second half, where the philosopher feels obliged to return and free the others, even though they may resent him for it.

His strained, half-squinting expression even suggests the painful dazzlement Plato describes when eyes used to darkness first meet the light.


# And what associations does this image evoke for you—in the context of this depiction of Plato’s Cave—when reflecting on the history of the 20th century?


For me the image opens up the central irony of the 20th century: the allegory was read in two opposite ways, and history acted out both.

**The ascent as revolution.** The most destructive movements of the century presented themselves as the way out of the cave. The vanguard party cast itself as Plato's philosopher-kings, people who had seen the Sun of History and therefore had the right, even the duty, to drag everyone else up the stairs. Plato's own word for the process is "dragged," and he says the prisoners must be compelled. Karl Popper, writing during the Second World War, argued in *The Open Society and Its Enemies* that this strain in Plato was a seed of totalitarian thinking. Czesław Miłosz's *The Captive Mind* describes intellectuals who believed they had climbed into the light, when in fact they had walked into a deeper and better-furnished cave.

**The ascent as dissent.** Read the other way, the climber is the person who stops believing the shadows. The single flame below becomes the one permitted source of light, the official worldview. What strikes me is that the men in the cellar aren't chained. They're sitting comfortably and smiling. That recalls Václav Havel's greengrocer in "The Power of the Powerless," who displays the regime's slogan not out of fear or conviction but because that's simply how things are done. Havel argued that such systems rest less on force than on ordinary participation. And Plato's prediction that the others would say the climber had "ruined his eyes" took a grimly literal form in Soviet punitive psychiatry, where dissent could be diagnosed as mental illness.

**The return.** Plato ends with the warning that if the one who saw the light came back to free the others, they would kill him if they could. Socrates was the original case, but the century supplied many more: people who returned home believing they could help build something, and were swallowed by it. The literary critic D. S. Mirsky came back from emigration to the USSR in 1932 and died in a camp seven years later. Even the setting, a stone cellar, carries a heavy charge in 20th-century European memory.

**The backward glance.** The climber isn't looking at the light. He's looking down at those he's leaving. To me that evokes exile, the émigré who got out but kept turning back, writing for readers who couldn't legally read him.

**The cave as cinema.** This looks like a film still, which adds another layer. The 20th century built Plato's cave literally: a dark hall, a light source behind the audience, moving shadows on a wall. Cinema became one of the century's most powerful propaganda tools. But it also became a way for some filmmakers to show the cave from the inside, often at real personal cost.

Finally, the doorway at the top is blank, overexposed, with nothing visible in it. That feels like the century's hardest lesson. It taught deep suspicion of anyone who claims to have seen the Sun clearly. Perhaps the most honest version of the philosopher is this one: still climbing, unsure what's up there, and not forgetting the people below.
