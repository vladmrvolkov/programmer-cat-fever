# The attached image is a metaphorical illustration of a well-known philosophical concept. Did you guess which one?

My best guess is **Heidegger's "das Man"** (usually translated as "the They" or "the One"), together with his related idea of **idle talk (Gerede)**.

A few details point there for me. The loudspeaker has no speaker behind it. It's just a voice on a pole, which matches Heidegger's point that "the They" isn't any particular person. It's the anonymous "one" in "one doesn't do that" or "everyone's saying…" The chains also run to people's *mouths*, not their ears. They aren't just hearing the broadcast; they're repeating it as if it were their own speech, which is exactly idle talk: opinions passed along without anyone owning them. And the scene is deliberately ordinary: a crosswalk, coffee, shopping bags, phones, earbuds. Heidegger located das Man in everyday life, where people drift along absorbed in what "one" thinks and does, mostly without noticing.

A couple of other readings are plausible too. The chains strongly echo **Plato's Cave**, with prisoners shackled to a second-hand version of reality. The street-corner voice hailing passersby recalls **Althusser's interpellation**. And the unchained child walking beside the chained adults is a nice nod to **Rousseau's** "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains."

Which one did the artist have in mind?

# Note that one end of the chain is attached to the loudspeaker, and the other to the ears.


You're right, I misread that. Chains running from a mouthpiece to listeners' ears change the reading, because that's an almost exact quotation of a famous classical allegory.

**Lucian's Gallic Hercules (Ogmios).** Lucian describes a Celtic image of Hercules as an old man leading a crowd by thin chains that run from his tongue to their ears. The point is that eloquence binds people more powerfully than force. Renaissance emblem books made it a standard symbol of persuasion. Here the loudspeaker, essentially a giant artificial mouth, replaces the orator's tongue, and the earbud wires turn into the chains. So the concept would be the power of rhetoric, updated as mass media or propaganda: people steered not by force but by what they listen to.

The slack chains add another layer. In Lucian, the captives follow cheerfully and could easily break free, but don't. In the illustration, the chains hang loose and people walk along calmly, coffee in hand. That fits **La Boétie's "voluntary servitude"**: domination lasts because people go along with it, not because they're dragged.

Heidegger still fits, even better with ears than mouths. In *Being and Time* he says Dasein fails to hear itself because it is busy "listening away" to the They. Only the call of conscience can interrupt that listening.

So my revised answer is the power of persuasion over a willing audience, with Gallic Hercules as the likely visual source.


# You correctly identified the prototype of the image - it is Lucian's Gallic Hercules (Ogmios). Now you need to determine which philosophical concept (it is not from antiquity and not Dasein) this metaphor relates to.

Then it's almost certainly **Hobbes's "artificial chains" of civil law** from *Leviathan* (1651), chapter 21, "Of the Liberty of Subjects." Hobbes borrows the Gallic Hercules image directly. Having built the Commonwealth as an "artificial man," people have also made

> "artificial chains, called civil laws, which they themselves, by mutual covenants, have fastened at one end to the lips of that man or assembly to whom they have given the sovereign power, and at the other end to their own ears."

So the concept is the **social contract** as Hobbes conceives it. Subjects bind themselves to the sovereign's word by their own agreement, and law is simply the sovereign's command, heard and obeyed.

The illustration fits this in several ways:

- **The voice is impersonal.** It comes from a loudspeaker on a utility pole, a piece of public infrastructure, not an individual orator. That suits a sovereign who can be "a man or an assembly."
- **The chains hang slack, and people go about their business.** For Hobbes, a subject's liberty lies in whatever the law is silent about. Within those gaps you walk, shop, check your phone. He also says the chains are weak in themselves and hold only because breaking them is dangerous, not because it's hard.
- **The child is unchained.** Hobbes says law doesn't bind children, since they can't make covenants, which neatly explains why the little boy walks free.
