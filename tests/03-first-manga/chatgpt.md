I think the joke works, especially as a **character-based misunderstanding rather than a one-line gag**.

The structure is clean: everyone sees “a talking cat,” the waiter immediately assumes that her unusual trait must be her profession, and offers her a circus job. Meanwhile, she treats being a talking cat as completely irrelevant and assumes he must be recruiting her **as a programmer**. The final line—

> “Sounds tempting. But what would they need a programmer for?”

—is funny because it reveals that the two characters have been having entirely different conversations.

A few things make it stronger than the basic premise. Her business clothes, briefcase, impatient expression, and very ordinary coffee order quietly establish that **she regards herself as a normal working professional**. So the programmer reveal doesn't come completely out of nowhere. Her deadpan “Yes, I’m a cat. Are you bringing my order or not?” also establishes the personality needed for the punchline: she isn't amazed by herself and doesn't understand why anyone else should be.

The circus imagination panel is useful too. For a moment it looks as though she's actually considering becoming a circus attraction, which leads the reader toward the waiter's interpretation. Then the final panel corrects that assumption. That's a nice little piece of misdirection.

The one thing I'd consider tightening is the punchline wording. **“But what would a circus need a programmer for?”** is slightly more immediate than “what would they need a programmer for?”, because “circus” and “programmer” collide in the same sentence. Your current version sounds more natural conversationally, though, so I'd keep it if her dry, matter-of-fact personality is the priority.

I also like that the joke has a small satirical layer: everyone is fixated on the spectacular thing about her, while she thinks of herself in terms of her actual skills and career. So it's essentially:

**Waiter:** “You’re an extraordinary animal!”
**Cat:** “Yes, yes. Anyway, I work in software.”

That contrast is the heart of it, and the comic's pacing supports it well.
