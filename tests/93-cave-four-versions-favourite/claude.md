My favorite is **the third one**, although the second one is the one I keep thinking about.

The third works because it changes the thing every other version of the allegory takes for granted: the cave itself. Here the "cave" is just two walls of paneling propped up with wooden braces in an empty desert. No one is chained and there is no rock. The open world is a few steps away, and the real sun is casting real shadows of their chairs across the ground while they lean toward a candle in a brass model of the solar system. The best detail is the window. Someone installed blinds in a wall with nothing behind it but more desert, and the thin beam that comes through is treated as a curiosity. This is very close to Plato's own point in Book VII: education doesn't put sight into blind eyes, it turns the whole soul around toward the light. The painting makes that turn look almost embarrassingly easy, which makes their failure to do it more damning. The lanyards help too. What keeps these people in place is habit, the institution, and the authority of the man running the demonstration.

The second picture has the most interesting gesture. Blowing out the candle is the destructive half of philosophy: you put out the false sun before you've found the real one. In this version the blinds are dark, with no beam of daylight as in the first image, so for now the act leads into total darkness. You can read that pessimistically, as a skepticism that tears down the model with nothing to replace it. You can also read it hopefully: a faint crack of real light is invisible while a bright flame burns in front of your face, and only after the candle goes out can your eyes find it. There's also something slightly irreverent about it, like blowing out the candles on someone else's birthday cake while the others are still deep in thought. It's the one moment of action in a scene that is otherwise all contemplation, and I like that a lot.

The fourth is the most literal, since a man has climbed a ladder and broken through the roof to stare at the sun. It has a sharp twist, though: the "real" cosmos outside is also an orrery, with Earth, Jupiter and Mars on brass arms and the sun held in a clamp. Either you escape one cave only to find a bigger one, or, more in Plato's spirit, reality turns out to be a rational mechanism, like the mathematical cosmos of the *Timaeus*. It's the most ambitious of the four, but also the busiest, and it explains itself more than the third does.

The first is the cleanest statement of the classic setup. A beam of real daylight cuts across the room, and every person is looking at the candle instead. It's beautifully done, but it feels like the baseline the other three play against.

One thing I noticed: the curly-haired man with his back to us seems to be the same figure throughout. He sits passively in the first, blows out the candle in the second, is back in his seat in the third, and in the fourth his chair is empty because he's the one on the roof. Were these meant as a sequence?
