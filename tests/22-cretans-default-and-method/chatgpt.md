Both versions work, but they do **completely different things** — and that is exactly why they work so well side by side.

**The left one** visualizes the *logical structure*. It is almost emblematic: a Cretan, the inscription "I AM CRETAN", and the statement **ALL CRETANS ARE LIARS** physically loops back to the speaker with an arrow. The result is almost a diagram of self-reference. I especially like the ribbon: it is at once a scroll, a logical loop and even, slightly, a Möbius strip. The viewer hardly needs to be told where the problem lies.

The left version does have one philosophical inaccuracy, though: it tells the viewer very confidently "here is a paradox", while the famous phrase on its own does not make a strict liar paradox. If the Cretan's statement "all Cretans are liars" is false, it only follows that **not all** Cretans are liars; it does not follow that this particular Cretan is telling the truth right now. And in ordinary language *liar* means "a person who lies", not "a person every one of whose statements is false". A real logical loop needs stronger premises.

**The right** version is, in my view, intellectually funnier. It no longer tries to depict the paradox itself; it shows **a wrong conclusion drawn from it**:

> "I'm Cretan. All Cretans are liars."  
> "At last, someone I can trust."

It is the last line that makes the picture good. The boss seems to have decided: "Since the man admitted he can't be trusted, he deserves trust." This is a very recognizable human attempt to resolve logical uncertainty with a psychological heuristic. It becomes a joke not only about logic but about the **epistemology of trust**.

There is one more amusing detail: the **REFERENCES** paper strengthens the joke. Formal recommendations turn out to be less convincing than the candidate's paradoxical self-confession. So the right picture can be read as a small satire on hiring:

**"At last, a candidate who honestly said he lies."**

If I compare them by function, I would put it like this:

- **left:** "Here is the problem of self-reference."
- **right:** "Here is what happens when a person tries to make practical use of that problem."

The right one, perhaps, asks half a second more work of the viewer. At first the boss seems simply stupid; then you realize that his line is one more turn of the same problem: **can an admission of unreliability be a reason for trust?**

For an article or a collection of philosophical visualizations I would therefore **keep both**. The left one is a fine headpiece or an illustration next to the definition; the right one is much better as comic relief after the explanation.

And I would not correct the right picture by making it logically "right". The logical wrongness of the line **"At last, someone I can trust"** is precisely the joke. It turns the rather worn "Cretan paradox" into something new.
