Both diptychs tell the same two-step story: first someone **sees through an illusion**, then **that act of seeing through becomes a new illusion of its own**, usually one you can buy and wear. A critique of the system gets absorbed by the system and sold back as a product or a uniform.

**The first diptych** riffs on John Carpenter's *They Live*, where special sunglasses reveal the hidden commands behind advertising. On the left, the man holds the glasses up to a dreamy Amalfi-coast ad (sun, champagne, lemons, a beautiful woman) and sees its real message: BUY. It's a moment of clarity. On the right, he's in a boutique wearing those same sunglasses as a fashion accessory and a T-shirt that says BUY, with the price tags still hanging off it. He's admiring himself in the mirror. The command that was once hidden is now printed on his chest in giant letters, and he's paying for it. Knowing the trick didn't free him; it just became his new look.

**The second diptych** does the same thing with *The Emperor's New Clothes*, drawn as an ukiyo-e print with cats. On the left, the emperor parades in robes everyone pretends to see, the crowd bows, and only the little kitten in dark glasses perceives the truth (the grey inset shows what the glasses reveal: he's naked). On the right, the truth is out, but look at how the crowd expresses it. They all wear identical blue coats printed with 裸 ("naked"), they point and jeer in unison, and a stall on the right is selling spectacles, fans, and prints of the procession. The kitten who first saw the truth now covers its face in dismay. The pointing crowd is exactly as conformist as the bowing crowd was. They've just switched slogans. Even the inset changes from stark grey to cheerful color: the raw insight has been turned into a marketable image.

The parallels line up neatly. Glasses are the tool of revelation in both, and in both they end up as merchandise. The revelation itself becomes a slogan on clothing (BUY, 裸). And the person or crowd who "sees through it" ends up looking at themselves or performing for others rather than actually being free.

The idea has a few names. The Situationists called it *recuperation*: radical ideas get defused by being turned into commodities. Thomas Frank wrote about it as "the conquest of cool," and Žižek/Sloterdijk describe "cynical reason," where people know perfectly well they're being manipulated and go along with it anyway, now with an ironic smile. There's even a real-world twist tied to the first image: the "OBEY" imagery from *They Live* inspired Shepard Fairey's street art, which became the OBEY clothing brand. That's pretty much the right-hand panel come to life.
