Both diptychs explore the power of appearances and symbols to construct a reality that we mistake for something intrinsic.

In the first diptych, a man encounters the command BUY through a pair of sunglasses. In the next image, he is wearing that very command on his chest. What began as an external message has become part of his identity. He has not simply bought a product; he has internalized the logic of consumption and turned himself into its advertisement.

In the second diptych, a cat is treated as an emperor because he wears the elaborate robes and insignia of power. Remove those garments, and the same cat becomes an object of ridicule. Nothing essential about him has changed. Only the symbols that determine how others perceive him have disappeared.

The common idea is that we confuse symbols with substance. Clothing becomes dignity, consumption becomes identity, and appearances become authority. We assign value to things and people not necessarily because of what they are, but because of the meanings we have learned to attach to them.

There is also a subtle difference between the two stories: the first shows how symbols control our perception of ourselves, while the second shows how they control other people's perception of us.

I would summarize both with a single sentence:

“We give symbols their power, then forget that the power came from us.”

That is what makes both diptychs satirical: they reveal how much of what we consider natural, desirable, or worthy of respect depends on conventions that could disappear as easily as a change of clothes.
