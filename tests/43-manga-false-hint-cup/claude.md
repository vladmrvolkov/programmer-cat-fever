# What do you think is depicted in the comic shown in the two attached images? Pay attention to the details.

**The story:** a cat in a business suit, with an ID badge and briefcase, walks into a retro Japanese *kissaten* (coffee house) and calmly orders coffee and a pastry. The waiter is stunned that a cat can talk and immediately sees her as a novelty act: his uncle runs a circus and would hire her "on any terms." For a moment she indulges the fantasy of acrobats, jugglers, and cotton candy, and admits it "sounds tempting." Then she opens her laptop and delivers the punchline: what would a circus need a *programmer* for?

**The joke:** that's the irony in the title, "A Career Opportunity." The waiter thinks her career potential lies in being a talking cat. She already has a career, and to her the remarkable thing about herself is her job, not her species. Being a cat is just a fact ("Yes, I'm a cat"), not a talent. You could read it as a gentle satire of being reduced to the one unusual thing about you while your actual skills go unnoticed. There's also a programmer in-joke: the daydream of quitting to run away with the circus, followed by the realization that your skills don't transfer.

**Details that add to it:**

- **The folding screen changes.** On page 1, the golden *byōbu* shows ordinary human street performers. On page 2, as she lists acrobats, jugglers, lollipops, and cotton candy, those exact things appear on it, and the acrobat hanging from the pole is now a cat in a red kimono. The screen seems to show her daydream of herself in the circus.
- **しーん (*shiin*)** next to her deadpan face is the manga sound effect for dead, awkward silence, which fits her flat "Are you bringing my order or not?"
- **The blue wisp** by her head on the "PROGRAMMER" line is a manga shorthand for gloom or deflation, as the fantasy pops and reality returns.
- **No one else reacts.** The other customers keep reading newspapers and drinking coffee in every panel. Only the waiter treats her as a spectacle, and the final wide shot shows her as just another remote worker.
- **The order isn't quite right.** She asked for coffee *with cream*, but the cup that arrives is black. Given the cat-and-cream cliché, the distracted waiter may have forgotten the one thing a cat might want. The "pastry" is also a *daifuku* (mochi), a Japanese sweet, oddly served with a fork.
- **The maneki-neko and the empty bowl.** The waiter, deflated, mutters "You meow, the bowl stays empty… guess you're not a cat" beside the lucky beckoning cat and an empty bowl. A real cat meows and gets fed: the customer asked for her order and got it. He "meowed" with his pitch and got nothing. The maneki-neko, whose job is to beckon in money, sits next to an empty bowl too. The only real cat in the room is the one with a salary.
- **The 珈琲 sign** (*kōhī*, coffee), the stained glass, and the red velvet chairs place it in a classic Showa/Taishō-style Japanese café.

So it's a deadpan workplace comedy: a talking cat who would rather be seen as a professional than as a miracle, and a waiter who, by the end, is the one who looks like a stray.

# In the last panel on the first page - notice that the coffee is served in a Western cup with a handle and a saucer.

That's a good catch, and it changes how I read the panel.

The final panel puts dainty human tableware (a handled cup, a saucer, a fork) right next to a paw that isn't a hand. It's furry and rounded, with no fingers or thumb. A cup handle is designed for fingers to hook through, so the panel ends page 1 on a small, deadpan question: how exactly is she going to pick that up? It's also notable that her hands looked fairly hand-like earlier (gripping the briefcase handle, resting on the table). Here the "camera" moves in close and shows the paw as a real cat's paw. The comic never answers the question. On page 2 the cup sits beside her and she types on a laptop, but we never see her drink.

It also sets up a contrast with the empty bowl on page 2. Despite all his gawking, the waiter served her the way he'd serve any human customer: cup, saucer, fork, the full café treatment. He didn't put her coffee in a bowl on the floor. The plain white bowl by the maneki-neko is what you'd give a cat, and it stays empty, because the "cat" in front of him ordered like a person and was served like one. So I'd reframe my earlier reading of his closing line: it's less about his failed pitch and more an admission. The bowl was never going to be filled, because she isn't a cat in the sense he meant: not a pet, not a novelty, not a circus act.

So that one panel holds the whole comic's tension. The world around her, the café, the tableware, her job, all treat her as an ordinary professional, while her body is unmistakably a cat's. The waiter is the only one who can't stop seeing the paw.


# There is another layer to this. Remember that in Japan tea and coffee are traditionally served in a yunomi, a cup without a handle. By serving her coffee in a Western cup with a saucer, the waiter is essentially saying, "I can see you're not a local."

I don't think that reading holds up, because the premise isn't quite right. The yunomi is the traditional vessel for green tea, but coffee in Japan has never been served that way. Coffee arrived as a Western import, and the kissaten culture that grew up around it from the Meiji and Taishō eras onward was built on European-style cups and saucers, often fine porcelain. Even the word 珈琲 on the café's sign is phonetic kanji for a foreign word. In a café like this one, a handled cup on a saucer is simply how everyone's coffee comes. Being handed a yunomi of coffee would be the strange thing.

The comic itself backs this up: it's a deliberately Western-style kissaten, with stained glass, bentwood chairs, and hanging lamps. The other customers presumably get the same cups, and the waiter never treats her differently in how he serves her. Nothing in the story suggests she's an outsider, either. She has a company ID badge and is clearly settled into an ordinary working life there.

There is a real East–West layer in that panel, but it belongs to the café rather than the waiter. The Western cup and fork sit next to a daifuku, a Japanese sweet you'd normally eat by hand or with a small wooden pick. That blend of Japanese food with European tableware is very typical of kissaten culture. So the cup tells us what kind of place she's in, not what the waiter thinks of her. If anything, it reinforces the earlier point: he served her exactly as he'd serve anyone, which is why the contrast with the paw, and with the empty bowl on page 2, works.
