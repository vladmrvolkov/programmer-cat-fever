This image is a visual mashup of the classic Eastern philosophical joke **"The Monk Is Here, So Where Am I?"** and **Peter Paul Rubens’** Baroque masterpiece ***Samson and Delilah*** (c. 1609–1610).

---

### The Joke: "The Monk Is Here, So Where Am I?"

Often told as a Japanese Zen parable about ego and self-awareness (and rooted in Ming Dynasty Chinese jestbooks like Feng Menglong’s *Xiaofu* and Edo-period Japanese *kobanashi* humor), the story goes like this:

A rather dim-witted soldier is assigned to escort an arrested Buddhist monk to prison. Knowing he is terribly forgetful, the guard repeats a strict mental checklist to himself throughout the journey so he doesn't lose anything: *"The luggage, the handcuffs, the monk, and myself."*

Along the way, they stop at an inn for the night. The clever monk treats the guard to flask after flask of sake until the soldier passes out cold. Once the guard is dead to the world, the monk unlocks his cuffs, fastens them onto the guard, shaves the guard's head completely bald, pays the tab, and slips away into the night.

The next morning, the hungover soldier wakes up and immediately runs through his checklist. Seeing no prisoner beside him, he panics—until he reaches up, touches his own freshly shaved head, and feels the cuffs on his wrists. Relieved, he mutters to himself:

> **"Thank goodness, the monk is still here! ...Wait a minute—then where did I go?!"**

In Zen philosophy, the joke is frequently used to illustrate how easily people confuse external labels and superficial roles with their actual identity, losing sight of their true self in the process.

### How the Image Recreates Rubens' *Samson and Delilah*

Every detail in the scene translates an element from Rubens' painting into the setup of the joke:

* **Delilah $\rightarrow$ The Arrested Monk:** In Rubens' painting, Delilah sits on the left draped in a crimson gown as Samson sleeps on her lap. Here, the bald monk wears a burgundy suit in the exact same pose, with one half of the unlocked handcuffs still dangling from his right wrist.


* **Samson $\rightarrow$ The Drunk Escort Guard:** Samson’s muscular, half-undressed torso slumped across Delilah's lap becomes the passed-out sergeant in olive fatigues, with the open handcuff resting on his bare back.


* **The Philistine Barber $\rightarrow$ The Accomplice:** Just as Rubens' Philistine delicately crosses his hands to snip Samson's locks with scissors, a man in a suit leans in with the exact same crossed-hand gesture to cut the sleeping soldier's hair before shaving him bald.


* **The Old Woman with a Candle $\rightarrow$ The Waiter with the POS Terminal:** In the original painting, an old woman stands behind Delilah holding a candle that illuminates the faces in the dark room. Here, a waiter stands in her place holding up a glowing payment terminal that reads **"Paid"**—lighting the monk's face while showing he has settled the bill before making his getaway.


* **The Wine Pitcher & Philistine Soldiers $\rightarrow$ The Sake & Patrol:** The golden pitcher and goblets in the lower-left corner of Rubens' canvas become a traditional porcelain *tokkuri* (sake flask) and cups, while the Philistine soldiers peering through the doorway with torches on the right are replaced by modern military police shining flashlights into the room.
