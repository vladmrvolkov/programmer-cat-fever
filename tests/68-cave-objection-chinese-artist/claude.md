# The attached image represents Plato’s allegory of the Cave through a reworking of Joseph Wright of Derby’s painting of a philosopher demonstrating an orrery. This is certain: the artist meant exactly that.  Take this identification as given.

There is, however, a philosophical difficulty. The people in the picture know that they are looking at a model, not at reality; they do not mistake shadows for real things. In Republic VI (510d–511a), visible mathematical diagrams can assist reasoning about intelligible objects. In Republic VII (529a–530c), Socrates argues that astronomy should lead beyond observation of the visible heavens toward the study of intelligible mathematical relations. Studying a model is therefore not, by itself, evidence of intellectual captivity.

In this image, people are absorbed in an illuminated model of the cosmos while daylight is shut out. A straightforward Cave reading seems to make their engagement with the model a form of confinement, with the neglected light outside suggesting a possible release. Yet Plato’s account does not simply oppose misleading intellectual models to liberating sensory experience.

Some reconciliation with Plato’s educational programme may be possible, but treating every element as a straightforward illustration of his teaching seems strained. For this task, assume that the image nevertheless offers a meaningful use of the Cave allegory.

Suppose also that the artist is a contemporary Chinese painter.

How would you interpret the image as a whole? Propose the reading you find most convincing, explain how it accounts for this tension, and support it with specific features of the image. What does your interpretation leave unresolved?

---


The reading I find most convincing is that the picture is about **substitution** rather than deception. The figures are not fooled by the model. The model serves them so well that the thing it stands for no longer seems necessary. Their captivity comes from sufficiency, not error.

Wright's own title points this way. He exhibited the painting in 1766 as *A Philosopher giving that Lecture on the Orrery, in which a lamp is put in place of the Sun*. Plato's key to the allegory makes the same equation: the fire inside the cave corresponds to the power of the visible sun (517b). The reworking takes Wright's subtitle literally and asks what the substitution costs.

**A later stage of the Cave**

This places the scene at a later stage of the Cave than the familiar one. There is no wall and there are no shadows, the most predictable elements of any Cave illustration. Instead the figures sit at the fire looking directly at a crafted object, which is the position of the released prisoner who turns toward the fire and the artifacts carried before it (515c–d). At 532b–c, Plato assigns exactly this turning to the mathematical studies.

Even the lowest segment of the Line is in the picture and passed over. The polished table mirrors the flame and the brass, like the reflections "in smooth, bright surfaces" of 510a, and nobody looks down at them. These people have climbed. The question is whether they have stopped.

The painter also changes one thing in Wright's composition. Wright hid the lamp behind a silhouetted boy. Here the flame sits fully exposed at the centre, plainly an oil lamp in a brass bowl. Everyone can see that the sun is a lamp, and it makes no difference. Exposing the mechanism does not break its hold.

**How this handles the tension**

Your objection is right: studying a model is not captivity. The reading answers it with Plato's own distinction at 511b. Dialectic treats hypotheses as footholds and springboards (*epibaseis te kai hormas*) toward an unhypothetical first principle. *Dianoia* takes its starting points as given, gives no account of them, and reasons downward to conclusions (510b–d). That is why Socrates says the mathematical arts only "dream" about what is (533b–c).

The same model can be a springboard or a dwelling, and nothing in the model decides which. So the painting need not condemn models. It shows the conditions under which a model has become a place to live, and those conditions are visible.

The first condition is that darkness is what gives the model its authority. The blinds were drawn from inside, presumably for the demonstration, as for any presentation. In a sunlit room the flame would look feeble; the lamp can serve as a sun only because the sun is shut out. The same holds in Plato's cave, where the fire throws shadows only because the place is dark. No one is chained. The confinement is chosen, for good practical reasons, which is more Platonic than it looks: Plato's prisoners are attached to their condition and hostile to anyone who would lead them out (515d–e, 517a).

The second is a closed circuit of intelligibility. Plato's contrast in 529–530 is not between a model and unmodelled reality. The visible heavens are themselves like diagrams drawn by Daedalus (529d–e), the work of the "craftsman of the heavens" (530a). Inside and outside are both models. What differs is authorship. The orrery and its light belong to the people in the room; the sun does not. *Dianoia*'s inability to account for its starting point appears here as a model whose first principle is one of its own components.

The third is the institution. Everyone wears a lanyard with a blank badge. These are credentials that grant admission but carry no name, a fair visual equivalent of a hypothesis accepted without an account. The presenter wears one too. He holds a small black object that reads as a remote, and his other hand grips the outermost arc of the armillary, so he literally holds the boundary of the cosmos.

In Plato, the people who carry artifacts before the fire manufacture what is seen. In Wright, the philosopher brings the light. The painting fuses the two, and Plato needs that ambiguity. His liberator must compel the turning (515c–e), and his philosopher-rulers are the most institutional figures imaginable (519c–520c). From the table, the pedagogy that frees and the pedagogy that confines look identical. I take that to be the picture's sharpest point.

The fourth is posture. Wright painted wonder: children's open faces, the *thaumazein* that begins philosophy (*Theaetetus* 155d). Here there are no children. Adult professionals lean on fists and forearms, appraising rather than astonished. One man transcribes the model onto a tablet, making a model of the model. The composition is horizontal: table, slats, the white band of the window. Only the presenter stands. Plato's ascent is a rough, steep drag upward (515e); here nobody climbs.

**What the daylight means**

The daylight is where the reading must correct the obvious iconography. If the window simply meant sensory experience ("stop modelling, go and look"), the picture would be anti-Platonic. Two features resist that.

First, the cold beam is less a view than a crack. Nothing outside can be seen, only light. It angles into the room and fades in the air above the presenter's outstretched hand, touching no one. Second, the painting includes a mocking "upward" of its own: a large ring hanging from the ceiling, apparently an unlit fixture, a perfect circle that nobody regards. It recalls Socrates' joke at 529b that gazing at ceiling decorations is not the soul looking up. Literal upward or outward looking is not the ascent.

The window is where the painting must show, by visible means, what the model cannot contain: its own principle. A painter can signal the unhypothetical only through something visible, so daylight is the sign, not the destination. Plato would add that untrained eyes could not use it anyway (515e–516a). That makes its neglect a measure of where these people stand rather than a simple failure of curiosity.

**The Chinese authorship**

I would resist the quickest reading, in which the blinds are censorship and the slit of light is forbidden truth. The picture has no chains and no enforcer, the blinds were drawn from inside, and the room is global: the suit-and-badge interior of any ministry, bank or consultancy, with Wright's cast largely retained.

What the authorship plausibly changes is the history the orrery carries. For a Chinese painter, Wright's canvas is an icon of the Enlightenment science China was repeatedly told it lacked. That history runs from the Jesuit astronomers at the Ming and Qing courts, through Macartney's 1793 embassy, whose showpiece gift was a planetarium, to May Fourth's call for "Mr. Science." The Macartney episode was long told as China closing its door to modern science, a story later historians have complicated. The painting asks what became of that light once it was acquired: it became a lamp on a boardroom table, in a room that belongs to no single country. Socrates says the prisoners are "like us" (515a), and the picture leaves "us" deliberately wide. A Chinese viewer might also notice a round heaven over a square earth (*tian yuan di fang*) in the circular orrery on its rectangular table, though I wouldn't lean on that.

The authorship matters more for method. Reworking Wright belongs to the tradition of *fang* (仿), painting "after" an old master. Literati theory from Dong Qichang onward treated this as thinking through a predecessor rather than copying him. There is also a recent precedent for the specific device: Zeng Fanzhi's *Last Supper* (2001) dressed Leonardo's disciples in Young Pioneer scarves and gave Judas a Western yellow tie. Here the ties and lanyards do similar work.

This doubles your tension inside the work itself. The painting uses its own model, Wright, as a springboard in the way 510d and 511b allow, while depicting people who use theirs as a resting place.

**What the reading leaves unresolved**

First, the window is still daylight. My reading turns it into a sign of the unhypothetical, but nothing in the image forces that over the empiricist reading: leave the model and look at the world the meeting is about. Both lie "outside the model," and a visible medium cannot tell them apart. I resolve the tension by interpretation, not by evidence the picture supplies.

Second, release. There is no door, no stair, and no one moving. Because the presenter could be liberator or puppeteer, the picture has no unambiguous agent of release. It diagnoses rather than narrates. If anyone is invited out, it is the viewer, but we stand in the dark behind the bowed foreground figure, whose face and gaze we cannot read.

Third, the painting's own standing. By Book X, a painting of a model is at best third from the truth (597e). *Fang* implies a different, non-Platonic view of imitation, so the work may be staging that disagreement rather than settling it.

Fourth, affect. The painter lavished care on the warm interior, the brass, the flame and the faces, while the daylight gets a few cold strokes. Like Wright's original, the picture may love its demonstration more than its argument permits. Plato himself grants that the heavens are beautifully wrought, so it remains open whether the model's beauty is the trap or a genuine glimpse of intelligible order.


# To which philosophical system do you think the artist belongs?

I can't know which system the artist belongs to, and "belongs" is probably too strong for most painters. A single image can show an affinity, not a membership. With that caveat, my best guess is that the artist is not a Platonist. They seem closest to a position that runs from **Heidegger's critique of the modern "world picture" to its Chinese reworking in Yuk Hui's idea of "cosmotechnics."** I'd also avoid assuming that a contemporary Chinese painter must be Confucian or Daoist. Many are steeped in Western theory, so the evidence has to come from the picture.

**Why not Plato, or a few other candidates**

Plato's educational ladder is in the picture, but the painting doesn't trust it. There is no liberator anyone could identify. The outside is shown as unmade light rather than form. The painting's own method, reworking Wright "after" the master, treats models more generously than Book X does.

Baudrillard's simulation, where the model comes before and replaces the real, fits the boardroom but fails on one detail: the beam of daylight survives. For Baudrillard there would be no outside left to leak in.

Zhuangzi offers a tempting parallel: the gardener who refuses a labour-saving device because machines breed a "machine heart." But that reading would condemn the instrument, and the painter clearly loves it. The brass and the flame get far more care than anything else in the room.

**The Heideggerian features**

Several features fit Heidegger closely. In "The Age of the World Picture" (1938), he does not mean a picture *of* the world. He means the modern condition in which the world itself is grasped as something set up before a representing subject, arranged and lit for inspection. A room with the blinds drawn and a lamp-lit cosmos at its centre stages that condition almost literally.

The same essay describes science turning into an organised enterprise (*Betrieb*). The scholar gives way to the research professional who moves between meetings, projects and conferences. Wright's natural philosopher wearing a lanyard is that transformation made visible.

Heidegger's "Plato's Doctrine of Truth" also changes the tension you raised at the start. He reads the Cave as the moment truth shifts from unconcealment to correct looking at the *idea*, the "look" or form. On that view the orrery is not a misuse of Platonism but where Platonism leads. A Heideggerian painter would be using Plato's image against Plato on purpose.

**Why Hui fits better than Heidegger alone**

Heidegger alone can't explain why a Chinese painter would choose this particular object. Hui's cosmotechnics, in *The Question Concerning Technology in China* (2016), can. Hui argues that technologies are not neutral. Each carries a cosmology, a way of joining cosmic order and moral order through making.

He builds on a line from the Great Commentary of the Yijing: what is above form is called *dao*, and what is below form is called *qi*, the instrument or vessel. (The modern Chinese word for metaphysics, *xing'ershang xue*, comes from this line.) Hui also argues that the late-Qing formula "Chinese learning as substance, Western learning as function" failed, because the borrowed instruments brought their own substance with them.

Read this way, the orrery is a *qi* cut off from its *dao*. It is a European clockwork cosmos, the emblem of a modernity China was told to import, now set up in a placeless global room and lit by a flame made inside the room. My earlier distinction between the made light and the unmade light maps naturally onto the old Chinese contrast between what is human and what is heaven-given. Even the armillary rings matter here: China had armillary spheres for well over a millennium, and they were political and ritual instruments, since the calendar belonged to the emperor.

**What this does to the original tension**

On this reading, the tension with Plato is not a flaw the interpreter has to manage. It is the point. The painting borrows the Cave's structure while denying its premise that the way out leads up to intelligible form. The problem in the room is neither illusion nor even the stalled ascent I described before. It is an instrument that has lost touch with what it was an instrument of.

**What would change my mind**

Titles, statements, or other works would settle this quickly. A series about Wang Yangming would favour a different reading: as a young man he spent days staring at bamboo to grasp its principle and fell ill, which made him reject looking for principle in external things. That story fits the staring figures uncomfortably well. Equally, an explicitly Platonic body of work would push me back toward the reading I gave earlier.

# Have you considered the possibility that the artist might be using irony in relation to Plato’s concept? Note that the painting employs postmodern techniques (a classical subject rendered in a contemporary form) which opens the door to irony.


I considered it only partly. I touched on irony when I suggested the painter might be turning Plato's image against Plato, but I treated the painting as a sincere argument. I didn't ask whether irony might be its governing mode. That's a fair challenge, and I think it changes the picture more than I allowed.

One qualification about the premise first. Putting a classical subject into contemporary form opens the door to irony but doesn't walk through it. Renaissance painters set biblical scenes among people in Florentine dress without irony. Wright's original already did something similar: it gave a contemporary science lecture the gravity of history painting, borrowing (as art historians often note) the lighting of candlelit Nativity scenes. Charles Jencks called this kind of thing "double coding": a work speaks in two registers at once. Double coding permits irony without requiring it. So the evidence has to come from signals inside the picture.

**Signals in the picture**

Wayne Booth's *A Rhetoric of Irony* lists the usual clues: clashes of style, conflicts of fact within the work, and warnings in the author's own voice, such as a title. We lack a title, but the first two clues are clearly present:

- **Clashes of style:** old-master chiaroscuro sits beside a tablet and stylus, and an oil lamp burns in a boardroom whose modern ceiling light is switched off.
- **Conflicts of fact:** an eighteenth-century orrery stands on a conference table, and the badges certify membership while bearing no names.

There are also signs of overdetermination, which often marks deadpan irony. At least four figures strike variations of the same chin-on-fist pose, a catalogue of pensiveness that starts to look staged. The presenter's mane of white hair is pushed toward the stock image of genius. The daylight arrives as a hard, almost laser-like streak rather than a natural shaft. Wright's sacred Nativity light has become a slide presentation. The case for irony is stronger than I acknowledged.

The Chinese context strengthens it further. The most visible tradition in Chinese painting since the 1990s is deadpan irony executed with high finish: the Cynical Realism of Fang Lijun and Yue Minjun, and the Political Pop of Wang Guangyi, both named by the critic Li Xianting. Zeng Fanzhi's *Last Supper*, which I cited earlier, is usually read in that spirit.

**Three possible targets**

The decisive question is what the irony is aimed at. There are three main candidates, and they lead to quite different readings.

**The institution.** The painting could be mocking corporate and technocratic culture for dressing itself in the aura of the Enlightenment and the Academy. That is stable irony in Booth's sense, with a recoverable meaning. Plato still serves as the measuring stick, so this mostly confirms my earlier readings rather than displacing them.

**Plato himself.** The Cave and Wright's picture are both versions of what Lyotard called the narrative of emancipation, the story that knowledge sets us free. Postmodernism, in his account, is incredulity toward such stories. In their place comes "performativity," knowledge legitimated by efficiency inside institutions, which describes the boardroom very well. On this reading, the beam of light is not a way out but just glare through the blinds. Your original tension disappears differently than I suggested: the painting needs no reconciliation with Plato's educational programme because it doesn't believe in it.

**The interpreter.** The Cave allegory always flatters whoever invokes it. To spot "prisoners," you have to imagine yourself already outside. The painting puts the viewer in the dark, behind the bowed foreground figure, and offers a scene that invites exactly the elaborate decoding I supplied: Stephanus numbers, the divided Line, cosmotechnics. An ironic painting would make my interpretations one more lamp-lit model passed around a table. I should take that seriously rather than exempt myself from it.

**The reading I find most convincing**

I think the most convincing version combines these, in what Linda Hutcheon calls postmodern parody's "complicitous critique": the work installs what it subverts. Pure irony doesn't fit a painting that lavishes this much love on the brass and the flame. Instead the painter can mean the Cave and keep it in quotation marks at the same time, admiring the instrument while doubting the story of escape it once served.

This doesn't make the painting un-Platonic. When Socrates explains the allegory, he adds that only a god knows whether it is true (517b). The Cave was told by an ironist from the start, so irony toward it may be a kind of fidelity.

**How this revises my earlier answers**

Allowing for irony changes my previous answers in one respect. If the governing mode is ironic, the question of which philosophical "system" the artist belongs to becomes less apt. An ironist's commitment is closer to a stance than a doctrine, and the Heidegger-to-Hui reading I offered would be the earnest version of a position the painting might itself hold at arm's length.

The risk is the familiar one: once irony is admitted, any sincere feature can be redescribed as deadpan, and no evidence can refute that. A title, an artist's statement, or the rest of the painter's work would settle more than any further close reading of this canvas.
