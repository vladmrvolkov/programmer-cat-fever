My reading is that the picture doesn't show prisoners who mistake shadows for things. It shows people who have already turned away from the shadows, found the fire and the artifacts, and settled there. I'd call this orbit without conversion. Pelevin supplies the reason this is still a cave (knowing that it's a model is no exit, and may be the most comfortable cell). He also supplies the doubt that keeps the picture from being a sermon: the light at the window may be one more apparatus.

**Where in the cave we are**

The objection assumes these figures stand for the chained prisoners at the wall. But Plato's cave has more in it than shadows. There is a fire behind a low wall, and carriers parade artifacts past it: figures of men and animals made of stone and wood (514b–515a). The released prisoner is first turned around and shown these artifacts themselves (515c–d), and only later dragged up the ascent. The orrery fits that station exactly. It is a crafted replica of real things, lit by a flame that stands in for the sun.

Wright's full title already names the substitution: *in which a lamp is put in place of the Sun*. Plato names it too. When he recapitulates at 532b–c, he describes the cave's images as cast by "another light" which, judged against the sun, is itself a stand-in. So "they know it's a model" is no objection to the Cave reading. At this station, knowing that you're looking at a model is precisely what you know. The prisoner who has seen the puppets is better informed than the one watching shadows, and still underground.

**What, then, is wrong with them**

Your citations point to the answer. Plato's test is not which object you look at but which way the looking runs. Geometers draw a square in order to reason about the square itself (510d–e), so the visible figure points away from itself. At 529d–e, the visible heavens are compared to diagrams by Daedalus: superb workmanship that a geometer would admire, but would think it absurd to study for the truth of ratios. Hence 530b: leave the things in the heavens alone. The same brass orrery could serve dianoia or Daedalian fascination. The image signals which one through composition and light, not through the object.

**The composition is a second orrery**

The planets circle the flame. The people sit in a ring around the table at different distances, every face turned toward the center. Above them, the room's own ring-shaped ceiling light hangs dark, a third circle, and the armillary arcs add more. Everything in the picture revolves, and nobody turns.

Plato's word for education is periagoge, turning the whole soul around (518c–d). An orbit is the exact visual opposite: endless motion bound to a center, held by attraction rather than chains. This is how the image can make model-study a confinement without claiming that the model lies. The captivity is gravitational, not epistemic. The faces are lit from below by the flame, so they literally take their light from the model.

The individual figures reinforce this:

- **The postures** (chin on hand, fist at the mouth) are postures of thought, and thought is not the problem. They are also completely still.
- **The young woman in red**, Wright's wondering child grown up, leans toward the glowing spheres in pure fascination.
- **The note-taker** doesn't look at the model at all. He looks at his tablet, producing a copy of a copy.
- **The polished table** mirrors the glow. Reflections, which in Plato belong to the first stage *outside* the cave (516a), are here manufactured inside, in varnish.

**The light**

Plato's cave has its entrance open to the light (514a). Nothing keeps the prisoners in but chains and habit. Here the opening has been reduced to a slit in closed blinds, so the exclusion is architectural and deliberate. The beam enters behind the right-hand group, which is the cave's geometry: light at the prisoners' backs. It dies in the air before it reaches the table. The woman nearest the window looks the other way, toward the lecturer.

Meanwhile the modern room's electric light is switched off. The institution has chosen the antique fire and staged an Enlightenment séance. In Wright's painting the lamp is hidden behind a silhouetted boy. Here the flame is in plain view, and the silhouette has become a hunched adult at lower right with his back to us, hiding nothing. The mechanism is exposed and the spell holds anyway. That is the Pelevinian heart of the picture.

**The sensory problem**

You're right that Plato would not send anyone to the window to check the weather. By 517b, the literal sun belongs to the cave: the fire is "the power of the sun" in the visible region. So the beam cannot mean that sense experience beats models. It stands for what lies beyond the model, what the model is a model *of*.

A painting can show that only as light, just as Plato could present the Good only as the sun, and his whole teaching only as an image (517a–b). The real opposition in the picture is between a model treated as sufficient and a model treated as a threshold. The orrery ought to be a window. Instead, the room has turned its actual window into a wall.

**The lecturer and the badges**

Wright's philosopher is a benign demonstrator. This one stands alone, one hand resting on the armillary ring and the other holding what looks like a small remote or presentation clicker, his eyes down on the apparatus. That places him somewhere between two figures in Plato:

- the returned philosopher, who must go back down and teach in the cave's own terms (520c);
- the carriers behind the wall, who move the artifacts (514b–515b).

He also wears a lanyard and an ID badge, like everyone else, and every badge is blank. Wright's domestic, amateur science has become an institutional session: boardroom table, suits, tablet. Credentials have replaced chains. What keeps you at the table is that you belong to it, and understanding the model is the price of the badge.

**Pelevin**

This is where your objection becomes the point. Plato's prisoners are captive because they don't know. Pelevin's are often captive because they *do* know:

- **Generation P.** The copywriters and technologists who manufacture digital politicians know perfectly well that they are models, and knowing it only draws them deeper in. The Pelevinian cave's error is not "the shadow is real" but "the model is all there is," or "the model is enough."
- **Omon Ra.** The cosmos gets its own version: the space programme is staged underground, and the lunar landscape turns out to be a metro tunnel. It is a cosmos you can travel through without leaving the building.
- **The Recluse and Six-Toes.** This is perhaps the closest relative of this picture. Broiler chickens take the factory lamps for their heavens until two outcasts discover the windows.
- **Chapaev and Void.** This adds the darker note. Liberation there is not reaching the truer of two worlds but seeing that both are projections.

That is why the beam here doesn't look like daylight. It is a hard, cold, bluish blade that fans out more like a projector beam than like Wright-era sunshine. The exit is offered and doubted in the same stroke.

**How this resolves your tension**

The image doesn't contradict Plato's educational programme. It depicts the hinge where that programme fails. Everything the ascent requires is in the room: a mathematical model of the heavens, a teacher, attentive students, a ray of outside light. What's missing is the turning. The model has become the center rather than the threshold, and an institution has formed around keeping it so. This is why a straightforward illustration feels strained. The image applies the Cave to a case Plato didn't treat: not ignorant prisoners, but initiated ones.

**What the reading leaves unresolved**

First, whether there is an outside at all. By Plato's own key at 517b, every visible light is cave-fire, so a picture can never show an exit, only another light. The image leaves undecided whether the beam is the sun (Plato: there is a Good beyond all images) or another projector (Pelevin's Buddhism: the hope of a final outside is itself a frame). My reading leans on both views and cannot choose between them.

Second, the inward difference cannot be painted. Dianoetic use of a diagram and Daedalian fascination look identical from outside: a rapt face, a hand at the chin. My verdict of captivity rests on circumstantial evidence (the blinds, the dark ceiling ring, the unanimity), not on anything in the faces. Plato himself, in *Timaeus* 47b–c, says sight was given to us so that we could watch the heavenly revolutions and correct the revolutions of our own thought by them. On that view, circling a model of the cosmos is discipline, not captivity. My orbit metaphor has to override a Platonic text.

Third, the lecturer stays ambiguous: guide or operator, returned philosopher or puppeteer with a clicker.

Fourth, the target is unclear. It could be Wright's Enlightenment itself, whose lamp-for-sun was always cave-fire, or that Enlightenment's corporate afterlife. The image fuses the two eras and doesn't say which.

Last, reflexivity. We look out of the dark at a glowing picture, over the shoulder of the dark foreground figure, and an interpretation like this one is just one more circuit around a lit model. The image may count its viewers among the prisoners, and today's sense of the word "model" pushes the joke a step further. If the picture was itself produced with generative tools, as its finish suggests, it pushes further still. The image leaves that question with the viewer, which may be the most Pelevinian thing about it.
