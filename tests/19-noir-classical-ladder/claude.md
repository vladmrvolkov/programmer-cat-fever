# What is happening in these pages? Tell the story in your own words.

It's a short, deadpan comedy about a talking cat who gets mistaken for a novelty act when she sees herself as a professional.

**Page 1.** On a rainy night in the city, a sleek spotted cat in a business suit, with a lanyard ID badge, gold hoop earrings, and a leather briefcase, walks into a cozy, old-fashioned café. It has a Japanese flavor: a coffee sign in kanji, a black beckoning-cat figurine on the counter, and folding screens painted with circus scenes. She sits down and politely orders coffee with cream and a pastry. The older waiter, who has been polishing glasses, just stares and flatly points out that she can talk. A close-up of her half-lidded, unimpressed eyes shows her reaction: yes, obviously she's a cat, and is he going to bring her order or not? He brings the coffee and a round white sweet that looks like mochi, remarks that she doesn't even have an accent, and makes a pitch: his uncle runs a circus, and she'd get room, board, and her travel paid.

**Page 2.** She leans back, chin in paw, and repeats the offer back to him: the acrobats, the jugglers, the sweets. Behind her, one of the painted screens shows a cat in a kimono swinging from a trapeze, which is exactly the job he has in mind. The waiter is already on the phone behind the counter, excitedly telling his uncle that she talks and even wears a suit. Meanwhile she has opened her laptop and gone back to work. She says the offer sounds tempting, but she can't imagine what a circus would need a *programmer* for. The last panel is wordless: the phone receiver lies off the hook on the empty counter, the waiter is gone, and she keeps typing alone in the quiet café as the rain falls.

**Why it works.** The joke is two people talking past each other, both completely unfazed. The waiter's response to a talking cat isn't wonder but opportunism: he instantly sees a sideshow act. She's so secure in her identity as a working professional that she either doesn't register the insult or is dryly refusing to entertain it. She assumes any job offer must be for her actual skills. That makes the title, "A Career Opportunity," ironic, and the circus screens act as a visual punchline hiding in plain sight from the first panel. The ending, with the call left hanging and the cat unbothered, suggests the waiter's big idea just fizzled out.

# What does the waiter stand to gain, and whom is he working for?

The comic never says outright, but the panels offer a few clues.

His official job is at the café. He's behind the counter, taking orders and polishing glasses. As soon as she speaks, though, he starts acting as a talent scout for his uncle. His pitch is oddly specific (room, board, and travel paid), as if those terms were already agreed on, and after serving her he goes straight to the phone. We only hear his side ("She talks. Suit and everything."), and the comic never shows who is on the other end. I made it the uncle in my continuation, but that was my invention, not the artist's.

The most likely payoff is a finder's fee or standing in the family business. Delivering a real talking cat to a circus would be a coup. The café's decor hints at a deeper connection: the painted screens show exactly the acts he describes, including a cat on a trapeze. So either the café is linked to the circus, or someone has been hoping for a cat like her for a long time. That might also explain his flat reaction to a talking cat. He doesn't act surprised so much as ready.

The black beckoning cat adds a symbolic layer. It appears beside him at the counter and again when he makes the phone call. It's a lucky charm that businesses display to draw in customers and fortune. Next to a figurine lucky cat, he's trying to recruit a real one, so in that reading, what he wants is luck itself.

The noir mood allows for a darker interpretation too: the rainy night, the man in the dark hat at the bar in the first panel, the hushed phone call, and the receiver left off the hook. You could read the waiter as a small player reporting to someone bigger than an uncle with a circus. The comic doesn't push that reading, but it leaves room for it.

# What happens to her if she accepts? If she refuses?

Since the comic leaves both paths open, this is speculation, but the details point in some interesting directions.

**If she accepts.** Look closely at the offer: room, board, and travel, but no wage. She would be trading her independence, and apparently her career, for a place to live and a spot in the show. The painted screens hint at what that life looks like. The cat on the trapeze, in a kimono and swinging for the crowd, could be her future self or a predecessor who took the same deal. The screens are decorations, and a cat who joined the circus would become one too: admired and on display, but no longer the woman with the briefcase and the ID badge. Her suit and lanyard mark her as someone who has built her own life, and the offer asks her to give that up.

**If she refuses.** In the lighthearted reading, nothing much happens. She finishes her pastry, the waiter sulks, and maybe her next refill takes longer. The noir details suggest refusing may not end things, though. The phone call has already gone out, and "She talks. Suit and everything" works less like gossip than like a description passed to someone who will look for her. Her badge could identify where she works. The man in the hat is still at the bar. Once someone knows a talking cat exists, a polite no may just mean they try a different approach.

The comic's own answer seems to be a third path. She never quite accepts or refuses. She turns the offer into a question about her real skills and goes back to work. That's why the ending feels like a small victory: whatever the waiter and his uncle had in mind, she has recast herself as the one being recruited for what she can do, not for what she is.

# Is she in danger? Is anything illegal being proposed? What in the pictures or the words shows it?

Not really, and nothing illegal is proposed. The noir readings I offered earlier come from mood, not from anything the comic actually shows.

**The words.** The waiter's offer is ordinary: his uncle runs a circus, and she'd get room, board, and her fare paid. Traveling circuses have long hired performers on those terms, so nothing in it is a crime. The one real gap is that he never mentions pay. In real life, a stranger offering lodging and travel for a job far away, with no wage stated, is a known warning sign of labor exploitation. Here, though, it reads more like a waiter giving the highlights than a trap, and nothing suggests deception or pressure. His phone call ("She talks. Suit and everything.") is made openly at the counter while she sits in the room, and it fits his stated plan of telling his uncle. That's not how a conspirator behaves.

**The pictures.** Nothing in the art is menacing. The waiter looks tired and deadpan, not threatening, and he does his actual job: he takes her order and brings it. The man in the hat and the man with the newspaper are background customers with their backs turned, and I read too much into them earlier. The lighting is warm and golden, more cozy than sinister. The receiver left off the hook works best as a comic beat, since he seems to have wandered off mid-call. Most tellingly, her body language never shows fear. She's composed, bored, and a little sarcastic, and in the last panel she's alone in an empty café, relaxed and typing with half-closed eyes.

**The real tension** is about dignity, not safety. The waiter treats her as an attraction. He's surprised that she talks and that she has no accent, and his first thought is to put her on display. The unanswered question the comic hints at is whether a talking cat would be treated as an employee or as an animal act. If it's the latter, she might have none of the protections a person would expect. That's the edge beneath the joke, and her reply about being a programmer is how she pushes back: she insists on being valued for her work rather than her novelty.
