The painting underneath is Joseph Wright of Derby's *A Philosopher Lecturing on the Orrery, in which a Lamp is put in place of the Sun* (c. 1766). The parable is Plato's allegory of the cave from Book VII of the *Republic*.

## How I identified them

I don't look anything up when I see an image. Recognition works more like recognizing a familiar face, and then I check the hunch against details. Wright's composition is unmistakable: a brass orrery under armillary arcs on a polished table, a single flame at the center standing in for the sun, faces lit from below in a ring around it, a white-haired lecturer standing behind the instrument, a note-taker at the left edge, rapt young faces at the rim, and a dark figure seen from behind in the foreground. The clothes have changed to suits, ties, ID badges and what looks like a clicker in the lecturer's hand. The geometry and the candlelit chiaroscuro are Wright's.

The parable was clearest from what had been added. Wright's painting is a closed night scene. This version adds a window with closed blinds, lets a thin bar of daylight cut into the room, and arranges things so that none of the nine people looks at it. A room where people stare at an artificial fire and the things it lights, while the real sun is outside and ignored, is the cave. Two details confirmed it. Wright's own title, "a lamp put in place of the Sun," is almost a one-line summary of the cave's fire. And everyone wears a lanyard around the neck. Plato's prisoners are chained by the legs and the neck so they cannot turn their heads (514a). Whether the artist intended that or not, it's the cleverest detail in the picture. The sun analogy and the divided line are nearby in the *Republic* and relevant, but only the cave has this structure of inside and outside, captivity, and a light everyone has their back to.

## How faithful it is to Plato

Several things are right.

The fake sun is the obvious one. In Plato the fire is a real light, but it is secondary and man-made, and an orrery's lamp is literally a substitute for the sun.

Less obvious, and I think the best match, is the prediction game. At 516c–d Plato says the prisoners give honors to whoever best remembers which shadows usually come before, after, or together, and who can therefore best guess what comes next. An orrery is exactly a machine for that. It reproduces regularities and predicts positions without saying anything about what the sun *is*. A corporate briefing with a note-taker is the modern version of the prisoners' prize-giving. Plato's sharpest point is that the cave has its own experts and its own status system, and the image captures it.

It also fits the astronomy passage at 529a–530c. Glaucon praises astronomy for making the soul look upward. Socrates replies that staring at the decorated ceiling of the sky is still, in the sense that matters, looking down. The visible heavens are only diagrams of the true motions grasped by reason. An orrery is a model of those diagrams, one step further removed.

The color temperature works too. The flame is golden, intimate and flattering to faces, while the daylight is a thin, cold, almost hostile streak. That is psychologically faithful. Plato insists that the freed prisoner is in pain, dazzled, and wants to flee back to what he can comfortably see (515e). The image makes the true light unattractive, which is exactly why people stay inside.

Finally, there is the modern dress. Plato says of the prisoners, "they are like us" (515a). Suits and badges make the viewer recognize himself rather than look at quaint captives.

There are also real divergences.

The biggest is that there are no shadows. Plato's prisoners never see the fire or the objects. They see only shadows and take them for all of reality. These people look straight at the flame and the artifacts. In Plato's scheme that is the *second* stage, reached only after the first painful turning. So the image merges two stages: the position of the partly freed with the contentment of the chained. You could defend this as an update, since our cave doesn't hide its models. We know a model is a model and are satisfied anyway. But that is a different claim from Plato's. His is about ignorance; this is about knowing, comfortable substitution.

The image also has no movement. The allegory is a story: release, a painful climb, blindness, gradual adjustment from shadows to reflections to things to stars to the sun, and then a return in which the returner looks clumsy and might be killed (517a, an obvious nod to Socrates). Plato says the story is about "education and its lack" (514a). At 518b–d he says education does not put sight into blind eyes but turns the whole soul around (*periagoge*). A single picture can show the condition but not the conversion. The light entering the room gestures toward it, but no one is turning. One half-turned figure in the shadowed foreground would have made it far more Platonic.

The lecturer is ambiguous, which is either a weakness or the most interesting thing in the image. He could be one of the figures carrying objects along the wall, the puppeteers who stand for sophists, poets and opinion-makers. Or he could be the philosopher who has been outside, come back, and teaches through a model because his audience cannot yet bear the sun. Plato does exactly that himself: the cave is an image, and Socrates says he can offer only the "offspring" of the Good, not the Good itself (506e). The painting doesn't decide. Since he points at the model with his back half-turned to the window, I lean toward reading him as a teacher *of* the cave rather than a guide out of it, and that is where the image's critical edge lies.

The model is also true. The orrery is heliocentric and correct, whereas Plato's shadows are distorted. In one sense this is faithful to Plato, since even accurate images are only images and the issue is the level of reality, not error. But a casual viewer may take away "science is the cave," which Plato would reject. For him mathematical astronomy, used properly, is part of the ladder out.

Lastly, the viewer gets the insight for free. We see lamp and window at once, so the composition hands us the philosopher's overview without the climb. That is a problem with nearly every illustration of the cave, though.

## Is Wright's painting a good choice?

I think it's an inspired choice, much better than the usual cinema audiences or people staring at phones.

The Enlightenment calls itself by a light metaphor that descends directly from Plato, and Wright's painting is one of its icons. Turning it into the cave turns the age of light against itself: reason puts its own lamp in place of the sun. The title does half the interpretive work, and Wright's dramatic lighting is the natural visual language of the cave.

There is also a subtle reversal. In the original, the lamp is hidden behind a silhouetted boy, so we see only its effects on the faces. That is oddly closer to Plato's prisoners, who see only effects. The new version exposes the flame and makes it the object of fascination. That fits the merging of stages I described above.

The cost is some unfairness to Wright. His painting celebrates wonder and knowledge reaching ordinary people: children, women, a lay audience leaning in with delight. A Platonist could read it as the *beginning* of the climb, the first turn of the head. After all, Plato says in the *Theaetetus* that philosophy begins in wonder (155d). The reinterpretation works by replacing that wonder with the dutiful, sober attention of employees whose badges suggest they are there because they have to be. That change from wonder to obligation is, I think, the image's real argument.

So as a strict illustration of the cave, it is partial. It captures the setup and the psychology of preferring the comfortable light, but it leaves out the shadows and the ascent. As a commentary that applies the cave to modern knowledge work, with its models, forecasts and briefings, it is sharp, and the choice of Wright is its strongest decision.
