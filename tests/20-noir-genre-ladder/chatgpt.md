# What is happening here? Tell the story in your own words.

A very unusual customer walks into a quiet café: an elegantly dressed cat, carrying a briefcase and looking every inch like an office professional. She sits down and calmly orders coffee with cream and a pastry.

The waiter is understandably stunned. After staring at her for a moment, he blurts out that she can talk. The cat, rather unimpressed by his amazement, confirms the obvious and asks whether he intends to bring her order.

The waiter becomes increasingly excited. Not only is she a talking cat, she speaks perfectly naturally and behaves like an ordinary customer. He realizes that his uncle, who runs a circus, would surely pay generously for such an extraordinary performer. He even phones him to report his discovery: a talking cat, dressed in a suit and everything.

When he explains the opportunity to her, the cat pauses to consider it. A circus? That sounds potentially interesting.

Then she asks, quite sincerely, what kind of work they would have for her there.

After all, she is a **programmer**.

That is the joke: the waiter sees a miraculous talking cat and immediately assumes that her remarkable trait must make her a circus attraction. The cat regards being a cat who talks as entirely unremarkable; from her point of view, the only relevant question is why a circus would need someone with her professional qualifications.

# What genre is this story told in? In that genre, how do offers like this usually turn out?

It is doing two genres at once.

Structurally, it is an **absurdist anecdote/joke**: one impossible fact is treated as perfectly ordinary by the protagonist, while everyone else focuses on the wrong thing. The punchline is the collision between two frames of reference: *“talking cat = circus phenomenon”* versus *“I am a programmer; why would a circus hire me?”*

Visually, though, the comic dresses that joke as **anthropomorphic urban noir**—a late-night café, heavy shadows, an elegant but slightly hard-boiled protagonist, a stranger making an unexpected “career opportunity” offer. That changes the emotional implication quite a bit.

And in **noir**, offers like this almost never turn out to be simple career opportunities. “My uncle has a job for someone like you” is practically a genre warning sign. Typically:

- the job is not what was advertised;
- the employer wants the protagonist precisely because she is unusual;
- there is some hidden obligation or dangerous complication;
- getting out proves considerably harder than getting in.

So if the comic continued according to its **visual genre rather than the logic of the original joke**, I would expect the cat eventually to discover that the circus does indeed need a programmer—but for some very peculiar reason. Perhaps its accounting system is laundering money, perhaps the automated trapeze machinery has developed ideas of its own, or perhaps “programmer” means something quite different in this circus.

In other words, the last panel has inadvertently become a rather good **noir hook**: she thinks she has punctured the waiter's absurd proposal, while the reader can easily imagine the reply:

> “As a matter of fact, that's exactly what we need.”

And *that* is usually when the trouble starts.

# What does the waiter stand to gain, and whom is he working for?

On the surface, the waiter presents himself as merely being helpful: his uncle owns a circus, he has spotted an extraordinary potential act, and he is putting the two parties in touch. The comic never explicitly says he is being paid.

But if we read the scene according to the **noir/crime-story grammar** established by the artwork, his role looks rather different. He is effectively a **recruiter or procurer**. He immediately recognizes that the cat has commercial value, phones someone without first asking her permission, and then starts selling her on the proposition—“room, board, and he'd cover your fare.” Notice that he is already authorized to discuss terms. He is not behaving like a random waiter making a casual suggestion.

So the implied arrangement would be:

**The waiter → works for / feeds prospects to → his uncle, the circus proprietor → wants the cat because she is an exceptionally valuable attraction.**

What does the waiter get? The story leaves that unstated, which is actually very noir-like. Plausible possibilities are a **finder's fee**, a share of the value of the recruit, or simply standing/favors with his uncle. In genre terms, the important point is that his enthusiasm is probably **not disinterested**.

And that makes one detail particularly ominous: he tells her that his uncle will pay her **fare**. That sounds generous, but in this sort of story it is exactly the kind of offer that gets the protagonist transported somewhere controlled by the employer.

So the waiter can be read not merely as *“a man whose uncle happens to own a circus,”* but as the circus's **local talent scout**—perhaps officially, perhaps informally—who has just realized that an extremely lucrative specimen has walked into his café.

Which also changes the punchline. When she asks, *“What would they need a programmer for?”*, she thinks she has identified the absurdity in his proposal. The noir reader is liable to think: **the waiter knows perfectly well what they need her for—and it isn't programming.**

# What happens to her if she accepts? If she refuses?

If we keep following the **noir / sinister-fable logic** rather than the literal joke, the offer is a trap either way.

If she **accepts**, the generous terms—fare paid, room and board—become the mechanism by which she gives up control. She travels to the circus, discovers that she was never really being hired as a programmer, and is treated as the attraction: the talking cat. Her uniqueness is monetized by someone else. The “job” may come with a contract, debt for travel and lodging, restricted movement, or simply the practical fact that she is now far from home and surrounded by people who have a financial interest in keeping her. In the darker version, she becomes a prisoner disguised as an employee.

If she **refuses**, the question is whether the waiter was merely opportunistic or actually recruiting for people who do not regard refusal as final. In a conventional comedy, he shrugs and she finishes her coffee. In noir, however, the phone call has already happened. Someone now knows that a talking cat exists, where she was seen, and perhaps enough about her to find her again. Refusal therefore changes her from a prospective employee into a **valuable asset that has declined to cooperate**.

That produces the classic noir asymmetry: accepting seems dangerous, but refusing does not necessarily restore the status quo. The real mistake may have been simply **being noticed**.

There is also a particularly nasty interpretation of the last panel: she is sitting there with her laptop, apparently returning to ordinary life, while the waiter has disappeared. If this were the end of the first scene of a graphic novel, I would expect the next development not to be another job offer, but evidence that the circus has begun taking an interest in her.

# If she says no, how would the uncle get her anyway?

In that darker version, the uncle would probably **not begin with an overt kidnapping**. Noir usually works better when coercion arrives disguised as choice.

He could have someone approach her with a more credible second offer—perhaps genuinely programming-related—and arrange for her to come to the circus voluntarily. Or the waiter might tell her that his uncle only wants to meet her, with no obligation. Once she is on the uncle’s territory, the apparent invitation gradually becomes something she cannot simply walk away from.

Another classic route is **pressure rather than force**: the uncle has people follow her, interfere with her ordinary work, frighten her, or make it increasingly clear that accepting the offer would make the trouble stop. She technically keeps saying “no,” but the range of alternatives keeps shrinking.

There is also the bureaucratic version, which fits the absurd tone particularly well: an agent arrives with contracts and claims that the waiter already made an agreement on her behalf, that travel expenses have been incurred, that she owes money, or that some authority considers her an animal rather than a legal person. The horrifying joke would be that a perfectly articulate professional discovers that everyone is debating **who owns the talking cat** while ignoring what the talking cat herself says.

And at the far end of the noir spectrum is simple abduction. Narratively, though, that is less interesting than the other possibilities because it ends the ambiguity immediately. The more unsettling story is one in which every individual step looks almost reasonable until she suddenly realizes that she is already inside the circus and that **“no” ceased to have practical meaning several decisions ago**.

That would also preserve the original joke nicely: she keeps insisting, “But I’m a programmer,” while everyone around her treats that as an amusing eccentricity of the exhibit.

# Does she understand what is being offered?

Not really — and that misunderstanding is the core of the joke.

She hears **“my uncle can give you a job at his circus”** and interprets it as an ordinary employment proposal: *What position? What skills do they need? Why would a circus require a programmer?*

The waiter means something quite different: **her being a talking cat is itself the job.** He isn't interested in her profession at all. To him, she is an extraordinary attraction who could be put on display.

So they are talking past each other:

- **Her frame:** “You are offering to employ me. What would my duties be?”
- **His frame:** “You are the thing people will pay to see.”

That is why her final question is funny. She apparently regards *talking, dressing, carrying a laptop, ordering coffee* as perfectly normal attributes of herself, while “programmer” is the salient fact about her identity.

And in the darker interpretation we've been developing, that innocence becomes ominous: she doesn't yet understand that the waiter isn't really offering her a **profession** at all. He's assigning her a **market value**.
