# A:

I think the most interesting reading begins one level below the obvious misunderstanding. The real subject is not **“a cat who fails to understand why a circus wants him”**, but **who is entitled to define what a person is socially**.

### The broader joke: a failure of recognition, not of comprehension

The waiter encounters something extraordinary and immediately makes a category decision:

> talking cat → spectacle → circus.

He does not first wonder what the cat does, what the cat wants, or even whether the cat is looking for work. He sees one spectacular attribute and lets it swallow the rest of the individual.

Sociologically, this resembles what is sometimes called a **master status**: one conspicuous characteristic becomes so salient that everything else about the person becomes secondary. A very tall physicist is asked whether he plays basketball; somebody with an unusual voice is told he ought to be on television. The apparent compliment contains a small act of dispossession: *I know what your conspicuous feature is for better than you do.*

And importantly, the waiter is not hostile. He is delighted. He thinks he is being helpful.

That makes the joke sharper. It is about **benevolent objectification** rather than straightforward hostility. The cat is being offered an opportunity precisely because he is regarded as exceptional — but the offer denies him the right to be ordinary.

That, to me, is the central social idea:

**Equality includes the right to be boring.**

The cat may not want his most remarkable characteristic to become his occupation.

There is another distinction underneath it: **being versus doing**. The cat apparently wants professional identity to depend on something he *does* — programming. The waiter wants to employ him for something he *is* — a talking cat. The circus represents the economy of spectacle: *your existence is the product*. Programming represents, at least ideally, the economy of competence: *your work is the product*.

That makes “programmer” unusually good here. It would not work quite the same way with “plumber.”

A plumber's competence is concrete, physical and externally legible. Programming is largely invisible. You cannot look at someone and see whether they are a programmer. The cat therefore supplies an **identity claim that the waiter cannot verify visually**. The whole joke becomes a struggle between two descriptions:

**“I see what you are.”**  
versus  
**“I tell you what I do.”**

And the cartoon never establishes that the second description is true.

That ambiguity matters.

The cat may genuinely be a programmer. He may also be lying. Or “programmer” may be a deliberately chosen counter-fiction: a respectable, opaque occupational identity used to stop a stranger from converting him into a freak-show attraction. In that reading, whether he can program is almost irrelevant. The statement is performative: *Please treat me as someone with an ordinary social role rather than as a specimen.*

There is also a very programmer-specific form of wit here. The cat's reply can be read as **weaponized literalism**.

He almost certainly understands perfectly well what the waiter means by “hire you.” But instead of acknowledging the implicature — *hire you as a talking-cat act* — he deliberately interprets “hire” through his alleged profession:

> “A circus? What possible use would they have for my professional services?”

It is the same conversational tactic as answering an offensive insinuation with “I don't understand — could you explain exactly what you mean?” You force the other person to say explicitly what was more comfortable when left implicit.

The programmer stereotype helps: programmers are stereotypically literal-minded. The joke can therefore pretend that the cat has missed the implication when in fact **literalism may be his rhetorical weapon**.

And the choice of a **cat** makes this much better than a generic talking animal. Cats are culturally coded as autonomous, aloof, contemptuous of imposed duties and remarkably uninterested in pleasing humans. A dog being enthusiastic or confused about employment is one thing; a cat silently refusing somebody else's conception of its purpose is almost archetypal.

There is even a modern internet-era irony. Cats are perhaps the paradigmatic creatures of online *content*. Programmers make the infrastructure on which that content circulates. So the waiter is effectively saying:

**“You could be content.”**

while the cat replies:

**“I make things.”**

The species most relentlessly turned into internet spectacle insists that he belongs behind the machinery rather than in front of the audience.

### What the drawing adds

Here I think the illustration is substantially cleverer than the bare joke.

First, the cat does **not look confused**.

That is crucial.

The narrowed eyes, slightly raised head and almost contemptuous sideways glance read much more naturally as *“Really? This again?”* than as *“Why on earth would a circus hire me?”* The picture therefore shifts the probability away from innocent misunderstanding and toward deadpan resistance, irritation or trolling.

The waiter, meanwhile, is almost grotesquely expressive: bulging eyes, sweat, wild hair, enormous nose, bent posture, clutching his chest and gesticulating theatrically.

There is a beautiful visual reversal here:

**the man who wants to send somebody to the circus looks much more like a circus performer than the cat does.**

The waiter is performing astonishment. The cat is sitting quietly drinking coffee.

So although the verbal premise says *the cat is the spectacle*, the composition makes **the waiter the spectacle**.

That may be the drawing's best addition to the joke.

Notice also the status arrangement. The waiter is standing in uniform, leaning toward the customer. The cat occupies an enormous upholstered chair like an established café regular, holding a cup, with a pastry beside him. Visually, the cat is not trespassing in human civilization at all. He is entirely at home in it.

The waiter effectively attempts to reclassify him:

**customer → curiosity → employee/exhibit.**

That is more socially pointed than merely saying, “Wow, a talking cat!”

He has encountered the cat in one legitimate social role — paying café customer — yet immediately decides that the cat's *proper* place must be somewhere else, where his difference can be displayed.

The illustration consequently suggests something like: *You may participate in ordinary society, but your difference makes ordinary membership seem unnatural to me.*

And because the offer is cheerful and well-intentioned, the mechanism becomes recognizable. Nobody has to hate somebody in order to exoticize them.

Another small but important choice: **there is no computer**.

Nothing in the picture confirms that the cat is a programmer. Had the illustrator placed a laptop covered in code in front of him, the interpretation would become much flatter:

> He really is a programmer, and the waiter has misunderstood his profession.

Instead, all we have is the cat's assertion. That keeps open exactly the possibilities you mention: bluff, sarcasm, invention, role-playing, tactical misdirection.

“Programmer” may be biography, or it may simply be the identity the cat chooses to present at that moment.

The background figures are interesting too. Nobody appears remotely as astonished as the waiter.

Cartoon convention can explain that away, of course. But if we take the drawing literally, several stranger possibilities emerge. Perhaps talking cats are perfectly normal here and the waiter is simply provincial. Perhaps everyone else has already got used to this particular cat. Perhaps the cat has endured this reaction dozens of times.

Or, in the most radical reading, perhaps there is no objectively extraordinary cat at all: what we are seeing is **the waiter's subjective representation** of somebody he has mentally classified as an exotic creature.

I wouldn't make the hallucination reading primary, because the drawing gives the cat considerable physical presence. But the indifference of the surrounding café does create a tiny crack in objective reality. The waiter may be less reliable than the usual telling of the joke assumes.

There is even a class joke hidden in the drawing. The cat is enormous, comfortable, sedentary and almost proprietorial in his armchair — literally something of a **fat cat** — while the waiter is thin, nervous and working. If the cat really is a well-paid technical professional, then the unsolicited offer of circus employment becomes even more absurd: the poorly paid service worker has seen one exotic attribute and instantly invented a new career for somebody who may be considerably better established than he is.

That makes the waiter's “My uncle runs a circus” amusing for another reason: the miracle of a speaking animal is immediately processed through **networking**.

Not metaphysics. Not science. Not philosophy.

“I know a guy.”

Even supernatural events are converted into referrals.

### There is also a philosophical joke hiding here

Human culture has traditionally treated language — *logos* — as one of the great boundary markers between humans and animals.

So imagine the philosophical magnitude of what has happened. An animal is not merely making sounds; it is participating in a semantic conversation, apparently possessing concepts, occupations, intentions and irony.

And the human response is essentially:

**“Cool trick. We can monetize that.”**

The waiter receives what could be revolutionary evidence about animal rationality and interprets it as an entertainment opportunity.

That is wonderfully deflationary.

Speech, which ought to establish the cat as a subject, instead makes him a more valuable object.

In that sense the joke concerns **recognition**. Having the capacities associated with personhood does not automatically result in being socially recognized as a person. The cat demonstrates rational agency; the waiter hears an act.

### The strongest literary and cinematic parallels

- **Franz Kafka, “A Report to an Academy.”** This may be the closest parallel of all. Red Peter, formerly an ape, acquires human language and behavior and eventually becomes a variety-theatre performer. His crossing of the human/animal boundary does not simply liberate him; it makes him performable. Your cartoon almost reverses Kafka: this cat appears to say, *No, I will not take the Red Peter career path. I already have a profession.*

- **Kafka, *The Metamorphosis*.** Gregor Samsa has the opposite problem. Internally he retains human consciousness while his visible body becomes animal-like, and increasingly everyone treats the body as the truth of what he is. The programmer cat is an inverted Gregor: an animal body claims an ordinary human vocational identity, but appearance again threatens to override self-definition.

- **Saki, “Tobermory.”** A cat is taught to speak, and the important consequence is not “what a marvelous circus trick,” but that the cat suddenly becomes an autonomous social observer who knows embarrassing things about humans. Speech gives the animal judgment. Your cat's half-lidded expression has something of Tobermory's dangerous superiority: perhaps the alarming thing is not that the cat talks, but that he has opinions about us.

- **Natsume Sōseki, *I Am a Cat*.** Here the cat becomes the detached, sardonic observer of bourgeois human society. Visually, your cat belongs to this tradition much more than to the “performing animal” tradition: comfortable, judgmental, faintly contemptuous, watching humans make fools of themselves.

- **E. T. A. Hoffmann, *The Life and Opinions of the Tomcat Murr*.** Murr is not interesting because he can perform tricks; he considers himself an author and intellectual. That is almost exactly the ontological joke here: *why should an intelligent cat's species determine his vocation?*

- **The Elephant Man** and, in a rather different register, **Tod Browning's *Freaks***. Both turn on the distinction between possessing an unusual body and being socially reduced to that body. The relevant parallel is not suffering as such but the gaze: one party sees a person; another sees something displayable.

- **Star Trek: The Next Generation, “The Measure of a Man.”** Data is already an officer with a profession and social relationships, yet an institution suddenly treats the feature that makes him extraordinary — his artificial nature — as more fundamental than all those ordinary identities. Structurally it is extremely close: *You think you are a colleague; I think you are an interesting specimen.*

One could even add **Pinocchio and Stromboli** as the children's-story version: the moment somebody discovers a being whose mere existence can draw a crowd, the next thought is to put him on a stage.

So my preferred interpretation of the cartoon would be this:

**The waiter thinks he has discovered an extraordinary cat. The cat thinks the waiter has failed an extremely ordinary social test: can you meet someone unusual without immediately turning the unusual thing about them into their entire identity?**

And the drawing makes that sharper by making the supposedly impossible creature the calmest, most socially settled figure in the room, while the “normal” human becomes frantic, theatrical and faintly ridiculous.

The cat may be naive. He may be offended. He may be lying. He may be trolling. But visually, I think the artist has deliberately made **“he understands perfectly well”** the richest reading. The punchline then becomes not a misunderstanding but a refusal to cooperate with the waiter's framing.

That is much closer to social satire than the isolated panel suggested.

And the skirt is not arbitrary. It introduces another layer of categorical uncertainty: species, clothing, perhaps gender presentation, perhaps class or professional identity. This makes the immigration/minority analogy unmistakable.

> You cannot really be from here.

The cat's answer—

> “From here. Yes, I’m a cat. Where’s my order?”

—is almost a textbook rejection of what is sometimes called the **perpetual foreigner** experience.

The remarkable thing is that the cat doesn't demand philosophical recognition. He demands his pastry.

That makes the satire much better. His response is essentially:

**My ontology is not currently relevant. I am a customer.**

The waiter cannot maintain that ordinary frame. Every time the cat tries to conduct a normal commercial interaction, the humans drag him back into the category of anomaly.

That strengthens the idea I suggested before: **the right the cat is implicitly demanding is the right to be mundane.**

The waiter strokes him:

> “Ooh, it’s soft!”

The cat hisses.

This is a very important addition because now the cartoon distinguishes two forms of encountering another being:

**conversation** and **handling**.

The cat has just demonstrated language, self-awareness and social agency. Yet the waiter immediately touches his body as though examining an interesting object.

Speech should establish subjecthood.

Instead, physical difference reasserts objecthood.

And the cat's hiss is funny because at that instant he behaves exactly like an ordinary cat. But socially it means something more interesting: he has exhausted the language of polite interaction and resorts to the most primitive possible boundary signal—

**Don't touch me.**

There is an obvious human analogue in unsolicited touching of someone's hair, clothing, body, disability aid, pregnancy, etc. The person is approached not quite as a person but as an interesting physical phenomenon.

### 4. The circus proposal is now explicitly a freak-show proposal

The expanded wording removes almost all doubt:

> “You’d be the STAR! Bigger than the bearded lady AND the two-headed calf!”

This is not employment in the normal sense.

The waiter has mentally placed the cat in a taxonomy:

- bearded woman,
- two-headed animal,
- talking cat.

That is exactly the mechanism we were discussing: the salient deviation becomes the person's entire economic identity.

And then comes perhaps the darkest little joke:

> “Your own cage—er, DRESSING ROOM!”

That correction is doing enormous work.

The waiter knows, at some level, what relationship he is proposing.

“Cage” is the honest conceptual category.  
“Dressing room” is the socially acceptable euphemism.

So the cartoon now contains a miniature critique of **benevolent language masking unequal status**.

He believes he is offering the cat privilege—star billing, money, special accommodation—while inadvertently revealing that he still conceptualizes him as an exhibit.

It is not hostility. That is precisely why it works.

This panel may be one of the cleverest.

The cat has become a public spectacle before even accepting the job. Someone is photographing him and says:

> “Come on, kitty—give us a smile!”

That phrase has a recognizable human social resonance, especially when addressed to women: the stranger feels entitled not merely to look at you but to demand a particular pleasant expression from you.

So the progression is now:

**look at us → let us touch you → perform for us → smile for us.**

The cat's interiority becomes increasingly irrelevant precisely as everybody becomes increasingly fascinated by him.

That is a rather sophisticated paradox:

**the more extraordinary a person appears to observers, the less interested they become in that person's ordinary subjectivity.**

This is where I think the full version becomes much better than a straightforward discrimination allegory.

Yet he looks delighted.

That is wonderfully destabilizing.

If the cartoon wanted a simple moral—“poor cat is being objectified”—this panel would be a mistake. Instead, it admits that **being objectified can also be attractive**.

Attention is pleasurable. Celebrity is pleasurable. Money may be pleasurable. Being special can be pleasurable.

The cat may perfectly understand the bargain and still think:

> Actually, that sounds rather fun.

So the opposition is not simply oppression versus dignity. It is also:

**ordinary professional identity versus celebrity identity.**

And this makes “programmer” much more interesting.

The cat has a legitimate ordinary profession, yet he is suddenly offered something much more glamorous merely for existing.

That is a very modern dilemma.

A programmer can write software for twenty years and remain anonymous. A talking cat can become internationally famous by lunchtime.

In contemporary terms, it is almost the difference between **having a career and becoming content**.

That doesn't logically prove competence—perhaps he is still bluffing—but visual grammar tells us that we are supposed to accept the profession as real.

He has heard:

- “bearded lady,”
- “two-headed calf,”
- “cage—sorry, dressing room.”

It is very difficult to believe that an intelligent speaker capable of programming genuinely has no idea why the circus wants him.

So:

> “What would they need a programmer for?”

can function as an extraordinarily polite refusal to accept the premise.

He translates their proposal into *his* social vocabulary and forces them to confront the mismatch.

In effect:

That is wittier.

### 8. And then the last panel reveals the deepest layer

The final exchange is:

> “A programmer? Can you believe it?”

and then the old man:

> “First they talk. Next they’ll want to vote.”

This retrospectively reframes the entire cartoon.

We have moved through three progressively more serious levels of recognition:

**speech → occupation → citizenship.**

The cat first demands recognition as a conversational subject.

Then as a worker with a profession.

And the old man immediately imagines the terrifying logical conclusion:

> If we accept these beings as rational agents, eventually they may demand political membership.

That is exactly the slippery slope which historical systems of exclusion often fear. The anxiety is not really that the subordinate group can speak. It is what speech implies.

If the cat can say:

**“I want coffee.”**

then perhaps he can say:

**“I don't want to be touched.”**

then:

**“I have a profession.”**

then:

**“I determine what kind of work I do.”**

and eventually:

**“I should have a say in the rules governing me.”**

The old man's complaint is ridiculous because the premise is a talking cat—but structurally his reasoning is perfectly coherent.

Once you recognize subjecthood, it becomes increasingly difficult to explain why recognition should arbitrarily stop at the café door.

That gives the joke an unexpectedly political-philosophical structure.

### There is another delicious visual reversal

Notice who behaves most “civilized.”

The cat:

- carries a bag,
- orders politely,
- speaks excellent English,
- waits for his food,

### It also changes the meaning of “programmer”

In the short version, “programmer” could merely be an arbitrary incongruous profession.

Here it has a more specific function.

Programming is almost the ideal occupation for this joke because it is:

- intellectual rather than bodily;
- invisible rather than spectacular;
- judged, ideally, by output rather than appearance;
- associated with people who may work remotely or anonymously;
- stereotypically associated with literalism and resistance to social theatrics.

The circus wants to monetize **what the cat looks like and what he is**.

Programming monetizes **what the cat can do mentally**.

So the final question is not merely occupational misunderstanding. It stages two incompatible theories of human—or feline—value.

**Circus:** you are valuable because you are exceptional to look at.  
**Profession:** you are valuable because you possess a transferable competence.

And the cat apparently prefers to define himself according to the second.

### The literary parallel with Kafka becomes much stronger

The full strip makes **Kafka's “A Report to an Academy”** almost uncannily appropriate.

Red Peter the ape learns human language and manners because they provide a “way out,” and eventually becomes a performer on the variety stage. Civilization recognizes him precisely by converting his crossing of the species boundary into entertainment.

> Red Peter joined the variety theatre. I went into software.

That is an excellent compressed description of the difference.

But I think the closest cinematic parallel may now be **District 9**. Its aliens are intelligent subjects, yet bureaucracy, commerce and public culture continually classify them as biological objects, problems, commodities and categories. The details are obviously much darker there, but the conceptual mechanism is strikingly similar.

So yes: the larger strip supports the previous interpretation, but makes it both more explicit and more complicated.

The short cartoon could be read as a misunderstanding.

The full one is much harder to read that way.

By the time the cat asks why a circus needs a programmer, **everyone—including the cat—knows exactly what is happening**. The real joke is that each side insists on a different answer to the question:

**“What kind of thing is this creature?”**

The waiter says: **a marvel.**  
The circus would say: **an attraction.**  
The crowd says: **content.**  
The old man says: **a future political problem.**  
The cat says, with magnificent banality:

**a programmer who would still quite like his coffee and pastry.**

**work.**

The reply—

> “Single. Not even a cat.”

—is particularly good because it refuses yet another assumption: apparently even the idea that the cat must pair with another cat is not something the waiter is entitled to take for granted.

I wouldn't force a specific sexuality/inter-species interpretation onto that line. Its strength is precisely that it says: *you don't know nearly as much about this person as your taxonomy makes you think you know.*

### “Cage—dressing room” still does excellent work

This remains one of the best lines from the previous version.

The waiter promises:

> “Your own cage—dressing room! Your own dressing room!”

He involuntarily produces the real category and then corrects himself into polite HR language.

What makes the new version better is that the waiter now looks almost like a recruiter. He is kneeling beside the laptop, holding up what resembles a circus brochure, making an unsolicited employment pitch:

> “Any terms you like!”

This makes the scene surprisingly close to bad recruitment:

**I have seen one salient characteristic about you and have immediately decided what job you ought to want.**

The alleged generosity makes it worse rather than better. He is not exploiting the cat cheaply. He is prepared to offer excellent conditions while still completely misunderstanding what the cat considers professionally relevant.

### The new sentence about the circus changes the punchline enormously

This is probably the single biggest improvement:

> “A circus, you say? The one that sold the same season twice and folded in March? Sounds tempting.”

Now there is almost no plausible way to interpret the cat as simply naive.

The cat knows:

- which circus this is;
- its business history;
- that it behaved dubiously;
- that it subsequently failed.

And “Sounds tempting” is obviously dry sarcasm.

Therefore when the cat continues:

> “But what would they need a programmer for?”

we can no longer comfortably say that the cat has missed the point.

**The cat is trolling him.**

Or, more precisely, the cat is refusing to cooperate with the category error.

The waiter says:

> “My uncle wants to hire *the phenomenon you are*.”

The cat responds:

> “Fine. Then let's discuss *the professional services I provide*.”

It is almost a Socratic maneuver. The cat doesn't accuse him of prejudice. The cat accepts the waiter's vocabulary—*job, employment, terms*—and pushes it until its hidden assumption becomes ridiculous.

That's much wittier than innocent misunderstanding.

And there is an additional modern joke lurking there. The circus starts to sound rather like a dubious startup:

- unstable company;
- questionable previous business practices;
- desperately recruiting;
- “any terms you like”;
- extravagant perks;
- unclear role.

The programmer's response becomes:

> Interesting pitch. What's the actual job description?

That is specifically a **programmer joke**, rather than a generic profession substituted into an animal joke.

### It is also visually less morally prescriptive than the previous version

I think this matters for publication.

The earlier image ended:

> “First they talk. Next they’ll want to vote.”

That was a good line, but it told the reader what the allegory was about. It pushed the cartoon decisively toward civil-rights/political-enfranchisement satire.

The new one removes that explanatory coda.

Instead we get the silent dog drinking from his bowl.

That is much better visual storytelling because it leaves the reader to perform the final inference.

The previous cartoon says:

**This is an allegory about recognition and exclusion.**

The new cartoon asks:

**Why was the dog at the piano normal while the programmer cat wasn't?**

That's a more interesting question.

### There is one possible drawback for publication

This version contains **more ideas**, and therefore also more possible readings competing with each other:

- minority stereotyping;
- patronizing praise;
- professional discrimination;
- gender/reproductive assumptions;
- assimilation;
- entertainment versus intellectual labor;
- questionable recruitment;
- programmer literalism;
- the piano-dog meta-joke.

For an essay, that's excellent.

For a standalone cartoon, “Married? Any kittens?” and “the same season twice…” may almost over-engineer it. The original joke has beautiful economy; this one deliberately turns it into social satire.

So I would choose according to purpose:

**For a meme or simple joke:** the first image is stronger.

**For an article about what the joke means:** this one is the strongest of the three.

And aesthetically I also think this one looks more publishable: restrained blue/black palette, much clearer panel hierarchy, less visual frenzy, more consistent characterization, and—most importantly—details that acquire significance on a second reading.

The cleverest thing it does is something the other two did not:

**it puts another extraordinary animal in plain sight and asks us not to notice him.**

The waiter doesn't notice him either.

That makes the waiter himself demonstrate the mechanism the cartoon is about: **we do not react to difference itself; we react to difference that violates our expectations about where someone belongs.**


# How does the second page of this comic (attached) change your understanding of the first page? (pic 17.jpg)

Yes. The second page changes the first page quite radically. It does not merely continue the joke; it **retroactively destabilizes what we thought we had understood**.

My earlier reading of page one was essentially about **social classification**: the waiter sees “cat” and therefore assigns the cat to the category *spectacle*, whereas the cat insists on the category *professional*. Page two adds a deeper problem:

**Who is actually doing what we think we are seeing?**

That shifts the comic from identity and prejudice toward **agency, attribution, authenticity, and performance**.

### The dog was not background decoration after all

On page one I treated the piano-playing dog as a useful contrast: society has already found a comfortable social role for one extraordinary animal—*performer*—while being baffled by another animal claiming to be a programmer.

Page two shows that this was only half the joke.

The dog turns out to be the **hidden causal agent**.

The cat says:

> “I can’t sing. No ear, no voice.”

Nevertheless, she apparently sings magnificently. Then the waiter explains:

> “Of course it isn’t real. She only opens her mouth. The dog does the singing. He’s a ventriloquist.”

So the animal whom everyone has been treating as background accompaniment is actually producing the remarkable phenomenon.

That makes the dog almost a visual demonstration of a general cognitive error:

**we attribute agency to the salient thing rather than to the thing actually causing the result.**

The glamorous cat is surrounded by phones.  
The dog sits quietly at the piano.

The cat receives astonishment.  
The dog supplies the voice.

This immediately suggests much broader analogies: celebrity versus ghostwriter, executive versus technical staff, influencer versus production team, interface versus backend, frontman versus session musician.

And given that the cat claims to be a programmer, the **frontend/backend analogy becomes particularly delicious**.

The cat is the interface.

The dog is the implementation.

### Which means page one itself becomes suspect

This is the cleverest retrospective effect.

Once we learn that the dog is a ventriloquist, we have to ask:

**Was the cat actually talking on page one?**

The second page does not settle this.

Perhaps the cat genuinely talks but cannot sing, and the dog merely supplies the singing voice. That is perfectly consistent with the dialogue.

But suddenly another possibility exists: perhaps **all the cat's speech has been the dog's ventriloquism from the beginning**.

Then “Coffee with cream and a pastry,” “Yes, I’m a cat,” “I’m a programmer,” and even the sarcastic remark about the bankrupt circus may have been produced by the dog.

And this is much better than simply revealing “Ha! The cat wasn't really talking,” because the pictures refuse to tell us how far the deception extends.

The cat does type on the laptop. She appears intelligent and socially coordinated. She knowingly participates in the singing trick. So she is certainly not just a dummy.

We now have a distributed act:

**cat + dog = talking/singing cat.**

Where exactly is the agency?

That question is considerably more interesting than “Can the cat really talk?”

### It weakens one part of my previous interpretation—and strengthens another

Previously I described the cat as resisting conversion into spectacle.

Page two proves that this cannot be quite right.

She is perfectly willing to become a spectacle.

When the waiter announces:

> “Ladies and gentlemen—the talking cat! And now she will sing for you!”

she does not indignantly leave. She protests only that she **cannot sing**.

And then she enthusiastically participates in the deception, opening her mouth while the dog supplies the music.

So her objection on page one was probably never:

> “It is degrading to perform for humans.”

It was closer to:

> **“You don't get to decide what I am for.”**

That is a stronger idea.

The distinction is between **being assigned a role** and **choosing to perform a role**.

She can reject the circus's attempt to turn her species into her occupation and, five minutes later, voluntarily participate in a ridiculous café act.

Agency rather than solemn dignity is the issue.

That also makes her programmer punchline better. Perhaps “What would they need a programmer for?” is not wounded pride at all. It is simply an intelligent creature maliciously refusing somebody else's framing.

### “Is it AI generated?” changes the level of the joke

This little speech bubble is extremely important.

Someone in the crowd asks:

> “is it AI generated?”

Now the comic becomes self-referential.

There are suddenly at least **three levels of reality-testing**:

1. **Inside the café:** Is the cat really singing?
2. **Inside the fictional world:** Is this actually an intelligent talking cat, or some sort of trick?
3. **Outside the comic:** Is the image itself AI-generated?

And then the waiter says:

> “Of course it isn’t real.”

That sentence refers ostensibly to the singing—but in this context it becomes wonderfully unstable.

What isn't real?

The singing?  
The cat's voice?  
The talking cat?  
The persona?  
The picture?

And the woman responds:

> “Thank God. For a moment I thought she was real.”

That is almost a philosophical punchline.

The characters are **relieved by falsity**.

This is a major addition to the social reading.

On page one, people are astonished by something that does not fit their ontology.

On page two, once supplied with an explanation—*ventriloquism!*—they relax.

Whether that explanation is actually less miraculous is almost irrelevant.

Because think about it: their reassuring explanation is that **a dog is a ventriloquist capable of singing while playing piano**.

That is hardly normal.

But it fits an existing conceptual box: *performance, trick, entertainment*.

The talking cat did not.

So what matters is not plausibility. It is **categorical domestication**.

Humans tolerate the extraordinary once they can call it a trick.

That is excellent satire.

### This completely transforms the dog

I no longer think the primary point of the dog is a Rowlf-the-Muppet reference, although the piano-playing shaggy dog certainly evokes Rowlf visually.

The structural answer to your earlier question—*“Did you figure out what dog that is?”*—is now much better:

**he is the ventriloquist.**

And that is almost a contradiction in visual terms.

A ventriloquist normally occupies the visible position and makes the dummy seem alive. Here the relationship is inverted:

- the “dummy” attracts attention;
- the ventriloquist is treated as scenery.

He's an **invisible ventriloquist**.

That makes him much more than a joke character. He represents hidden authorship.

The comic has spent an entire page encouraging us to look at the cat—and thereby makes **us commit exactly the same attentional error as the waiter**.

We saw the dog.

We registered “funny piano-playing dog.”

But we didn't ask why he was there.

The second page punishes us for that.

That is genuinely good visual construction.

### And the final silent panel is important

The crowd disappears.

The cat gets up to leave, bag in hand, and casually takes the cup from the top of the piano. The dog looks up at her.

This suddenly makes them look like **colleagues**.

The public performance is over. No audience, no phones, no waiter announcing miracles. What remains is simply cat and dog sharing backstage space.

That final image almost redefines the entire relationship:

The crowd believes:

**CAT = star**  
**DOG = accompaniment**

But perhaps the actual structure is:

**CAT + DOG = team**

One handles presentation.  
One handles voice.  
Perhaps one handles software.  
We don't really know who contributes what.

And that uncertainty seems intentional.

### There is an AI analogy here that is much stronger than the literal “AI generated?” line

The two pages can now be read almost as a parable about AI systems.

A user interacts with a visible persona and naturally attributes intelligence, intention and authorship to that persona.

But behind it there may be something else producing the relevant output.

Then behind *that* may be still more machinery, people, training data, tools, infrastructure.

So:

> “The cat said it.”

may have the same structural ambiguity as:

> “ChatGPT said it.”

What exactly is the referent of the subject?

The interface?  
The model?  
The system?  
The developers?  
The corpus?  
Some combination?

The dog/cat act turns this into a comic problem of **mislocated agency**.

And “programmer” makes the analogy particularly recursive: the apparent software professional may herself be something like a user interface.

### It also introduces a very old literary device: the borrowed voice

This makes several parallels more exact than the Kafka ones I mentioned earlier.

**Cyrano de Bergerac** is an obvious one. Christian presents the visible body; Cyrano supplies the words. The beloved attributes the speech to the person she can see.

**Singin’ in the Rain** may be even closer. Lina Lamont appears to possess the voice the audience hears; Kathy Selden is the hidden voice provider. The spectacle depends upon systematic misattribution of performance.

There is also something of **The Wizard of Oz**: the impressive visible phenomenon causes observers to assign power in the wrong place, while the causal mechanism remains elsewhere.

And philosophically it edges toward problems such as the **Chinese Room**: when intelligent-seeming behavior emerges from a system, to what component—if any—should we attribute understanding?

But the comic improves on the simple philosophical puzzle by making **every candidate intelligent**. The cat seems intelligent. The dog seems intelligent. Their combination certainly is.

So there may be no need to locate intelligence in exactly one place.

### That is why I now interpret the first-page “programmer” line differently

Before seeing page two, I thought its deepest meaning was:

> *You regard me as an exotic body; I define myself by an intellectual profession.*

Now I would amend it:

> **Everyone is much too confident about knowing where agency resides.**

The waiter assumes the cat's visible species determines her proper occupation.

The crowd assumes the cat's moving mouth is producing the song.

We assumed the piano dog was background scenery.

Perhaps we assume the cat is the programmer because she happens to be sitting at the laptop.

The entire two-page comic systematically encourages **premature attribution** and then undermines it.

Even “the cat is a programmer” is no longer epistemically secure.

Maybe she is.

Maybe the dog is.

Maybe they work together.

Maybe “programmer” is merely the persona's backstory.

And because the second page explicitly introduces artificial generation and ventriloquism, those possibilities now feel intentional rather than fanciful.

So I would say page two changes the governing question.

Page one seemed to ask:

**“Why can't people see past what someone is and recognize what they do?”**

After page two, the deeper question becomes:

**“When we see something apparently speaking, creating, working, or performing, why are we so sure we know who—or what—is actually doing it?”**

That is a considerably more contemporary and, I think, more interesting comic.


# What version is the best

For publication, I’d choose the **blue two-page version**—the one with the programmer cat and piano-playing dog, followed by the ventriloquist reveal.

It is the strongest because it works on several levels **without explaining itself too much**. The first black-and-white cartoon has the cleanest punchline and is probably the funniest as a single gag, but its meaning is comparatively narrow. The colorful long version has many good ideas—touching the cat, “cage—dressing room,” “next they’ll want to vote”—but it tells the reader almost exactly what the satire is about. It leaves less room for discovery.

The blue version does the opposite. It plants information visually and lets the reader revise their interpretation later. The dog at the piano is especially important: on page one he looks like decorative absurdity; on page two he turns out to be causally central. That is genuine visual storytelling rather than illustrated dialogue.

It also has the best progression of meanings. At first it looks like a joke about stereotyping: *cat → circus* versus *cat → programmer*. Then page two shifts it into a joke about **misattributed agency**: the visible performer is not necessarily the source of the performance. And the “Is it AI generated?” line makes that ambiguity contemporary without turning the whole thing into an AI cartoon.

Most importantly, page two improves page one retroactively. After the ventriloquist reveal, even earlier facts become uncertain:

- Is the cat really talking?
- Is she really the programmer?
- Is the dog merely the voice, or something more?
- Are they collaborators?
- Was the waiter wrong because he stereotyped the cat, or accidentally right that she is a performer?
- Why does everyone accept a piano-playing ventriloquist dog more readily than a talking programmer cat?

A good comic rewards rereading. This one does.

I’d rank their **functions**, rather than simply their quality:

| Version | Best use | Main strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Black-and-white single panel** | Meme, magazine gag, quick illustration | Economy and immediate punchline |
| **Colorful long strip** | Explicit social satire | Rich catalogue of prejudice/objectification |
| **Blue page 1 only** | Illustrated essay | Subtle social and occupational satire |
| **Blue two-page comic** | Publication as a substantial cartoon | Layering, visual foreshadowing, reinterpretation |

There is one caveat. If the article's central subject is specifically **the original joke and its social meaning**, publishing both blue pages may almost become a separate work whose AI/authorship theme competes with the original point. In that case, **blue page one alone** may be the best illustration for the article.

But if you're publishing the comic **as a work in its own right**, I would unquestionably use both blue pages. The second page is what turns it from a clever adaptation into something with an actual second-order idea.

And I would definitely keep the dog. He is now the structural key to the whole thing.
