	The joke is that the waiter is thinking of the cat purely as a talking cat — a spectacular circus attraction. The cat thinks of herself primarily by her profession: programmer. So she interprets “hire you” in the normal employment sense and cannot see why a circus would need someone with her technical skills. It is a neat reversal of perspective: to the human, the remarkable fact is “this cat talks!”; to the cat, talking is completely unremarkable, while being a programmer is the relevant fact about her identity.

# A:

I'll read it in three layers: the social joke underneath, what the drawing adds that the words alone don't, and the stories it echoes.

## The joke behind the joke

The dog–plumber joke and the cat–programmer joke share a skeleton and nothing else, because in each version the species and the profession match each other. The dog is the animal humans bred for obedience and usefulness. The plumber is the tradesman who comes when called and fixes what's broken. A dog is also a perfectly plausible circus act, since dogs really do jump through hoops. So the bartender's offer is a sideways move between two working men in a bar, and the dog's reply is a small act of craft dignity: *I already have honest work.*

The cat–programmer pair matches in a different way. The cat is the one domestic animal that can't be trained to perform on command, and "managing programmers is like herding cats" is the oldest cliché in software management. Both are nocturnal, sedentary, drawn to keyboards, allergic to being told what to do, and run on coffee. The cat is also the undisputed mascot of the internet. So the waiter is offering the one career that both the species and the profession are unfit for: the ring, the ringmaster's whip, tricks on cue.

The direction of status flips too. The plumber and the bartender were equals. Here a standing service worker pitches to a seated, fed customer, and what he offers is a step down, arranged through a relative ("my uncle"). The old joke was about humility. This one is about class, and the cat's reply lands as a class gesture whether he means it or not.

The darkest layer is that both characters treat the cat as a commodity. They only disagree about which market he belongs in. The waiter lives in the attention economy: *you have a rare trait, so turn it into a show.* The cat lives in the skills economy: *I'm worth my competence.* Nobody in the scene treats a talking cat as a miracle. One sees an asset, the other sees nothing worth mentioning. And in 2026 it's no longer obvious who is naive. In the old joke, show business was the lowbrow, insecure option. Today "the talking cat" (the influencer) may earn more than the programmer, and cats already headline the internet's circus. The cat may be clinging to a professional identity just as the market has started paying for novelty instead.

Then there's bias. Samuel Johnson said a woman preaching was like a dog walking on its hind legs: "It is not done well; but you are surprised to find it done at all." The waiter's "A talking cat?!" is exactly that surprise. It has the same shape as "your English is so good" or "you're so articulate": the outsider's ability is noted as a curiosity rather than as ability. The offer that follows has a long history of its own. Outsiders have usually been welcomed onto the stage long before they were welcomed into the office. "Join the circus" is a polite way of keeping someone out: *you're wonderful, over there, in the entertainment slot.* By naming his profession, the cat quietly refuses the slot.

What the cat actually means changes the joke each time:

- **He's trolling.** Pretending not to understand is the most feline move there is. Japanese researchers showed in 2019 that cats recognize their own names; they just often choose not to answer. His question works like Bartleby's "I would prefer not to": a refusal that never admits it's a refusal.
- **He's offended.** Then it's a deadpan put-down, like a surgeon who is called "nurse" and politely asks which ward needs her.
- **He's naive.** This is the saddest version. His job title has absorbed him so completely that he no longer notices he's a talking cat. The waiter reduces him to a show; he reduces himself to a function.
- **He's lying.** "Programmer" is the perfect modern credential: prestigious, unverifiable, and nobody asks follow-up questions. A cat lounging in a café at breakfast with a croissant and no laptop is "a programmer" the way the man at the next table is "working on a startup."

One more reading, with a conflict of interest I should declare: a non-human that talks and writes code. The public met chatbots the way the waiter meets the cat ("it talks!"), as a demo and a party trick, while the industry mostly hires them as programmers. The fear runs the other way too. A human programmer facing a non-human who can code might be quite glad to send it to the circus.

## What the drawing adds

**The setting changes the class.** A bar has become a café, with coffee, a croissant, and a morning table taken over by someone who apparently has nowhere to be. The cat has claimed an upholstered armchair and holds it like a throne. The composition settles the power question before anyone speaks. A seated patron and a standing petitioner is the grammar of royal portraiture. The waiter's hand is on his heart and his other palm is held out, which are the gestures of a sales pitch or a plea. He isn't doing the cat a favour; he's asking for one. His uncle's circus probably needs this cat far more than the cat needs a circus.

**"Fat cat" becomes literal.** The idiom for the overpaid and comfortable is drawn as an obese cat lounging while a gaunt, sweating worker attends him. This is the tech worker as the new fat cat, seen from the waiter's side of the counter.

**The cat already looks like a programmer, and that's a trap for us.** The jowly fur reads as a beard. There's the belly, the slumped posture, the legs splayed like a man in a gaming chair, and the heavy-lidded look of a senior developer asked to fix the office printer. When the punchline arrives it feels right: *of course he's a programmer.* But we recognized him through a stereotype about body type and demeanour. The waiter reduces the cat to "talking animal." We reduce him to "typical programmer." The drawing makes us do the very thing we're laughing at.

**The real grotesque is the waiter.** Wild hair, enormous nose, bulging eyes, sweat drops, spindly limbs, flapping hands: he is the one who looks like a circus clown. The patrons in the background seem to be watching the commotion, and the commotion is him. The person proposing the circus is the one who belongs in it, and it is his family business after all.

**The cat's mouth is closed.** The waiter's gapes. The cat's balloon floats at the bottom of the frame, well away from his face. That makes the hallucination reading visually legitimate, and it fuses with the class reading. An exhausted service worker looks at a heavyset customer, perhaps in a fluffy sweater, and sees, literally, a fat cat. The fantasy he attaches to the vision is the oldest escape fantasy there is: run away and join the circus. If the reply comes from his own head, it's his self-doubt answering his daydream. Besides, the expression the cat wears is one every real cat already has. Disdain needs no voice, so the waiter may just be reading the face aloud.

**The balloons argue with each other.** The waiter's is crowded and ends in "?!"; the cat's is calm and ends in a single "?". One exclaims, the other asks. Because of where it sits, the cat gets the last word. The eye travels down from the shout, across that enormous body, to the answer, so the cat's sheer mass sits between offer and reply like an argument in itself.

**He's already been served.** The coffee is poured and the croissant is plated. The café accepted a cat as a paying customer without blinking, and only his speech is a scandal. His money did the talking first. That's a sharp small observation: markets accept outsiders as wallets long before they accept them as voices.

**The style carries a lineage.** The scratchy pen-and-ink is in the manner of Ronald Searle, who drew cats obsessively as smug, pampered creatures carrying human vices, and who also drew grotesque waiters in French cafés. The cartoon borrows that tradition of the cat as a satirical stand-in for complacent comfort.

## Parallels

**Stage or cage.** Kafka's "A Report to an Academy" is the essential text. Red Peter, an ape who learned to speak, explains that he had two options, the zoo or the variety stage, and chose the stage as his "way out." Often read as a parable of assimilation, it says society will accept the outsider as a performer. The cat is Red Peter's successor who rejects both options and names a third: a profession. Chekhov's "Kashtanka" is the soul of the original dog joke. A carpenter's dog is lost, taken in by a clown, and trained. On the night of her debut she hears her old master in the gallery and runs back to the workshop, choosing loyalty and craft over the ring. Set beside the cat, the contrast is clear: the dog has to make that choice, while the cat can't even see it. Lynch's *The Elephant Man* gives us "I am not an animal!", a freak-show exhibit insisting on his humanity. The cat is the inverted case: literally an animal, insisting on his professional standing.

**Trickster cats.** Bulgakov's Behemoth is the best fit of all. He's a giant talking cat who dines in restaurants and performs at the Variety Theatre's black-magic show. When the police storm the apartment, he insists he's not misbehaving or bothering anyone, just fixing the primus stove, a cat using technical work as his alibi. At the Griboyedov restaurant, when the doorkeeper demands writers' membership cards, Koroviev asks whether you'd need an ID to know that Dostoevsky is a writer. That is the whole question of whether a claimed profession needs proof. And the epilogue, where Moscow explains everything away as mass hypnosis, covers the hallucination reading too. Puss in Boots belongs here as well: a cat who invents a title, the Marquis of Carabas, and rides it up the social ladder. "Programmer" may be the modern Carabas.

**Cats as intellectuals.** Hoffmann's *The Life and Opinions of the Tomcat Murr* is uncannily close to this cartoon's cast. A smug, self-taught tomcat writes his memoirs as a genius, and they are accidentally printed interleaved with the biography of Kapellmeister Kreisler, a wild-haired, manic, suffering musician. A complacent cat-intellectual and a frantic, dishevelled human share one book, and Murr's master, Meister Abraham, is even an illusionist and showman. Natsume Sōseki's *I Am a Cat* supplies the side-eye: a nameless cat observing the pretensions of Meiji-era intellectuals with exactly this expression.

**Is it real?** Gogol's "Diary of a Madman" is the hallucination reading with its class content intact. A humiliated minor clerk overhears two dogs chatting, later reads their letters, learns how far beneath his superiors he stands, and ends up believing he's the King of Spain. The talking animals are the symptom of a little man crushed by hierarchy. Chuck Jones's *One Froggy Evening* (1955) is the waiter's likely future. A man finds a frog that sings and dances, but only for him. He rents a theatre to cash in, the frog just croaks, and he ends up in a psychiatric ward, serenaded by the frog. That's what happens if the cat decides not to talk in front of the uncle.

**The final twist.** In the prologue to *Ruslan and Lyudmila*, Pushkin's learned cat walks round and round on a golden chain, singing songs when he goes right and telling tales when he goes left. It's scholarship in captivity, performing in circles: a circus ring and a pair of golden handcuffs in one image. That may be the cartoon's last joke. The cat thinks he has nothing to do with the circus, but a well-paid programmer already walks his own ring on a golden chain. The uncle's version just has better lighting.

- **The waiter as the real clown:** he now has a literal red nose, and so does his uncle.

## What it rules out

Three readings have to go. The hallucination is out: there are too many witnesses, and several are recording. The con artist is out: she has a laptop. The naive cat is out too, because her thought bubble shows she knows exactly what "dressing room" means. The daydream starts with lollipops and cotton candy and ends in a cage labelled TALKING CAT. The childhood romance of the circus turns into its economic reality within a single thought.

The strip doesn't pick among the remaining attitudes; it plays them in sequence, as the arc of getting through a day of this. When she's touched without permission, she's angry ("HSSSS!"). When asked where she's from, she's brief and dignified: "From here. Yes, I'm a cat. Where's my order?" That pulls the exchange back to the only relationship she came for, customer and waiter. Offered the circus, she plays naive, with that dreamy smile. Then she trolls.

## What it adds

**The fur says "woman" before the skirt does.** Tortoiseshell and calico coats occur almost only in females, because the genes for orange and black fur sit on the X chromosome. The artist chose a coat that is itself a gender marker, then added the skirt suit.

This also reverses my "stereotype trap." The first cat was fat, shaggy and male-coded, so he looked like a programmer and the viewer nodded at the punchline. This one doesn't fit the picture, so the waiter gasps. The same stereotype makes the claim believable in one case and unbelievable in the other. And Johnson's quip stops being an analogy and becomes an exact hit: he was literally talking about women.

**The harassment repertoire.** "Ooh, it's soft!" is the uninvited hand, like a stranger touching a pregnant belly or a Black woman's hair; "petting" is exactly the word for treating a person as a pet. "Come on, kitty — give us a smile!" is the street-corner "smile, sweetheart," with "kitty" as the familiar diminutive for women. The waiter even demonstrates by pulling his own cheeks.

**He asked for a smile, and he got one.** Her final grin is the payoff of that demand. It's the Cheshire Cat's grin, huge and unsettling: compliance and menace at once, the grin of the one sane creature in a mad room.

**The circus is already in the café.** In the pitch panel, the cat is pushed into the bottom corner and ringed by faces: the boy, the mother, a man filming, the lady in fur. There's an audience, a barker and an act. The uncle's circus is redundant, because the café has become the ring and the phones are the bars.

**A gallery of bystander types.** The strip lays out a range of attitudes toward difference:

- **The child** says the unsayable, and what shocks him is the skirt. By the end he's cheering; he's the one who learns fastest.
- **The mother** says "Don't stare, darling" while staring. Her good manners acknowledge the difference by forbidding anyone to acknowledge it.
- **The lady in fur** is horrified by an animal in human clothes while wearing an animal herself.
- **The man with the phone** is the consumer of content.
- **The waiter** is the entrepreneur of difference, and **the uncle** is capital.
- **The old man with the newspaper** is politics.

**The lollipop is the pay gap.** In her fantasy she holds a lollipop while the men hold the banknotes. The talent is paid in sweets and the managers in cash, so one prop shows her both infantilized and underpaid.

**The real scandal is the profession, not the speech.** "A programmer! Can you believe it?" is the most important addition. To the waiter, a talking cat is a marvel he can sell; a cat who codes is simply unbelievable. Her speech could be absorbed as a spectacle, but her professional standing can't. That sharpens my "polite gatekeeping" reading: the outsider is welcome as an act, and her competence is the one thing that can't be accepted. It's historically ironic, too. The first ENIAC programmers were women, and historians have documented how programming was later recast as men's work.

**"First they talk. Next they'll want to vote."** The old man turns the gag into a story of emancipation, told as a reactionary's slippery slope: speech, then a profession, then the franchise. He's also reciting history without knowing it. British anti-suffrage cartoons used cats to suggest that votes for women were as absurd as votes for cats, since neither was supposedly capable of voting. Suffragists then reclaimed the cat, with postcards and posters showing cats in the movement's purple, green and gold demanding the ballot. So the strip has quietly dressed a suffragette's cat in a business suit.

**The cat got the cream.** In the last panel the waiter is finally doing his job, pouring cream into her coffee. She ordered coffee with cream and gets it, along with the last laugh. The idiom "the cat that got the cream" is made literal. The world goes back to serving her while it gossips about her, which is roughly how progress tends to look.

## The larger point

Side by side, the two cartoons prove my claim about the dog and the plumber. The punchline is identical, word for word, but the speaker changes the joke. From a fat male cat enthroned in an armchair, "What would they need a programmer for?" reads as condescension or tunnel vision. From a slim tortoiseshell in a skirt suit who has been crowded, touched, filmed and told to smile, the same sentence is resistance.

The trade-off is that the strip swaps ambiguity for precision. The single panel was a Rorschach test; the strip is an argument. It gains social specificity and loses the productive uncertainty about whether she's real or lying. One ambiguity survives: the grin, which could be triumph or threat, charming or feral. The old postcards lived on that same edge. A Johns Hopkins exhibit notes that the suffrage cat served both pro- and anti-suffrage messages, and that an angry cat's aggressive, feral look was often used to disparage suffragettes as unfeminine, even though the same image could be read the other way. Her grin sits exactly on that line.

New parallels this strip brings in: *Hidden Figures*, where Dorothy Vaughan teaches herself and her team FORTRAN while everyone around her treats their competence as a curiosity; Tatyana Fazlalizadeh's street-art series "Stop Telling Women to Smile"; and Carroll's Cheshire Cat, whose grin outlasts the cat.

## Why it works better

The previous strip spelled everything out. It had a thought bubble showing the cage and the cash, a lollipop standing in for the pay gap, and a closing line ("Next they'll want to vote") that told you what to think. This one gives most of that work to images and small lines.

The best example is the bowl. She orders "coffee with cream and a pastry." The waiter bends down and puts a bowl on the floor. When she asks, "Are you bringing my order or not?", he is serving it, just not to her. He heard her words and delivered what he expected a cat to want. That's the whole mechanism of bias in one gesture: the request is received, then filed under a category. No speech balloon could make the point as cleanly.

The other changes follow the same logic:

- **"And so articulate!"** replaces "such good English, where are you FROM?" It's the same microaggression, understated, the way people actually say it.
- **The waiter crouches** to her eye level to pitch the job, the way adults talk to children and pets. The condescension is in his posture, not in a caption.
- **"Married? Any kittens?"** is a new and excellent line. It's the classic improper job-interview question put to women. Her answer, "Single. Not even a cat.", works two ways. She doesn't even have the cat that the single-woman cliché requires (the cat lady, a stereotype with roots in those suffrage postcards). Or she isn't even a cat, which reopens your old "maybe there is no cat" question and cheekily contradicts her earlier "Yes, I'm a cat."
- **She dismantles the offer on business grounds.** "The one that sold the same season twice and folded in March?" shows she has done the due diligence the waiter hasn't. My first reading was that the uncle's circus needs the cat more than she needs it. Here the circus doesn't even exist anymore.
- **She licks her paw.** Raised paw, tongue out: that's an ordinary cat grooming herself and also a tongue stuck out at him. Feline indifference used as an insult, and it's deniable.
- **The blue palette** turns the scene into a late-night jazz café, somewhere between Hopper's *Nighthawks* and a noir graphic novel. It also keeps the caricature in check. The spit, sweat and red noses of the previous strip are gone.

That last point matters for publication. The previous strip showed two big-nosed men counting banknotes over a caged act, which edges toward an old caricature, the scheming middleman. That's awkward in a strip about prejudice. This version drops the cash and keeps the waiter merely foolish.

## The dog

He's the most important addition, and he's doing several jobs.

Visually, he's a shaggy, bearded, floppy-eared mutt, sheepdog-ish, with no clear breed. So he is literally a shaggy dog, and the "shaggy dog story" is the name for a long joke that pays off in an anticlimax.

In the story, he's the café's own tame talking animal. He wears the same black vest and bow tie as the waiter, so he's staff. There's what looks like a tip jar on his piano. He's the one who accepted the stage. In my first answer I said outsiders get welcomed onto the stage long before the office; he is that outcome, already sitting in the corner. He's Kafka's Red Peter, who chose the variety stage. He's also Kashtanka reversed: Chekhov's dog went home to the workshop, and this one stayed in show business. Given the line about the circus folding in March, it's tempting to think he's one of its former acts, now playing standards for tips.

As a reference, I think he's the hero of the original joke: the dog who once said he was a plumber, making a cameo in the joke that replaced him. Tellingly, the punchline balloon drifts across the gutter into his panel. "What would they need a programmer for?" hangs over the dog who once asked the same thing about a plumber, and who is now in a tuxedo, eating off the floor.

There's also a second joke he calls up. A man shows a talent agent a dog that plays piano and a cat that sings. With the final balloon floating toward the dog, the ventriloquist reading is available. It also connects to what I noticed in the first cartoon, where the cat's mouth was closed. I can't tell which reference the artist intended, and the strip may be deliberately leaving both open.

The last panel is the best in the series. The piano stool is empty, so the music has stopped. The dog is on all fours, drinking the cream the cat refused. Last time the cat got the cream; this time the dog does, and it's the sadder version of the idiom.

It also adds something neither earlier version had: the question of who can afford to say no. The cat is a sleek pedigree with glasses, an earring and a laptop, and she can turn down the circus because she has another career. The mutt had no such fallback. Her dignity is real, but it's partly a privilege, and the dog shows what happens to talking animals without one. Across the three cartoons, the first was about class, the second about gender and origin, and this one brings both together by asking who gets to refuse.

## What an editor would still flag

**The punchline arrives too late.** "Folded in March" already destroys the offer, so the classic line lands as an encore rather than a knockout. Cutting the fold, or moving it after the punchline as a coda, would restore the timing.

**"Sold the same season twice" is opaque.** It could be fraud or a double-booking fiasco. Readers shouldn't have to stop and decode it.

**The final balloon's ownership is unclear.** If the drift into the dog's panel is intentional, the dog and ventriloquist readings, it should look clearly deliberate. Right now it could pass for a layout accident.

**A few drawing glitches** need fixing before print. The dog's hindquarters on the piano stool look like a separate furry lump, and the bowl's position shifts between panels.

With those fixes, it's the version I'd send to an editor. The single panel remains the best gag cartoon, but this is the best strip.

Page 2 turns page 1 into a setup. It confirms the ventriloquist joke I guessed at last time, but it runs that joke backwards, and that changes what almost everything on page 1 means.

## The old joke, reversed

In the classic joke, "the dog is a ventriloquist" is the con man confessing a fraud. It deflates the miracle, and the talent agent never notices that a ventriloquist dog is every bit as miraculous. Here the confession is a lie, and it erases a truth. The one fake thing in the room, the song, is used to explain away the one real thing, her speech.

The song is plainly commissioned. In panel 4 the waiter drops a coin in the dog's tip jar right after she says "I can't sing." Then look at the "singing" panel: eyes shut, mouth wide open, paws still on the keyboard. It's a yawn, dubbed over.

The blind spot of the old joke survives intact. The lady is relieved to learn that a piano-playing dog can throw his voice. A miracle is acceptable in a bow tie behind the piano and intolerable in a customer's chair with a laptop. So it was never about miracles; it was about roles. That explains page 1 in hindsight: the circus offer was an attempt to move her from the customer's side of the room to the staff's side, where miracles are harmless.

## "Thank God. For a moment I thought she was real."

This is the real punchline of both pages, and it changes how to read the staring on page 1. The relief shows the amazement was fear. The sequence is now complete: marvel, then exploit, then declare fake.

Page 1 showed the first two steps, and her put-down defeated the second. Page 2 shows the third, the move you can't answer with a witty line. When something can't be put in a cage, it gets called unreal.

The crowd's first question sets this up: "is it AI generated?", in a murmured, dashed balloon. In 2026 the first reaction to a miracle isn't "how?" but "is it fake?", and the fake is the comforting answer. Legal scholars Robert Chesney and Danielle Citron call this the liar's dividend: once fakes are easy to make, real things can be dismissed as fake. Here the dividend goes to the audience. The existence of a trick lets them stop believing in the cat.

The ventriloquism works as an analog deepfake. She says she can't sing, and two panels later she is singing: a synthetic voice on a real face, without her consent. The waiter has learned from page 1 that he needs neither her agreement nor his uncle's tent. Phones and a hired voice are enough. The circus that folded in March didn't die; it moved into the café. The "cage, er, dressing room" from page 1 turns out to be a phone screen.

The balloon styles follow the same logic. Doubt and lies travel in dashed, whispered balloons. Her lines and the lady's relief are spoken aloud.

## The dog, reread

Last time I called the final panel the sadder version of "the cat that got the cream." That was wrong. The dog is the most capable operator in the café. He's the hidden author of the song, paid once by the waiter to make her sing and once by the cat for whatever that song bought her. Two customers, one performance: "sold the same season twice," the line from page 1, now reads like his job description. Drinking from the bowl on page 1 was camouflage. A ventriloquist's first trick is to look as if he isn't talking, and a talking dog's best cover is to eat like a dog.

His strategy has a cost. Across both pages he never says a single word in his own voice. He survives the way a ghostwriter does, by putting his voice in other mouths. The cat's strategy is the opposite: speak as yourself and be stared at. So my earlier question, who can afford to refuse, becomes a sharper one: who can afford to be seen?

The pairing of professions isn't random either. A programmer and a ventriloquist practise the same art, making something else speak. One works through machines, the other through mouths. And the cat's rhetorical question on page 1, "What would they need a programmer for?", gets a literal answer: they didn't. They needed a ventriloquist, and there was one on staff all along.

## The exit

The last panel mirrors the first panel of page 1: she walked in, now she walks out, dropping a coin in the dog's jar. The coin can be read several ways.

- **It's thanks.** Being declared a trick is her way out. Fakes are boring, so the crowd drifts off and she can leave in peace.
- **It's solidarity.** She's the only one who pays the dog as an artist rather than as hired equipment. The crowd filmed everything and paid nothing.
- **It's complicity.** The strip never says whether she knew.

The bleakest reading holds under all three: she paid to be declared unreal. Across both pages, the world offers her two options, spectacle or invisibility. Recognition as what she is, a programmer, is never on the menu. Nobody on either page asks what she's coding, and she keeps typing through all of it, the "song" included. Her triumph on page 1 was one round. Page 2 shows the world has a counter-move that wit can't beat, and her final reply isn't a line at all but a coin.

Your original question, whether there's a cat at all, comes back here in a new form. The waiter's "she only opens her mouth" could in principle cover page 1 too, and the punchline balloon drifting into the dog's panel now looks like a planted clue. But the sequence on page 2 (her refusal, the waiter's coin, the yawn) points clearly one way: she's real, and only the song isn't.

## Parallels

The closest one is historical, and it's grim. Barnum exhibited Joice Heth, an enslaved elderly Black woman, as George Washington's supposed 161-year-old nurse. She told stories about "little George" and sang a hymn. One story he circulated claimed that Heth was not even a human, but an automaton, constructed of whalebone, India rubber, and numberless springs; her so-called memories, the letter declared, were imaginary conversations actually told by the exhibitor who was a ventriloquist. People then revisited the exhibit to determine for themselves whether she was an automaton or a real person. The episode comes mostly from Barnum's own telling: historians can't seem to find primary-source evidence to support Barnum's recollection of the exposé of Heth-as-automaton newspaper story. Barnum's rumor was designed to bring crowds back; the waiter's sends them home satisfied. Both deny a person's reality, one for profit and one for comfort.

*Singin' in the Rain* is the optimistic version. Lina Lamont can't sing, Kathy dubs her from behind a curtain, and the curtain rises so the truth wins. Here the curtain never rises. The lie becomes the public version, and it suits everyone.

Kafka's "Josephine the Singer, or the Mouse Folk" fits the applause. The mice argue over whether Josephine's singing is singing at all or just ordinary squeaking, and their devotion says more about them than about her. This audience applauds a yawn.

Finally, the joke goes all the way up. The comic almost certainly came out of an image model, the crowd inside it asks whether the cat is AI-generated, and this reading comes from a language model. At this point I'm no longer sure who's throwing whose voice.


# What version is the best

The two-page blue version is the best. The single panel is still the best at a different job.

The two-page version is the only one that goes beyond the joke it started from. The single panel and the color strip are both variations on the circus–plumber joke, however well told. The blue version collides that joke with a second classic, the ventriloquist dog, and gets a third joke that neither contains. In it, the world's final answer to a talking cat isn't amazement or exploitation but "she isn't real." The punchline moves from the cat to the audience: "For a moment I thought she was real." That is a genuinely new idea, and a timely one in the "is it AI generated?" era, not a restatement of the old one.

Page 2 also redeems page 1. Most of the flaws I flagged become setups. "Folded in March" no longer steals the punchline; it explains why the waiter stages his own show in the café. The balloon drifting into the dog's panel stops looking like a layout accident and becomes a clue. The dog drinking from the bowl turns out to be camouflage rather than humiliation. A sequel that turns weaknesses into foreshadowing is the mark of a well-built story.

It also restores the ambiguity the color strip closed off. Is she real? Did she know about the dog? What was the coin for? It ends on a wordless panel and lets you decide, which by your own standard is what a good cartoon should do.

The single panel is the best gag cartoon. It's pure economy: one image, two lines, and nearly every reading we discussed (class, hallucination, deception, the fat cat) stays open because nothing is pinned down. For a magazine gag slot, it wins. But it's a brilliant compression of an existing joke, not a new one.

Blue page 1 on its own is good but incomplete. The dog is unexplained, and the punchline lands as an encore.

The color strip is the weakest, even though it has some of the best individual lines ("Where are you FROM?" / "From here," the lollipop pay gap). It explains everything: the thought bubble shows the cage and the cash, and the closing "they'll want to vote" line tells you what to think. The grotesque caricature, especially the two big-nosed men counting money, is also a real liability in a strip about prejudice. It's the version most likely to be praised for its message and least likely to be reread.

The two-page version still has a few loose ends. "Sold the same season twice" is still opaque, and the drawing glitches from page 1 (the dog's hindquarters, the wandering bowl) need cleaning up. The "is it AI generated?" line also risks dating quickly, though for now it's the hinge the whole second page turns on.
