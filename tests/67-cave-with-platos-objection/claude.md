# The attached image represents Plato’s allegory of the Cave through a reworking of Joseph Wright of Derby’s painting of a philosopher demonstrating an orrery. This is certain: the artist meant exactly that.  Take this identification as given.

There is, however, a philosophical difficulty. The people in the picture know that they are looking at a model, not at reality; they do not mistake shadows for real things. In Republic VI (510d–511a), visible mathematical diagrams can assist reasoning about intelligible objects. In Republic VII (529a–530c), Socrates argues that astronomy should lead beyond observation of the visible heavens toward the study of intelligible mathematical relations. Studying a model is therefore not, by itself, evidence of intellectual captivity.

In this image, people are absorbed in an illuminated model of the cosmos while daylight is shut out. A straightforward Cave reading seems to make their engagement with the model a form of confinement, with the neglected light outside suggesting a possible release. Yet Plato’s account does not simply oppose misleading intellectual models to liberating sensory experience.

Some reconciliation with Plato’s educational programme may be possible, but treating every element as a straightforward illustration of his teaching seems strained. For this task, assume that the image nevertheless offers a meaningful use of the Cave allegory.

How would you interpret the image as a whole? Propose the reading you find most convincing, explain how it accounts for this tension, and support it with specific features of the image. What does your interpretation leave unresolved?

---

My reading is that the image does not show the chained prisoners at all. It shows a later, more comfortable stage of the Cave. These people have already made the first turn. They know they are looking at a crafted model rather than at the heavens. Their confinement lies not in mistaking images for realities but in having made the model's own light the centre of their world. The captivity is one of sufficiency, not error, and that is what lets the image use the allegory without misreading Plato's endorsement of diagrams and astronomy.

**The scene is the Cave's interior stage**

The Cave contains more than shadows. When the first prisoner is released, he is turned to face the fire and the artifacts carried in front of it. Someone then points at each passing object and makes him say what it is (515c–d). Those artifacts are crafted things, figures of wood and stone. The image matches this stage closely:

- The orrery is a crafted artifact, and its planets are the passing objects.
- The lecturer stands over it with a hand extended toward the orbits, doing what 515d describes.
- The light source is a flame. Wright's full title already describes a lecture on the orrery in which a lamp takes the place of the Sun, and Socrates says the fire's light in the prison corresponds to the sun's power in the visible realm (517b).

The reworking keeps Wright's substitution but changes one thing decisively. Wright's scene is nocturnal, so the lamp stands in for a sun that is absent. Here it is daylight outside and the blinds are drawn: the darkness has been manufactured so the lamp can play the sun. Wright also hides his lamp behind a silhouetted boy, much as the Cave's fire burns behind the prisoners. Here the foreground figure's head is bowed and the flame is fully exposed at the centre. Looking at the fire and the artifacts directly is what the turned prisoner does, not the chained one.

**How this meets the difficulty**

In Plato, knowing that something is a model is not the mark of escape. It is the mark of a particular stage. The geometers of Book VI know the drawn square is not the square itself (510d), and that knowledge places them in *dianoia* rather than mere belief. What keeps them below the top of the Line is not their use of images. It is that they treat their hypotheses as starting points needing no account, so their reasoning runs down to conclusions instead of up to an unhypothetical first principle (510b, 511b).

The image gives that limitation a visible form. At the centre of the system sits a flame the group lit themselves and could put out. It is a posited principle doing the sun's work, and everything is organised around it: faces lit by it, gazes converging on it. The lecturer holds what looks like a small clicker, so the system's motions are his to advance. A model whose centre the modellers placed there themselves is self-grounding in just the way *dianoia* is.

Book VII sharpens this. Socrates's warning about astronomy is not against models. It is against mistaking a beautiful artifact for the place where truth resides. He says the visible heavens should be treated like diagrams drawn by Daedalus: a geometer would admire the workmanship but think it absurd to study them seriously to find true ratios in them (529d–530a). The orrery is a Daedalian object, all polished brass and glinting rings. What the image shows is scrutiny of the object itself: chins on fists, narrowed eyes, the man at left writing on a tablet. The picture leans toward reading the brass rather than reading ratios through it.

**The modern details carry meaning**

Every figure, the lecturer included, wears a lanyard and ID badge; this is a briefing room of credentialed experts. Plato's prisoners also have an institution. They award honours to whoever is sharpest at noticing the passing things, remembering their order, and predicting what comes next (516c–d). An orrery is a prediction machine, so the badges read as the Cave's honour system: status for mastering the sequence.

The postures complete the thought. Most of the seated figures hold a hand at the chin or a fist under the jaw, heads still and pointed forward. No one is chained, yet the image echoes the neck fetters of 514a as voluntary absorption. The woman nearest the window, at back right, faces away from it entirely.

**The window**

This is where a naive reading goes wrong. If the daylight meant "stop modelling and look at the real sky," the image would be anti-Platonic, since Socrates mocks Glaucon for thinking that gazing up at the heavens lifts the soul (529a–c).

But the beam is not an invitation to observation. It is a thin, cold, blue-white line against an amber room. It passes above everyone's heads, fades out near the lecturer's outstretched arm, and lights nothing. It is the only line in the picture not oriented toward the centre. Its job is to show that the room's light is borrowed and that the darkness is a choice.

So the image's claim is not that models confine and the senses free. It is that a way of knowing can close itself off, and that this shows only as a sliver of light one did not make and cannot place inside one's system. That echoes the Sun analogy (508b–509b): the source is not one more object in the field it makes visible. Read this way, the image does not reject Plato's mathematical curriculum. It depicts that curriculum's typical temptation: to stop at the stage that is most satisfying and most rewarded.

**What this leaves unresolved**

The first open question is whether the image shows a failure or a phase. Plato's route upward runs through exactly this kind of study (522c–531c) before dialectic. A painting freezes a stage, and a frozen stage always looks like an endpoint. Nothing tells us whether these people are stalled or passing through.

Second, the lecturer is ambiguous. He may be the artifact-carrier and master of the honoured game. Or he may be the philosopher who has been outside and returned, obliged to go back down (519c–520c) and forced to teach in the only medium cave-dwellers can follow. Wright's lineage points to the second; his badge, his clicker, and his downward gaze point to the first. The beam ends near his arm, which could mean he is closest to the light, or that he has learned to work beside it without looking. If he is the returnee, the scene becomes justified descent rather than confinement, and my reading's critical edge softens considerably.

Third, the window problem is managed, not dissolved. A painting can show only visible light, so to signify what lies beyond, it must borrow the sensory vocabulary Plato demotes; it can depict a sun but not the Good. Plato's exit runs upward through mathematics toward dialectic, and it requires a guide and compulsion (515e). Here there is no door and no one pointing away, only light coming in. The image can show that the room is closed but not the way out, and a viewer may fairly read it the empiricist way I have argued against.

Finally, the painting is itself an image of an image: a reworking of an eighteenth-century painting of a model of the visible heavens, which for Plato are themselves only images. The viewer, placed at the table behind the bowed foreground figure, is drawn first to the flame, just as the figures are, and may notice the window only later. Whether the painting can turn its viewer, or only reproduce the absorption it criticises, is a question it raises about itself and does not answer. In that sense it is another of Daedalus's diagrams.


# Suppose the contradiction is intended by the artist. What would that mean?

If the contradiction is deliberate, the image is no longer an illustration. An illustration has to be faithful to its source; a deliberate inconsistency turns the picture into a claim about the source, or a question put to the viewer. The tension stops being a flaw to explain away and becomes the content. It also means my earlier move was the wrong one. Reading the beam as a merely structural sign, so that the picture agrees with Plato, is exactly the kind of smoothing an intended contradiction is built to resist.

I see three things it could mean, and they are not equally strong.

**The Cave turned against Plato**

The bluntest meaning is that the artist uses Plato's allegory against Plato's cure. In the *Republic*, release comes through mathematics, and those released are meant to rule. The image shows what that looks like as an institution: credentialed experts in a sealed room, a senior figure advancing the model with a clicker, a picture of the cosmos more absorbing than the cosmos. The daylight Socrates told astronomers to leave alone (530b) returns as the thing they have shut out.

The history of astronomy gives this some force. The field advanced partly by ignoring that advice: Kepler gave up circular orbits because of a small mismatch between his model and Tycho's observations of Mars. On this reading, an orrery of tidy circles lit by a lamp, with real light ignored behind it, shows model-worship rather than model-use. Plato's ladder becomes a cage once it hardens into an institution.

The weakness is that the image undersells this case. The beam is thin, lights nothing, and no one turns toward it. A painter convinced that observation liberates would give the daylight more power.

**Plato's own contradiction, made visible**

A subtler meaning is that the contradiction is not the artist's against Plato but Plato's own. In the allegory, the sun outside stands for the Good (516b, 517b–c). In Socrates's own decoding, the cave's fire stands for the visible sun (517b). So the visible sun appears on both sides: as the prisoners' fire and as the image of what lies beyond.

The picture inherits this doubled sun exactly. The lamp at the centre is the sun as fire; the daylight at the window is the sun as the outside. The daylight is at once what Book VII tells astronomers to leave alone and what the allegory tells them to climb toward.

There is a related doubling in Plato. He warns against images by means of an image, and Socrates admits he can offer only an image of the Good (506e, 533a). An artist who built the contradiction in on purpose would be handing Plato's predicament back to him. Any picture of the ascent must use visible light to stand for what is not visible, and so must say something its source denies.

**The contradiction as a summoner**

The meaning I find strongest makes the contradiction do Platonic work. In Book VII, Socrates contrasts perceptions that leave the mind at rest with those that "summon" it. These are perceptions that report opposites at once, as when the same finger seems both large and small. Faced with them, the soul is at a loss and calls on reason (523b–524d). The first released prisoner shows the same symptom: he too is at a loss (515d). And dialectic, at the top of the Line, is the one method that advances by doing away with its own hypotheses (533c).

On this reading, the image is built to summon. It offers two decodings and makes both fail:

- A viewer who takes the window as the exit commits Glaucon's error, assuming that looking out or up means looking higher (529a–c).
- A viewer who sides with the model, defending these people as doing legitimate Platonic astronomy, joins them in the room, content with the lamp.

Neither position is outside. The picture contains no exit. The only movement it allows is the viewer being stopped by the inconsistency and having to think about what an exit would even be. The release is not depicted; it is provoked.

That would also explain the image's refusals. The beam is too weak to be the answer, the model too beautiful to dismiss, the lecturer too ambiguous to cast as either the returned philosopher or the puppeteer.

This reading also implicates the viewer. Decoding an allegory (lamp means fire, window means sun, badges mean honours) is the same skill the prisoners prize: recognising the passing figures and their order (516c–d). An image that deliberately breaks its own code denies the decoder that prize.

**What it would cost**

Treating the contradiction as intended makes the image more Platonic in method by making it less Platonic as illustration. The risk is that almost any inconsistency can be redeemed as productive puzzlement. Even granting intent, a contradiction can be careless rather than pointed, and a reading that turns every flaw into a design feature is hard to tell apart from simple charity.

The test is whether the contradiction is precise. Here it is fairly precise: pairing Wright's lamp-for-the-sun with a window of real sun lands exactly on Plato's doubled sun. That is a good sign the tension was chosen rather than stumbled into.

But the summoner reading carries an unavoidable irony. It works only while the viewer stays puzzled. Every reading that settles the puzzle, this one included, turns the summoner back into an illustration.


# Have you considered the possibility that the artist might be using irony in relation to Plato’s concept? Note that the painting employs postmodern techniques (a classical subject rendered in a contemporary style) which opens the door to irony.

Partly. The closest I came was reading the image as the Cave turned against Plato, but that was critique, not irony, and the difference matters. Critique replaces Plato's claim with a counter-claim, such as "observation frees, models confine," so it needs the daylight to be strong. Irony keeps Plato's framework on stage while undercutting it, and needs no positive alternative. That is why irony fits the evidence better. The weakness I counted against the critique reading becomes a point in irony's favour: the beam is thin, lights nothing, and is ignored. An ironist is happy with a weak window, or even a fake one.

**A small adjustment to the premise**

The anachronism runs the other way. The style is not contemporary: the lighting, warm palette and grouping are Wright's eighteenth-century candlelit manner, carried over almost intact. What is contemporary is the costume and props: badges, suits, a tablet, a clicker, venetian blinds.

This matters for where the irony sits. It would come less from a modern style treating an old theme than from ordinary modern matter dressed in a grand, solemn manner. That is the old mock-heroic device, epic style for an unheroic subject, and it predates postmodernism by centuries.

The doubling is what makes it richer here. Wright already gave a science lecture the gravity of a religious scene, with the lamp glowing like a devotional light. The reworking keeps that sacred lighting and lowers the occasion to a corporate briefing. The irony would then target not only Plato but the Enlightenment's habit of treating the model as sacred.

**Not every quotation is ironic**

Postmodern technique opens the door to irony but does not guarantee it. Fredric Jameson distinguished parody, which imitates in order to mock or judge, from pastiche, which imitates with no stance at all. A classical subject in modern dress can be pure pastiche. So the question is whether the image shows markers of an ironic attitude. I think it does, mainly through excess:

- **The badges.** Plato's prisoners, in effect, wearing name tags.
- **The clicker.** Wright's natural philosopher has become a presenter advancing slides.
- **The poses.** Five figures in some version of chin-on-hand: a stock repertoire of "deep thought," repeated until it reads as a performance of thinking.
- **The beam.** Sharp as a laser, close to a cinematic cliché of revelation.

Each is slightly too much, and overstatement is how irony usually signals itself.

**What the irony would target**

The most interesting target is Plato's confidence that understanding releases. In the Cave, recognising shadows as shadows is the beginning of release. Plato does foresee resistance: the freed prisoner wants to turn back (515e), and the others would laugh at whoever returns (517a). But in both cases the resistance comes from not yet seeing clearly.

These figures see clearly. They know it is a model, and they stay. Peter Sloterdijk called this "enlightened false consciousness": people who know very well what they are doing and do it anyway. On this reading, the philosophical difficulty you raised at the start is no longer an obstacle to the Cave interpretation. It is the joke. The image shows a cave Plato did not foresee, one where knowing it is a cave is part of the furniture.

Two other targets follow naturally. One is the Cave as a cultural cliché, invoked so often that everyone who invokes it casts themselves as the one outside. The other is the interpreter. A picture this dense with decodable symbols invites earnest decoding, complete with citation numbers. The solemn exegete becomes the sixth chin-stroker at the table, and I can't claim exemption.

**But irony toward Plato is a Platonic move**

Socrates is the founding ironist. Thrasymachus complains of his habitual irony in the *Republic* itself (337a). The Cave contains laughter at the returning philosopher (517a). And the *Theaetetus* tells of Thales falling into a well while gazing at the stars, mocked by a servant girl (174a), which is the original comic picture of the absorbed astronomer. There is also a long tradition, associated with Leo Strauss and Allan Bloom, of reading the *Republic*'s ideal city as itself ironic.

So the decisive question is which kind of irony this is.

**Socratic irony** wears a mask over a real commitment and aims to provoke. If the image's irony is Socratic, it converges with the "summoner" reading I proposed earlier, because Socratic irony is itself a summoner.

**Postmodern irony** has no commitment behind the mask. Richard Rorty's ironist doubts that any vocabulary sits closer to reality than another. Jean Baudrillard goes further: the model precedes and produces the real, so there is no outside to escape to. On this version the beam is one more effect, a lighting cue, and the Cave has no exit because there is nowhere else.

The beam is the hinge, and it is genuinely double. It is a different kind of light, cool against the amber, entering from a source the room did not make. That suggests the artist still believes in an outside and is mocking those who think knowing about models is enough. Yet it is rendered almost like a lens flare, a photographic artifact, which suggests the "outside" may itself be a manufactured effect. On balance I lean toward the Socratic reading. A fully postmodern irony would more likely make the window a screen or a painted backdrop, and this one bothered to make the light different in kind.

**What irony costs**

Irony is the hardest intention to verify, and it can protect any reading: every awkwardness becomes "knowing." There is also a tension with the stipulation that the image makes meaningful use of the Cave. Postmodern irony would use the allegory meaningfully only by emptying it. Socratic irony would use it meaningfully by teasing it. The picture's surface supports both, and the difference between them is exactly the difference between believing there is a sun and believing there are only lamps.

# To which philosophical system do you think the artist belongs?

No image can prove which system its maker belongs to, and most artists borrow ideas without signing up to a doctrine. But if I have to name a lineage, the image fits best with the Frankfurt School's critique of the Enlightenment, Adorno especially. Heidegger is the serious rival. Platonism and thoroughgoing postmodernism fit less well.

**Why the Frankfurt School fits best**

Three features of the image line up with Adorno's thinking almost point for point.

**Enlightenment turning back into myth.** The central claim of Horkheimer and Adorno's *Dialectic of Enlightenment* is that myth already contains the seeds of enlightenment, and that enlightenment, pushed far enough, becomes myth again. The image enacts this on Wright's painting, one of the defining images of the Enlightenment. The scientific demonstration has become something like a rite. There is a flame at the centre of a gleaming altar, a white-haired officiant whose clicker advances the mystery, and initiates marked by badges. I noted earlier that Wright gave science the gravity of a religious scene. Here that sacred quality has hardened into a cult of the model.

**The administered world.** Adorno's term for modern society was the "administered world": life organised by institutions, credentials and procedures. The badges, the hierarchy of presenter and audience, and the drawn blinds all make the darkness a social arrangement rather than a natural condition. The figures who know it is a model and stay anyway fit this tradition too. Sloterdijk's "enlightened false consciousness," which I invoked last time, was developed in direct dialogue with Frankfurt-style critique of ideology.

**The ban on images.** This is the strongest fit. Adorno held that a redeemed or truthful state of the world cannot be positively depicted. Echoing the Jewish prohibition on images of God, he argued it can appear only negatively, by showing the present as false. In the final aphorism of *Minima Moralia*, he says knowledge has no light except what redemption casts on the world, and that its task is to show the world estranged, cracked open.

A thin beam that lights nothing and only reveals the room as dark is close to a literal picture of that idea. It also changes something I treated as a weakness in my first answer. There I said the image can show that the room is closed but not the way out. On an Adornian reading, that refusal is a principle, not a limitation.

The irony we discussed last time fits as well. Adorno's prose is relentlessly ironic and dialectical: it states a position in order to show it turning into its opposite.

**The case for Heidegger**

Heidegger is the rival worth taking seriously, because he wrote about the Cave itself. In "Plato's Doctrine of Truth," he argues that the allegory marks a fateful shift. Truth stops meaning unconcealment, things showing themselves, and starts meaning correctness: the right gaze directed at the idea. For Heidegger, Plato's education by turning the gaze is not the escape from the cave. It is the start of the metaphysics that ends in modern technology.

This dissolves your original difficulty more directly than any other reading. The tension between "Plato endorses models" and "the model is confinement" is simply Heidegger's thesis. Other features support him too:

- The orrery is almost a literal "world picture." In his 1938 essay of that name, Heidegger argues that modernity grasps the world as something represented and set before a subject.
- In the same essay he describes research as an institutional enterprise, which the badges and briefing-room setting evoke.
- No one turns toward the window, yet the light arrives anyway. That matches truth as something that happens to us rather than something achieved by correct looking.

I rank Heidegger second for two reasons. First, he is not an ironist. His thought has almost no room for the mock-heroic bathos of name tags on Plato's prisoners. Second, the image's social specificity points toward a critique of institutions and power, not toward the history of Being.

**Why not the others**

A Platonist artist would show the model working as a ladder, or would show someone turning. Neither happens.

A thoroughgoing postmodernist, in the vein of Baudrillard, would more likely make the outside fake: the window a screen, the daylight a projection. Here the light is cool and blue-white against the amber interior, clearly a different kind of light from the lamp. That suggests the artist still believes in an outside, even one that cannot be pictured. That belief is Adorno's position exactly, and it separates him from his postmodern successors.

**What tells against my own answer**

Adorno distrusted polished, seductive, readily consumable images. He tended to see them as products of the culture industry, and he admired difficult, dissonant modernist art instead. This picture is glossy, beautiful and immediately legible.

So the image is Adornian in its argument but closer to what Adorno criticised in its finish. Either that gap is part of the irony, a critique of enlightenment delivered in the seductive form it critiques, or it shows the artist using Frankfurt ideas eclectically rather than belonging to the school.

The most honest version of my answer is this. The artist belongs to the broad family of thinkers who read the Enlightenment's light as also a form of darkness. Which member of that family depends on whether you weigh the badges more heavily, which points to Adorno, or the beam, where Heidegger and Adorno are closer than either would have liked.

# Can you explain why you only analyze Western schools of philosophy?

The material pulled me that way, and I followed it without checking the pull. Plato and Wright are Western sources, and your question about which "system" the artist belongs to invited me to trace a lineage. The lineage of those sources runs through Western philosophy, so that was a reasonable place to start. But it was no reason to stop there. The image's real themes are what models are for, when a means becomes a trap, and what light has to do with knowledge. No single tradition owns those questions, and several others have thought about them at least as sharply.

The omission also hid something. Every reading I offered shared one assumption: that light means truth and darkness means confinement. That runs through Plato's sun, the Enlightenment (whose very names, *Aufklärung* and *les Lumières*, mean light), Adorno's light of redemption, and Heidegger's "clearing." Some non-Western traditions deepen that assumption; others reject it. Either way, bringing them in changes the analysis, not just the list of names.

**Traditions that resolve the original tension more cleanly**

Your first question was how people who know they are looking at a model can be captive. Buddhist philosophy has a direct answer.

In the parable of the raft (*Alagaddūpama Sutta*, MN 22), the Buddha compares his teaching to a raft built to cross a river. Carrying it on your head after you reach the far shore is a mistake. The raft is good, and clinging to it is the problem. Nāgārjuna makes this more precise with his two truths: the ultimate cannot be taught without relying on conventional truth (*Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* 24.10). Models are not optional; they are how the ultimate gets taught at all. The error is reification, treating the conventional as if it had its own inherent reality.

This removes the tension without the long negotiation with Plato's divided line that my first answer needed. The people in the image aren't wrong to study the orrery; they are holding the raft on their heads.

Mahāyāna and Chan Buddhism add a stock image: don't mistake the finger pointing at the moon for the moon. The lecturer's hand points at a model that contains moons. His audience watches the hand and the model, two steps removed from the sky.

Zhuangzi offers a close parallel: the fish trap exists for the fish, and once you have the fish you can forget the trap (ch. 26). He also supplies a sharper critique than anything I drew from Adorno. In chapter 12, an old gardener refuses to use a labour-saving well-sweep, saying that those who rely on machines develop a "machine heart." That describes the absorption in the image better than "instrumental reason": the orrery is a beautiful machine, and the attention it draws reshapes the people giving it.

Chapter 17 also has its own version of the Cave. A frog at the bottom of a well cannot be told about the ocean, because its world is shaped by the space it lives in.

**Traditions that intensify the light**

Some traditions push the light metaphor further than Plato does, and they fit the image almost too literally.

The Kaṭha Upaniṣad (2.2.15) says that sun, moon and stars do not shine in the ultimate, nor lightning, and "how then this fire?" Everything shines only by reflecting that light. The orrery displays exactly those luminaries, with a fire at the centre. Vedānta, however, would relocate the source. The light by which all this is seen is the awareness of the one who sees, not a sun beyond the window. On that view, the image makes its own mistake by putting the true light outside the room rather than in the seer.

Al-Ghazālī's *Niche of Lights* comments on the Qur'an's Light Verse, which compares divine light to a lamp in a niche, the lamp in a glass like a shining star. Al-Ghazālī argues that only God is light in the full sense, and every other light is borrowed. A lamp in a brass vessel at the centre of a model of the heavens is very close to the verse's own image. My earlier point that the room's light is "derivative" was, without my saying so, a point al-Ghazālī made at length.

Here the West/non-West division starts to leak. Islamic philosophy inherited Plato and Neoplatonism directly; Averroes wrote a commentary on the *Republic* itself. The light metaphysics is a shared Mediterranean and Persian inheritance, not a Western possession.

**Traditions that reverse it**

The most unsettling challenge comes from Japan. Tanizaki Jun'ichirō's 1933 essay *In Praise of Shadows* is aesthetics rather than a philosophical system. It argues that beauty lives in dimness, and singles out gold and lacquer glimmering by candlelight in a dark room. He saw the Western drive toward ever-brighter light as a kind of blindness.

Read this way, the image's moral flips. The darkened room is where the brass becomes beautiful and attention becomes possible, and the blade of daylight is an intrusion: glare, not truth.

Daoism partly agrees. The Daodejing advises knowing the white while keeping to the black (ch. 28) and calls the source of things "dark upon dark" (ch. 1). A Daoist would be suspicious of the orrery as a machine and equally suspicious of the beam as the answer.

**What this changes**

On which system the artist belongs to, my answer stays roughly the same, though on narrower grounds. The sources and the corporate setting make a Western conversation the likeliest home for the artist.

But the question of what the image means is different from the question of the artist's affiliation, and there the picture changes in two ways. Nāgārjuna's two truths, with the raft beside it, handles your original philosophical tension more cleanly than anything I offered from the Western tradition. And Tanizaki shows that every reading I gave, including the "critical" ones, rested on an assumption the image never argues for: that the light at the window must be better than the glow at the table.
