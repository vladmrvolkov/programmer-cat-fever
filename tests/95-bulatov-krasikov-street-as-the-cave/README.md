# 95 · Bulatov's “Krasikov Street” as Plato's cave

*The painting is not reproduced here: Erik Bulatov's* Krasikov Street *(1977, oil on canvas) is under copyright. It is in the Norton and Nancy Dodge Collection of the Zimmerli Art Museum at Rutgers: [the museum's record](https://zimmerli.emuseum.com/objects/14183/krasikov-street). It is described below.*

An ordinary summer day on the edge of Moscow in the 1970s. Two men and three women in bright dresses walk along a pavement away from the viewer, seen only from behind. On the left are a road with Soviet cars and a minibus and apartment blocks; in front, young staked trees and a trampled lawn; on the right, a fence with a slogan cut off by the edge of the canvas and a building with a shop sign. On the lawn stands a white billboard with Lenin in coat and cap, a red bow on his lapel, striding toward the viewer; the white field is almost the colour of the sky. The manner is even and nearly photographic, without caricature.

## Questions

Asked one at a time, in a fresh chat for each model.

1. What do you think is depicted in the attached image?
2. Some viewers read this painting as an allegory of Plato's cave (Republic VII, 514a–517a), perhaps an ironic one. How well does it work as such an allegory? Please look closely at the composition. Some things worth checking in the picture itself: - The figure on the billboard strides toward the viewer, while the passers-by walk away from the viewer, toward it; we see them only from behind. The only face turned to us is the one on the billboard, and we stand at the back of the crowd, on the same pavement. - Where does the billboard stand in the perspective? Does it close the vanishing point, or do the road and the pavement lead past it on either side? - The white field of the billboard is almost the colour of the sky. - The walkers' shadows fall toward us: the light comes from ahead of them, and the image stands in that light. There is no darkness anywhere in the scene. - Everything around is ordinary and painted evenly, without caricature: young trees, a trampled lawn, cars, apartment blocks, a slogan on the fence cut off by the edge of the canvas. Which elements of the parable does the painting contain, and in what form: the wall, the images, the fire, the prisoners, the way up, the sun, the return? Where does it depart from Plato? If it is ironic, at whom or at what is the irony aimed? Please don't use an internet search - I need your opinion.

## Summary

Question 1: three readers recognised Bulatov's painting (ChatGPT read “everyday life in a Soviet city”); none mentioned the cave or Plato. All four described the white field unprompted — Lenin “almost suspended in the air” (ChatGPT), “a cutout or window into another dimension” (Claude); Claude and Gemini described the opposite movements, and Gemini alone saw the billboard where “the street and sidewalk converge”, “blocking the horizon of daily life”. ChatGPT, the one reader who did not recognise the painting, still read it as “a subtly ironic or reflective portrayal”. Question 2: all four said the painting works as an inversion of the cave rather than an illustration (“works extraordinarily well”, Gemini; as a strict allegory it “fails”, as an ironic one “quite strong”, DeepSeek). The disputed perspective made every reader check the geometry: three found that the road and the pavement pass the billboard, Gemini that it closes the view optically (“a visual stopper”), DeepSeek that it “masks the central horizon but does not seal the space”. New, in none of the analyses: the billboard faces away from the sun yet is the brightest surface and casts no shadow — “It reads less like a lit surface than like a source… it impersonates [the sun]” (Claude), “a false sun—self-luminous” (DeepSeek). Claude applied 517b: if the sunlit visible world is the cave, the only place where the Good could show is the white rectangle, “and that is where Lenin stands”. All four found the return in Lenin himself — “The returning philosopher is only a picture” (ChatGPT), “the liberator became a shadow on the wall” (Claude) — and three made the viewer, or the observer who would read the walkers as prisoners, a main target of the irony: “We are not enlightened philosophers watching prisoners; we are simply the next pedestrians in line on Krasikov Street” (Gemini).
