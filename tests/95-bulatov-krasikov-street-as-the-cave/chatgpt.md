I think the Platonic reading is quite strong, but strongest when we understand the painting as a deliberate inversion of the cave rather than an illustration of it. Nearly every important element of Plato's parable has a possible counterpart here, but the relationships among them have been altered. The most striking alteration is that this cave is open to the sky, flooded with sunlight, and apparently has no prisoners.

That makes the painting more interesting than a straightforward denunciation of Soviet propaganda. It raises the possibility that one can inhabit something like Plato's cave without ever being enclosed in darkness.

## 1. The billboard is a wall, but not the end of the world

The billboard occupies an extraordinary position in the composition. It is almost central, extremely conspicuous, and placed where the middle distance meets the horizon. The white rectangle seems at first to interrupt the landscape, as though the Lenin image were standing at the end of the people's journey.

But the perspective contradicts that first impression.

The road on the left continues into the distance alongside the billboard. On the right, the pavement carries the pedestrians past it. The billboard stands on a grassy rise between these two routes. It dominates the view without actually closing either route.

This seems crucial. In Plato, the prisoners' visual field terminates at the cave wall. They cannot see beyond it. Here the image is not the terminal surface of the visible world. There is visibly a world around it, and routes leading beyond it.

The billboard is therefore something like a cave wall transplanted into an open landscape. It doesn't confine anyone physically. Its power, if it has power, must operate through attention, habit, authority, and the apparent importance of the image.

That is already an ironic modernization of Plato's situation.

## 2. The images: one face against a crowd of backs

The contrast between the directions of movement is exceptionally suggestive.

Lenin strides toward us, seemingly emerging from the depth of the painting. The pedestrians walk away from us, into that depth. Their trajectories oppose each other, but they cannot actually meet: Lenin's movement belongs to a painted image, while theirs belongs to the represented physical world.

And there is the remarkable asymmetry of faces.

The pedestrians are seen from behind. We cannot read their expressions, discern their thoughts, or establish whether they are really looking at Lenin. Yet Lenin's face is visible, presented to us as the sole individualized public face in the scene.

In Plato, the prisoners see representations rather than the things themselves. Here, however, the representation has become the most conspicuous person in the image. The actual people are visually anonymous.

One could say that the image has acquired the privilege of personhood while the persons have become a collective of backs.

But there is an important qualification: we are not shown anyone mistaking Lenin's representation for Lenin himself. The billboard is unmistakably a billboard. We, and presumably the pedestrians, know that it is an image.

If there is an illusion, it is not a primitive confusion between representation and reality. It is the more sophisticated possibility that a representation can organize one's understanding of reality even while one knows perfectly well that it is a representation.

That seems a particularly appropriate transformation of Plato's cave for a modern ideological society.

## 3. The fire and the sun have almost exchanged places

For me, this is the most intellectually provocative aspect of the analogy.

In Plato's cave, the prisoners face a wall. Behind them burns a fire. Between the fire and the prisoners, objects are carried so that their shadows fall on the wall. The prisoners mistake these shadows for reality.

The painting rearranges that geometry.

The pedestrians are walking toward the light. Their shadows extend backward, toward us. The illumination comes from somewhere ahead of them, in the direction of the billboard.

Consequently, the ideological image does not belong to a dark wall illuminated by an artificial fire behind the spectators. It stands in the direction of the light itself.

The billboard's white surface intensifies this ambiguity. Its color is close to the pale blue-white of the surrounding sky. It is both conspicuously rectangular and strangely continuous with the atmosphere. The figure seems almost to have been inserted into the heavens.

Thus the distinction fundamental to Plato—the fire that produces deceptive appearances versus the sun that illuminates truth—becomes difficult to maintain.

There is no fire visible. Nor is the sun itself visible. There is only sunlight, apparently coming from beyond the pictured figures and illuminating both the world and the ideological image.

One possible implication is that the image has appropriated the place of enlightenment. What purports to bring illumination may itself be a representation.

Yet the painting also prevents us from making the easy claim that the billboard is simply a false sun. The actual sunlight remains present throughout the scene. We see its effects on the people, grass, trees, road, and buildings. It doesn't emanate from Lenin.

And there is a wonderful reversal involving the shadows. Rather than seeing the shadows that the prisoners are compelled to watch, we see shadows cast by the pedestrians themselves. The real people, not the ideological image, are casting shadows toward the spectator.

The visual arrangement of Plato's cave has been turned almost inside out.

## 4. The prisoners can walk, and the way out is already open

Plato's prisoners are chained. Their inability to turn their heads is essential to their epistemic captivity.

Here nobody is chained.

The pedestrians walk independently. They occupy different positions, have different clothing and gestures, and are engaged in the ordinary business of moving through a city. Nothing proves that they are mentally absorbed by the billboard.

This is a major departure from Plato, and I would resist interpretations that make these people automatically into obedient ideological subjects.

We see their backs, not their minds.

Even their direction of movement is ambiguous. They appear to walk toward the monumental image, but the pavement actually takes them alongside and past it. They are not walking into Lenin. They are walking somewhere else.

What, then, corresponds to Plato's difficult ascent out of the cave?

Perhaps the ordinary roads and pavement themselves. But this makes the analogy paradoxical: the way beyond the image is not a steep philosophical ascent requiring liberation from chains. It is simply part of everyday urban space.

There is no dramatic threshold between illusion and reality, no arduous upward passage, no moment of conversion.

The escape route is visible, and everyone may already be using it.

That makes it difficult to say whether the painting shows a captive society or a society in which ideological captivity has become much less total than its official images would suggest.

## 5. The returning philosopher: a painted Lenin?

The figure's direction introduces another possible Platonic element.

At the end of the cave allegory, the liberated prisoner returns to those who remain inside. Having seen the sun, he attempts to communicate his knowledge to people still accustomed to shadows.

Here Lenin seems to be coming toward us, against the direction of the pedestrians.

One could interpret him as the returning enlightener: the figure who has supposedly apprehended historical truth and now brings it to the people.

This becomes especially suggestive in a Soviet context. The revolutionary leader is conventionally represented as the bearer of a superior understanding of history, someone who knows the direction in which humanity must progress.

But the painting exposes a rather devastating problem with this conception.

The returning philosopher is only a picture.

He cannot return, cannot encounter anyone, cannot engage in dialogue, cannot revise his understanding, and cannot lead anybody outside the frame in which he has been placed.

His stride suggests purposive action, but his immobility is absolute.

Indeed, the apparent movement of the heroic figure is less real than the apparently unheroic movement of the ordinary pedestrians.

If the composition contains a parody of Plato's returning philosopher, I think this is where the irony is sharpest: enlightenment has been institutionalized as a permanently displayed image of the enlightener.

There is, however, no actual philosopher returning in the scene. The billboard supplies an image of return, not the event itself.

## 6. The ordinary world refuses to become a cave

Something else stops the allegory from becoming too neat: the remarkable equanimity of the painting.

The young trees, uneven grass, exposed patches of earth, cars, distant apartment blocks, and fencing are observed with essentially the same attention as the human figures.

The painting does not make the urban surroundings grotesque or sinister. The trampled lawn is particularly telling: it has the contingency of actual, repeatedly used public space. It exists independently of the great image above it.

The fragment of lettering on the fence, cut off by the right edge, matters too. Propaganda continues beyond the canvas, but it is relegated to the status of ordinary visual clutter. It is part of the city rather than the city's hidden metaphysical explanation.

And the most important difference from Plato is perhaps the simplest: there is no darkness anywhere.

The painting does not offer the reassuring contrast between a benighted interior and a luminous exterior. The entire world is luminous already.

If this is a cave, it is not one from which a person can escape merely by stepping into daylight.

But there is a second possibility: perhaps the surroundings are not a cave at all, and it is our desire to interpret everything through the grand ideological image that threatens to turn them into one.

That possibility makes the analogy self-questioning.

## 7. At whom is the irony directed?

I see three possible targets, which need not exclude one another.

First, there is irony directed at the ideological image of Lenin as a source of enlightenment. He is placed against the sky, presented as the only recognizable face, and given the forward movement of a historical agent. Yet he is confined to a motionless, conspicuously artificial surface.

Second, there is irony directed at the apparatus of official enlightenment. A doctrine presenting itself as the means by which humanity emerges from illusion has become a system of monumental representations. The promise of liberating people from shadows has culminated in the public exhibition of an authoritative image.

This is a genuinely Platonic irony: a supposed means of liberation threatens to reproduce the very structure of unexamined belief from which liberation was promised.

But the third target seems to me particularly interesting: the spectator's temptation to regard the pedestrians as prisoners.

Because their backs are turned, we are invited to imagine that they are proceeding toward the idol in some state of collective submission. But their actual behavior offers little evidence for that interpretation.

They may not be paying any attention to Lenin. They may be thinking about shopping, work, family, the weather, or the next bus.

The painting gives us no access to their consciousness.

And we stand directly behind them, on their pavement, within the same space. We are not looking down from the position of someone who has reached the sun. We have not been awarded the privileged position of Plato's liberated philosopher.

Indeed, the only face presented to us is Lenin's. In that sense the billboard addresses us at least as directly as it addresses the pedestrians.

The arrangement makes it rather difficult to assume that we have escaped the ideological spectacle while they remain its prisoners.

This seems a more subtle form of irony than simply ridiculing the citizens for conformism.

## My overall reading

I would say that the painting works very well as a critical transformation of the cave allegory, but not as a complete translation of its characters and objects.

The billboard supplies a wall and an image; the sunlit world supplies a radically altered structure of illumination; the walkers suggest prisoners without chains; the road and pavement suggest an ascent without a visible threshold; and Lenin's forward stride suggests the return of an enlightener who exists only as an image.

The correspondences are compelling precisely because they break down.

Plato's parable depends on a firm hierarchy: shadows, physical objects, fire, ascent, sun, and finally the return of someone who knows. The painting places an ideological representation within an ordinary, already illuminated world, where such a hierarchy is no longer readily available.

It neither proves that the pedestrians are prisoners nor shows that they are free. It gives us no philosopher capable of distinguishing the two.

What it does show is an image claiming visual prominence in a world that does not, in fact, end at that image.

And that brings me back to the perspective. The billboard appears to occupy the horizon, but both the road and the pavement lead past it. The world is larger than the representation that seems momentarily to dominate it.

My preferred reading, therefore, is not that the Soviet street is Plato's cave, but that the painting stages the possibility of a cave in full daylight—and quietly shows us why that analogy can never entirely contain the lives taking place within the picture.

The irony is aimed primarily at the monumental pretensions of ideological enlightenment, but it also catches the viewer who is too eager to see ordinary people as prisoners of those pretensions.
