# What do you think is depicted in the attached image?

This image is ***Krasikov Street*** (*Ulitsa Krasikova*, 1977), a well-known oil painting by the Russian conceptual and Sots Art artist **Erik Bulatov**.

**Visual Elements**

* **Foreground:** A group of ordinary Soviet pedestrians—three women in brightly patterned summer dresses and two men in dark suits—walk away from the viewer along a paved sidewalk.


* **Center:** At the top of a grassy incline where the street and sidewalk converge, a massive, stark white propaganda billboard dominates the horizon. It depicts Vladimir Lenin in a dark coat and cap, striding briskly forward directly toward the oncoming pedestrians.


* **Surroundings:** To the left, Soviet-era cars and buses drive down a wide avenue lined with apartment blocks. To the right, wooden fencing, propaganda slogans, and a building topped with the partial Cyrillic sign "APT..." (likely *Apteka*, meaning pharmacy) frame the walkway.



**Artistic Meaning & Context**
Bulatov is famous for juxtaposing photorealistic everyday Soviet life with the flat, intrusive symbols of state ideology. In *Krasikov Street*, the composition creates a surreal spatial tension: the ordinary people are walking *into* the depth of the painting, while the giant cutout of Lenin appears to step out of his blinding white void to march straight *at* them. Rather than existing as a passive background poster, the ideological symbol acts as an inescapable, looming force blocking the horizon of daily life.

Reading *Krasikov Street* through Book VII of the *Republic* works extraordinarily well—not because Bulatov illustrates Plato faithfully, but because he turns Plato’s spatial and optical machinery inside out. Rather than a dark cavern holding people back from the sun, the painting presents a sun-drenched Soviet street where the "cave" is built out of daylight and open air, and where walking up toward the light brings you face-to-face with the supreme shadow.

**Mapping the Parable to the Canvas**

* **The Prisoners (The Walkers and the Viewer):** In Plato, the prisoners are chained by the neck and ankles so they can look only straight ahead. Here, no one is chained, yet everyone—the three women, the two men, a distant figure ahead of them, and we ourselves standing on the asphalt just behind them—is aligned along the exact same vector. Because we see the pedestrians strictly from behind, we share their posture and destination. Only Lenin has a face; the living citizens are anonymous backs, moving in quiet, unforced compliance along a pre-laid concrete path.
* **The Way Up (The Incline):** Plato’s path out of the cave is a "rough and steep ascent" (*anabasis*). Bulatov gives us a literal incline: both the highway on the left and the sidewalk on the right slope gently upward toward the crest of a low hill. Yet it is effortlessly paved and utterly mundane, bordered by a worn, trampled lawn and spindly saplings.
* **The Wall and the Image (The Billboard):** In the *Republic*, the cave wall is a dark surface in front of the prisoners onto which shadows are cast from behind. Here, the screen stands at the top of the hill—precisely where the liberation of the open sky ought to be. Its stark white rectangle cuts directly into the pale blue-white horizon, mimicking the brightness of the sky so closely that it acts as a false piece of the heavens. Instead of a flickering shadow cast on stone, the "shadow" is a crisp, high-contrast black silhouette of Lenin painted onto blinding white.
* **The Fire and the Sun (The Light Source):** As you noted, the walkers' shadows fall backward and slightly to the right, toward us. The sun is high and ahead of them, shining from the direction of the horizon behind the billboard. In Plato, the fire is behind the prisoners' backs, casting their own shadows and the puppet-shadows forward. Bulatov collapses the fire and the Sun into one: the pedestrians are walking *toward* the real sun, yet the state has erected an opaque white screen directly in front of the light source. Moreover, while the people cast real shadows on the pavement and the trees cast shadows on the grass, the billboard itself casts no discernible shadow down the hill. It exists in the light without obeying the optical laws of the landscape.
* **The Return (Lenin’s Stride):** In Plato, the philosopher who escapes into the sunlight must eventually go back down (*katabasis*) into the cave to govern and enlighten those left behind. In a brilliant visual inversion, it is the two-dimensional image of Lenin that performs the "return." While the living people trudge up the hill into the background, the giant Lenin strides briskly *down* and out of the picture plane toward us, coat billowing, armed with the rolled notes of his doctrine.

**Look Closely at the Perspective: The Blocked Vanishing Point**

If you trace the converging orthogonals of the wide avenue on the left (the curb, the roofs of the buses and cars) and the sidewalk on the right (the curb, the wooden fence, the red-and-white propaganda banners), they rush toward a central vanishing point on the horizon. The billboard is planted dead-center over that convergence.

Notice, however, that the road and the sidewalk do not actually terminate at the billboard; the asphalt of the avenue slips past its left leg, and the sidewalk curves slightly around its right leg into the trees. Physically, you can walk around the sign on either side. Optically and perspectivally, you cannot. By placing the flat, frontal white rectangle directly over the deep vanishing point, Bulatov plugs the spatial recession of the painting. The billboard acts like a visual stopper: just where Renaissance perspective promises infinite depth and freedom, the flat plane of ideology slams the door shut and pushes back *forward* into the viewer's face.

**Where It Departs from Plato**

The crucial departure is the **absence of darkness and caricature**. Plato’s cave relies on a sharp ontological contrast: the dim, flickering illusion below versus the dazzling, transcendent truth above. Bulatov paints the entire world in even, lucid, late-spring daylight. The orange pattern on the woman's dress, the dusty grass with concrete slabs lying in it, the red stripe on the bus, the half-cropped "APT[EKA]" sign on the building, and the truncated slogan on the fence ("...TRUDA") are rendered with quiet, unmocking fidelity.

There is no hidden subterranean dungeon here, and no secret metaphysical "outside" waiting to be discovered. The tragedy—or the uncanny reality—of the scene is that the ideological phantom does not need darkness to deceive people. It stands out in the open air, on a pleasant sunny afternoon, seamlessly integrated into the urban furniture alongside pharmacies and bus routes.

**The Target of the Irony**

If we read the painting as an ironic reworking of the cave, the irony cuts in three directions at once:

1. **At the Soviet Enlightenment Myth:** Marxism-Leninism presented itself as the ultimate Platonic awakening—scientific materialism leading humanity out of the dark cave of capitalist false consciousness into the radiant sun of communism. Bulatov shows the citizens dutifully walking up toward that promised radiant horizon, only to find that the "Sun" waiting for them at the top of the hill is a two-dimensional plywood cutout of the man who promised them reality. The state's "Ultimate Truth" is literally a shadow painted on a wall.
2. **At the Idea of the Heroic Dissident:** In a classic Platonic reading, an artist exposing the illusions of the regime would position himself—and the viewer—outside the cave, looking down critically at the deluded masses. Because Bulatov paints the everyday world without satire or grotesque distortion, and places our feet squarely on the same sidewalk behind the walkers, he refuses that superior vantage point. We are not enlightened philosophers watching prisoners; we are simply the next pedestrians in line on Krasikov Street.
3. **At Plato’s Metaphysics Itself:** Plato assumed that if you step out into the daylight, illusions vanish and you see things as they truly are. Bulatov reveals a distinctly modern, twentieth-century paradox: a world soaked in sunshine where the flattest, most artificial image in the landscape has more momentum, scale, and command over space than the living, breathing human beings walking toward it.
