# What do you think is depicted in the attached image?

This looks like **Erik Bulatov's painting *Krasikov Street* (1977)**, one of the best-known works of Soviet unofficial art. Bulatov was a leading figure of Moscow Conceptualism and Sots Art.

**What's literally shown:** an ordinary summer day on the outskirts of Moscow in the 1970s. Two men and three women in bright patterned dresses walk along a sidewalk, away from the viewer. Beside them are a wide road with typical Soviet cars and a minibus, standard prefab apartment blocks, young trees planted along a scruffy lawn, and a building on the right with signage and what looks like a propaganda slogan board. In the middle of it all stands a huge billboard of Lenin in his coat and cap, with a red bow on his lapel, striding forward with a newspaper in hand.

**What makes it interesting:** Bulatov paints everything in a deliberately flat, photographic, almost official style, but the composition sets up a tension.

- **Two opposite movements.** The people walk *into* the picture, while Lenin strides *out of* it, toward us. They are headed toward each other but will never meet. The ideological image belongs to a different kind of space than the real street.
- **The billboard as a hole in reality.** The white rectangle doesn't sit naturally in the landscape. It reads almost like a cutout or window into another dimension. This boundary between "real" space and ideological, social space was Bulatov's central theme.
- **Indifference.** None of the passersby look at Lenin. Propaganda was so ubiquitous in late Soviet life that it became part of the scenery, as unremarkable as the trees or the traffic. The everyday world goes on beside it, and the ideology both dominates the space and is ignored.

So it's less a political cartoon than a quiet, slightly uncanny observation about how ideology lived alongside ordinary life in the Brezhnev era, often unnoticed.

My overall view is that the painting works very well as a cave allegory, but as an inversion rather than an illustration. Nearly every element of the parable is present, yet each has been moved, and the moves carry the meaning. I can't say whether Bulatov had Plato in mind, and some details (especially the direction of light) I'm reading from a reproduction. Still, the correspondences are too systematic to be accidental to the picture's logic, whatever the painter's intent.

**The geometry**

You're right that the billboard doesn't close the vanishing point. The road recedes to its left, and the pavement bends past it on the right, along the fence. The billboard stands off-axis, on the lawn between two routes. It isn't a wall at the end of the way, filling the field of vision the way the cave wall fills the prisoners'. You can walk past it, and the walkers will. This is where *Krasikov Street* differs from Bulatov's earlier *Horizon*, where a red band like an order ribbon literally covers the horizon. Here the horizon is free.

Lenin's feet sit at roughly the height of the horizon line, which is also our eye level, since the walkers' heads rest on it. So he doesn't walk on the ground the people walk on. He walks along the line where the visible world ends. Combined with the white field being almost the colour of the sky, the rectangle reads two ways at once. It is a surface on which a figure is displayed, and it is a hole cut in the landscape through which a figure arrives from the light beyond.

**The elements of the parable**

*The wall* is the billboard: a screen with an image, set facing the people. But it is free-standing in open air, a fragment of wall rather than an enclosure. There is also a second wall, the fence on the right, which the walkers walk alongside and which carries a slogan cut off by the edge of the canvas. I'd map this onto the echoes. In Plato, the carriers' voices bounce off the wall and the prisoners think the shadows are speaking (515b). Here the image is mute, and the words are elsewhere, on another wall, truncated. The painter crops the slogan as carelessly as the walkers ignore it.

*The images*: Lenin is formally almost exactly a shadow, a nearly black figure on a luminous ground, with one red accent. That is what a shadow play looks like. Yet he is also the carried statue, an image of a man, and several removes deep. Bulatov's canvas depicts a billboard that reproduces a canonical, photograph-derived image of Lenin. That is Republic X's painter at the third remove from truth, plus one more. Meanwhile, the only actual shadows in the picture belong to the walkers, and they fall toward us. The painted Lenin, naturally, casts none. In this world, having a shadow is the mark of a real body, the reverse of Plato's hierarchy.

*The fire*: at first glance there isn't one. But Plato's own key matters here. At 517b he says the prison-dwelling is the region revealed by sight, and the fire is the power of the sun. Taken literally, that means a sunlit street is exactly the cave, and its sun is the fire. If so, the true sun, the Good, cannot appear anywhere in the visible world. And the only place in this picture that isn't visible world is the white rectangle, a blank almost the colour of the sky, a gap in the landscape. That is where the Good would have to show itself, and that is where Lenin stands.

There is a displacement too. In Plato the fire burns behind the prisoners. Here the light comes from ahead and to the left, from the same direction as the image. As far as I can tell, the billboard's face is turned more or less away from the sun, yet it is the brightest and most uniformly lit surface in the painting, with no gradation at all. It reads less like a lit surface than like a source. The image hasn't hidden the sun; it impersonates it. One could even see the red bow on the lapel as the last trace of the fire, a flame reduced to a decoration, though I'd call that a stretch.

*The prisoners* are the walkers. But they are unchained, moving, in bright summer dresses, carrying handbags, on their way somewhere. Above all, they don't look at the image. Plato's prisoners are absorbed in the shadows; they hold contests and give honours to whoever predicts them best (516c–d). Bulatov's walkers have simply stopped looking. They aren't deceived; they're indifferent. Plato's cure is a turning around of the soul (*periagōgē*, 518c). But these people already face the light, and the image stands in it.

So who is actually looking at the image? We are. The only face turned toward anyone is Lenin's, and it is turned toward the viewer, who stands at the back of the crowd, on the same pavement, at the same eye level. We are also the ones looking down at the walkers' shadows on the pavement. If there is a prisoner in this picture, the picture puts us there.

*The way up* is the road and the pavement. Plato's ascent is steep and rough, and the prisoner must be dragged up it (515e). Here it is level asphalt, nobody blocks it, nobody needs to be forced. And it leads past the image into more of the same: haze, apartment blocks, traffic, trees. The ascent has been laid flat. It is hard not to hear the Soviet "bright path" toward the "radiant future" in it, a horizon that recedes as you walk.

*The sun* is never shown. It is off-frame, ahead, fairly low. That is faithful to Plato, for whom looking at the sun itself is the last and hardest thing. But the sky is an ordinary pale, slightly hazy summer sky. There is no revelation, only weather, and the place where the eye expects the source has been taken by the white rectangle.

*The return* is Lenin himself. The figure striding out of the sky-like field toward the people, and toward us, is the one who has seen the light and comes back down to lead. That is the Republic's demand that the philosophers go back down (519c–520c), and it is precisely the Soviet myth of Lenin. Plato expected the returner to be mocked and perhaps killed (517a). This one met a different fate. He was turned into an image and installed in the cave: the liberator became a shadow on the wall. Nobody mocks him, because nobody notices him. He walks toward us, they walk toward the light, and the two movements never meet because they don't happen in the same kind of space.

**Where it departs from Plato**

The obvious departure, daylight instead of darkness, turns out to be smaller than it looks once you apply 517b. The real departures are four. There are no chains, no fascination, and no one who compels. The fourth seems to me the most important: there is no hierarchy of reality. Bulatov paints everything with the same even, photographic finish: the Lenin, the staked saplings, the concrete slabs in the trampled grass, the backs of the walkers.

Plato's parable depends on degrees of reality you can feel: darkness against light, the pain of turning, the dazzlement of the exit. Even light abolishes that phenomenology. As a result, two readings are equally coherent. Either the whole sunny suburb is the cave. Or this is the upper world, and the billboard is a piece of the cave carried out into the sun, its shadow-figure walking back down. The painting gives no way to decide, so the parable can't be "run" inside it. You can't tell from within whether you're in or out.

**At whom the irony is aimed**

I see three targets, in descending order of weight.

The first is the Soviet appropriation of the ascent narrative. Marxism-Leninism is an enlightenment story with a Platonic skeleton: false consciousness is the cave, the party is the philosophers who have seen the light, and the radiant future is the sun. The painting shows where that story ended up. The enlightener has become the cave's main image, and the sun is a white rectangle on a lawn.

The second, and in my opinion the sharpest, is the observer who would read the walkers as prisoners. That could be the dissident intellectual, the Western collector, or the gallery visitor. The walkers are painted without caricature. They are the most solid and dignified things in the picture, and they aren't fooled. The would-be enlightened observer is placed at the back of the same queue and is the only one who receives the image's gaze. And a gallery visitor standing before a flat image on a wall is, quite literally, in the position Plato describes.

The third, more lightly, is Plato himself. His diagnosis assumes the problem is not seeing. The painting shows a world where everyone sees perfectly well and nothing changes, because power that no longer depends on belief can't be dissolved by disillusionment. There is also a quiet joke in the medium: the painter, the very maker of images Plato would banish, uses an image to expose images.

What the irony spares is just as telling: the people, the young trees, the light. It is aimed at schemes of enlightenment, not at the ordinary world. I think that is why the painting has lasted beyond its political moment.
