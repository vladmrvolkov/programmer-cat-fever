# 1

## My reading

The waiter isn't offering a job. He's looking at a specimen. The cat knows exactly what he means, and "programmer" is the cover story she uses to turn him down without a fight. The bitter part is that the cover story is also true: the laptop is the only thing standing between her and a cage, and it's a kind of cage too.

## What's happening, and which details carry it

**Panel 1.** The chairs are up on the tables, so it's closing time. It's raining at night and no one else is there. Only two things on the page are yellow: her eyes and the laptop screen at the next table, with a cup beside it. That colour link tells me the laptop is hers and she's been working here a while.

**Panel 2.** We're behind her ears, seeing what she sees. His amazement has turned into something else: the grin is too wide, his eyes are shadowed, and he's leaning over her table with the pencil ready, as if to write up "whatever terms you like."

**Panel 3.** This is the key panel. She closes her eyes and recites a child's circus: acrobats, lollipops, cotton candy. Behind her, his shadow swells across the wall, arms spread over her, framed by window shadows that read as bars. The words are the brochure; the wall shows the reality. A talking cat in a circus isn't an acrobat. She's the exhibit.

**Panel 4.** The eye opens. It isn't dreamy; it's narrow, cold and awake. The highlight in it is a yellow rectangle, the laptop. She says "sounds tempting" while looking at her work.

**Panel 5.** This mirrors panel 1. His grin is gone and the notepad hangs at his side. The moment has been defused, and she's still waiting for her coffee.

## Does she understand? Why answer that way?

Yes, she understands. The proof is the cut from closed eyes to open eye: the dreaminess was a performance. No adult weighing a job talks about lollipops. For one panel she plays the cute, naive cat he wants her to be.

She deflects instead of refusing because she's alone in a closed café at night with a man who has just realised she's worth money. Saying "I won't be your uncle's freak show" would start a confrontation. Pretending she misheard a job offer gives him nothing to push against. To argue, he'd have to say out loud what he actually meant. It's the move people use on a creepy proposition: "Oh, you mean for work? Sorry, I'm busy."

It also corrects how he sees her. He saw a species; she answers with a profession.

## Why "Alibi"

An alibi is proof you were somewhere else, which clears you of a charge. Her "charge" is being a cat, a curiosity. Her alibi is the job and the laptop that proves it: "I'm not a miracle, I'm a programmer, I'm accounted for." For anyone who is visibly other, a profession often works this way. It's the paperwork that lets you exist in public without being treated as a spectacle. The fake misunderstanding is an alibi in the looser sense too: a deniable excuse.

There's a sadder layer as well. "Sounds tempting," said while staring at the screen, may be partly sincere. Her real choice isn't freedom versus work. It's a cage with cotton candy versus a laptop at midnight in an empty café. Being useful is the price of being treated as a person. That's where the old joke's mismatch of frames becomes a social one.

## What I'm assuming beyond what's drawn

- **That he's predatory.** Literally, he only grins and leans. The menace comes from the shadow, which is just lighting. A generous reader could see an overexcited young man.
- **That "circus" means cage.** This comes from the bar-like window shadows and from what a circus would plausibly want with a talking cat. Nobody says it.
- **That the laptop is hers.** She sits at a different table, so I'm relying on the yellow colour link.
- **That the reflection in her eye is the laptop.** It's the only yellow rectangle in a scene whose windows are dark.
- **That she's playing dumb.** In the original joke the animal genuinely misreads the offer. My evidence that she doesn't is one facial expression.

If the idea fails for a reader, it will most likely be at panels 3 and 4. A reader who takes the shadow as mood lighting and the eye as plain cattiness will see the original joke with darker art. For me, both details landed.

# 2


The threat half of your intent lands. The improvisation half mostly doesn't. I'm one reader, but I'm a useful test case: without being told, I read the circus as a cage, the dreaminess as an act, and the line as a deflection. I also confidently decided the laptop was hers and the job was real. So the page closed off the very question you meant to leave open.

## What works

**The cage.** The window grid falls across the tables like bars, and the chairs stacked legs-up make a little forest of bars of their own. With closing time, rain and no witnesses, the room feels unsafe without anyone doing anything.

**The shadow in panel 3.** It accurately matches his pose in panel 2: a man leaning on a table with both hands. Only the scale and the angle make it look like a pounce. That keeps the waiter's intent open: the drawing shows what she fears, not what he is. Panel 2 is shot from behind her ears, which supports this. We see his grin the way she sees it.

**The bookends.** In panel 1 he's simply amazed, and in panel 5 he's a deflated young man holding a notepad. Both make it easy to read him as harmless, which balances the menace in the middle.

**The stall.** "A circus, you say?" repeats the question, a classic way to buy time. The list then drifts into trivia, lollipops and cotton candy, which reads as someone talking to fill the air.

**The reflection.** The yellow rectangle in her eye ties the laptop directly to the moment the excuse forms. It's a clever, quiet device.

## What works against it

**The colour code says "hers."** On a one-colour page, the only yellow things are her eyes and that screen. Readers will treat that as a bond: same colour, same owner. That's exactly why I assumed it.

**The empty café says "hers."** With chairs up and no other customers, there's no one else the laptop could belong to. A reader resolves that puzzle by giving it to the only candidate. The one hint the other way is that she sits at a different table, and that's too quiet to compete.

**Panel 4 shows looking, not finding.** A single still of an eye that already reflects the laptop reads as "she's glancing at her work." For it to read as a discovery, the reader needs to see the moment before.

**Her eye in panel 4 already looks decided.** The narrow, cold look makes "Sounds tempting" read as sarcasm from someone who already has her answer. That fits deflection, but it skips the beat where she's still searching.

## What I'd change

1. **Make the find a visible beat.** Split the eye strip in two. In the first, the eye opens and reflects only darkness: the black window, or the door, with rain. In the second, the yellow rectangle appears. The yellow arriving in the eye is the idea arriving. This also strengthens the threat, because it shows her checking for an exit first.

2. **Add one counterweight on ownership.** A trace of an absent human at the laptop table would balance the yellow link: a coat over the chair, or a wet umbrella leaning against it, since it's raining. One detail is enough. Too much and you make it clearly not hers, which closes the question from the other side.

3. **Optionally, show the alibi taking hold.** In panel 5, his eyes could drift toward the laptop. This is risky, because it may over-explain. But it would show the story working on him, and it might make readers wonder whether it worked on them too.

## The deeper point

The alibi persuading the reader isn't the problem. You may even want that: the reader believes her, just as the waiter does. The problem is that nothing lets the reader catch it being built, so there's no double take. Change 1 alone would mostly fix that. Change 2 would make the double take hold.
