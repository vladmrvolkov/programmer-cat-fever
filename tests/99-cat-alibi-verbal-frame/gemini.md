# 1

The comic transforms a lighthearted joke about naive miscommunication into a tense, noir-tinged encounter about **othering, commodification, and quiet self-defense**. Instead of a cute animal who doesn't understand how the world sees her, this is a story about a sharp, sentient outsider who understands *all too well* that she is being sized up for a cage—and uses deliberate, deadpan absurdity to shut the threat down.

### 1. What is happening on the page (and what is assumed beyond the text)

The café is closed, or moments away from closing. Outside, rain pours into a dark street; inside, almost every chair is stacked upside down on the tables. Only two tables have chairs down: the one where the cat is sitting, and a table in the back right where an open laptop glows beside a coffee mug.

Because the laptop screen is colored in the exact same stark yellow as the cat’s eyes—the only color on the entire page—the visual implication is clear: **that is her laptop**. She has been sitting alone in an empty café late at night working, and has either stepped over to a fresh table to order another coffee and pastry, or the waiter has only just walked out from the back and realized that his lone late-night customer is a cat.

When the waiter sees her, his shock immediately curdles into opportunism. He leans across her table, invading her space with a predatory grin, and tries to recruit her for his uncle's circus.

### 2. The visual details carrying this reading

* **The single yellow accent:** Linking her eyes to the glowing laptop screen establishes her as a working professional before she ever mentions being a programmer. In Panel 4, the tiny yellow square reflected in her pupil visually ties her focus back to that screen—she is thinking about her work, not cotton candy.


* **Shadows as cages:** In Panel 3, as the cat tilts her head back, the waiter's shadow looms heavily over her against the wall, intersected by the grid of the window frame. Those window shadows fall across the wall and table like **prison bars**, visually spelling out what "joining the circus" actually means for a talking animal.


* **The body language shift:** In Panel 2, the waiter hunches over her eagerly, pen poised. By Panel 5, his posture has completely collapsed—shoulders slumped, arms hanging slack at his sides, notepad lowered. She has taken all the wind out of his sails without raising her voice.



### 3. Does the cat understand the offer, and why does she answer that way?

**She understands completely.** In the original joke, the punchline relies on the animal being endearingly oblivious. Here, **Panel 4 explicitly breaks that innocence**.

In Panel 3, she closes her eyes and tilts her chin up in a performance of dreamy nostalgia ("acrobats, jugglers, lollipops"), letting the waiter think his pitch is working. Then Panel 4 cuts to an extreme close-up of a single, narrowed, calculating yellow eye. She knows he wants to put her on display as a freak.

Her final line—*"But what would they need a programmer for?"*—is not a mistake; it is a **tactical feint**. If she gets angry, argues for her rights, or says *"I'm not an animal for your uncle's cage,"* she validates his framing that her species is the central issue and escalates a confrontation in an empty, closed café at night. By pretending to take his "job offer" strictly at face value, she forces the conversation back onto her own terms: she is a skilled professional, and his offer is simply irrelevant to her résumé. It leaves him with nowhere to go unless he wants to drop the polite pretense and openly admit he wants to exhibit her.

### 4. Why is the page called "Alibi"?

The title works on three overlapping levels:

1. **Her tactical "misunderstanding" as an alibi:** Feigning literal-mindedness gives her a bulletproof cover to reject an insulting, potentially dangerous proposition without triggering hostility. It also gives the waiter a face-saving exit—an alibi for his own behavior—so both of them can pretend he was merely offering an ordinary job that didn't match her skillset.
2. **The waiter's euphemism:** Framing the exploitation of a sentient being ("put a talking cat in a circus") as a generous employment opportunity (*"could you use a job?... whatever terms you like"*) is a classic social alibi—the respectable cover story prejudice uses to make itself sound benevolent.


3. **Her cover in the human world:** Being "just a programmer grabbing coffee and a pastry" in a quiet café on a rainy night is a mundane, unremarkable alibi for an outsider operating in human society—one that holds up right until she has to speak.

# 2


The page carries **about 80% of the author's intent remarkably well**—specifically the psychological turn, the sense of threat, and the fact that the cat is playing a deliberate game rather than being naive.

Where it stumbles is the **mechanics of the improvisation**: in my first reading, I assumed the laptop belonged to the cat, meaning she *was* a programmer using her real job to brush him off, rather than spotting a stranger's laptop and inventing a lie on the spot.

### What works for the author's intent

* **The "circus = cage" threat:** The closed café with upside-down chairs, the rain, the waiter leaning across her space, and especially the barred window shadows falling across the wall behind his looming silhouette in Panel 3 communicate the danger immediately without a word of exposition.


* **Buying time vs. calculating:** The transition from Panel 3 (chin tilted up, eyes shut in a theatrical sigh) to Panel 4 (the tight crop of a sharp, slit-pupil yellow eye) nails the shift from "performing" to "strategizing."


* **The visual breadcrumb:** Making the cat’s eyes and the laptop screen the only yellow elements on the page successfully tells the reader those two things are connected.


* **The waiter's ambiguity:** While he looks opportunistic in Panel 2, his slumped, awkward posture in Panel 5 leaves room for him to be a clueless kid who thought he was doing a good deed rather than a cartoon villain.



### What works against the intent

* **The laptop looks like hers:** Because the café is otherwise completely deserted, seeing an open, glowing laptop beside a coffee mug at the only other unstacked table naturally implies *she* was just sitting there. If it looks like her computer, her final line reads like a statement of fact rather than an improvised alibi.


* **Broken eyeline and geography:** In Panels 1 and 5, the laptop is behind the cat over her right shoulder. In Panel 4, however, her face is angled forward/right, and the eye is looking straight ahead rather than darting backward toward the other table. Without a clear look toward the prop, the viewer doesn't see her *discovering* the excuse.


* **The rectangular pupil reflection:** In Panel 4, the yellow highlight is a neat yellow square sitting inside her black pupil. Instead of reading as a reflection of a distant screen she just noticed, it looks like a stylized graphic or a sign that she's already locked into her own thoughts.


* **The waiter leans a little too sinister in Panel 2:** His hunched posture and wide, sharp-featured grin in Panel 2 push him strongly toward "predatory," slightly undercutting the intended ambiguity about whether he meant any harm.



### What to change

1. **Give the laptop a clear human owner:** Put a coat draped over the chair at the laptop table, or show a background patron walking toward the restroom in Panel 1. Once the viewer knows the laptop belongs to someone else, the cat's *"what would they need a programmer for?"* instantly clicks as a quick-witted bluff stolen from the room around her.
2. **Fix the sightline in Panel 4:** Either place the laptop table in front of her (visible in the background past the waiter) so her eyes can naturally flick toward it, or angle her head slightly in Panel 4 so her eye is clearly looking *back and sideways* toward the table behind her.
3. **Make the eye reflection naturalistic:** Replace the centered yellow square inside her pupil with a small, angled yellow glint on the edge of the cornea, consistent with catching light from a screen across the room.


4. **Dial back the waiter's grin in Panel 2:** Keep him leaning in (to preserve the physical intrusion), but give him a slightly more earnest, open-mouthed expression so the reader genuinely wonders whether he's a threat or just an oblivious guy pitching a terrible idea.
