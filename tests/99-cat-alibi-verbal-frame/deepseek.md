# 1

I read the page as a quiet refusal, not a misunderstanding. On the surface: a talking cat is a customer in a nearly empty café; the waiter is astonished, then pivots into recruiting her for his uncle’s circus; the cat dreamily lists circus attractions, says “Sounds tempting,” and then asks, “But what would they need a programmer for?” The joke lands because the waiter has offered her a job as a novelty, and she answers as if he had offered her a job in her actual profession.

**Does she understand what is being offered?** Yes — I think she understands it very well. The waiter’s “whatever terms you like” is not a normal job offer; it is the eagerness of someone who has found a spectacle. He is not asking about her skills. He is amazed by the miracle of a talking cat and immediately wants to monetize it. The cat knows that the circus does not need a programmer; it needs a talking cat. Her question is therefore not genuine confusion but a deliberate category mistake. She takes “job” literally in the professional sense, which forces the waiter to confront the absurdity: if he says “they don’t need a programmer,” he admits the offer was never about her work; if he says “they do,” it is nonsense. Either way, she keeps the upper hand.

**Why does she answer that way?** Because direct refusal — “I don’t want to be a freak show” — would be confrontational, vulnerable, and might not even be heard. Deadpan professional literalism is armor. It lets her decline without naming the insult, and it makes the waiter’s benevolent-exoticism visible without her having to accuse him of anything. She also gets to say “Sounds tempting” honestly: the circus is a fantasy of escape from programmer drudgery. But the “but” uses her professional identity as a way out. She is tempted by the change, not by being exhibited.

**Why is the page called “Alibi”?** Because her question is an alibi in the ordinary sense: a cover story, a plausible excuse. It gives her a socially acceptable reason to refuse — “there’s no programmer role” — while hiding the real reason, which is that she refuses to be reduced to a talking cat. It also gives the waiter an alibi: he can pretend his offer was a normal job opportunity rather than a polite form of discrimination. And it relocates her identity: “I am a programmer” is her alibi against being seen only as a species/novelty. The title names the social technique of the whole page — the polite exchange is a cover for a power mismatch.

**Details that carry this reading:**  
- The order pad — “coffee w/ cream, pastry” — establishes her as an ordinary customer; she wants service, not fame.  
- The laptop with the yellow screen in the background is the visual clue to her programmer identity. In the final panel it remains in frame while the punchline lands: the circus doesn’t need a programmer, but the café has one.  
- The single yellow eye in the close-up is the “tell.” The dreamy closed eyes in the previous panel are a performance; the sharp, slit-pupil eye shows calculation and agency. The yellow links her eye to the laptop screen — perception and profession.  
- The waiter’s “whatever terms you like” and his eager lean are recruiter language, but also the language of someone who thinks he is being generous while treating her as a commodity.  
- His large shadow on the wall in the circus panel makes the offer feel looming and socially powerful; the cat is small in the foreground.  
- The empty, rainy, heavily hatched café creates isolation: there are no witnesses, and the cat has to manage this moment alone.  
- The final silence from the waiter is important. He has no answer because the cat has exposed the gap between what he meant and what he said.

**What I assume beyond the drawing:** that the laptop is hers; that she is a programmer; that the waiter means “perform as a talking cat,” not “programmer for a circus”; that the circus is a fantasy of escape rather than a real career plan; and that the waiter is not a cartoon villain but an ordinary well-meaning person whose amazement has turned into a microaggression.

So the story is about being seen as a novelty rather than a person — and about the deadpan, socially acceptable “alibi” a marginalized subject uses to refuse that framing without giving the other person the satisfaction of seeing her wounded.

# 2

It carries the intent **partially, and mostly after the fact**. The page gives you the pieces for the “alibi” reading — the title, the yellow eye, the laptop, the deadpan final line — but it does not clearly stage the crucial sequence: **threat → stall → spot laptop → invent excuse**. The default reading remains the original joke: the cat really is a programmer, and the line is a profession-based punchline. My earlier reading is evidence of that: I took the final line as identity, not improvisation.

**What works for the intended reading**

- **The title “Alibi”** primes the idea of a cover story. It tells you to look for a socially acceptable excuse rather than a sincere career answer.
- **The waiter’s “whatever terms you like”** and his eager lean make the offer feel like recruitment into spectacle. It can be read as generous, but also as the language of someone who has found a novelty and wants to secure it.
- **The waiter’s looming shadow** in the circus panel supports the “circus means cage” reading without making him a cartoon villain. It keeps his intent ambiguous: he may be harmless, but the power imbalance is visible.
- **The cat’s first line** — “Yes, I’m a cat. Are you bringing my order or not?” — establishes her as a customer who wants normal service, not fame. That supports her later refusal to be turned into an exhibit.
- **The yellow eye close-up** is the best hinge in the page. It breaks the dreamy pose and suggests calculation. The yellow also links her eye to the laptop screen, which is the right visual logic for “she spots the laptop and uses it.”
- **The laptop remains visible in the final panel**, so a reader can retroactively connect it to the word “programmer.” That is the page’s main alibi clue.
- **The waiter stays smiling and ordinary**, so whether he meant harm remains open. That part works.

**What works against it**

- **The laptop is not clearly in her line of sight.** In the final panel she faces the waiter, and the laptop is behind her on another table. There is no panel where she actually looks at it. So the causal chain “she finds it with her eyes” is missing. Many readers will read the laptop as set dressing, or as hers, not as the thing she just noticed and used.
- **The dreamy panel reads as sincere, not as a stall.** Closed eyes, upturned face, soft “Sounds tempting” — it looks like genuine longing for the circus. If she is buying time, we need a small tell: one eye not fully closed, an ear angled toward the laptop, a tail tightened around the chair leg, claws gripping. Right now the “performance” is not visibly a performance.
- **There is no visual sense of a cage.** The café is calm and empty; the circus list is pleasant. The waiter’s shadow is ominous, but the cat herself shows no fear or tension. If “circus means cage” is the emotional engine, the page needs one restrained visual rhyme — window shadows falling like bars across her, or the chair back reading as a cage — not a literal cage.
- **The final line is the original joke’s punchline**, so it strongly implies she really is a programmer. It arrives smoothly, with no beat of invention. The reader has no reason to think she is improvising rather than stating her occupation. The title “Alibi” helps, but it is doing too much work alone.
- **The yellow eye/laptop link is too subtle.** The eye is yellow; the screen is yellow. But without a reflection or a glance, it can read as mere color consistency. The page needs one more beat to make that link causal.

**What I would change**

1. **Add a silent beat panel between “Sounds tempting” and the final line.** Show the cat’s eye flicking to the neighboring laptop; show the yellow screen reflected in her pupil, or her pupil narrowing toward it. Then she turns back and delivers the line. This is the single most important change: it turns the final line from identity into improvisation.

2. **Make the laptop clearly a neighboring table’s object, and show code or a terminal on the screen.** That gives her a visible source for the word “programmer.” It also preserves the ambiguity: she may or may not be a programmer, but she can see the screen and use it. Do not show her using the laptop earlier.

3. **Make the dreamy pose visibly a performance.** Keep the deadpan style, but add one contrary detail: one eye slightly open toward the laptop, an ear swiveled, her tail wrapped tight around the chair leg, or her claws gripping the seat. The reader should feel she is reciting the circus brochure while thinking about escape.

4. **Use the window shadows as bars across her body in the circus panel.** Not a literal cage — just a compositional rhyme. That would carry “circus means cage” without making the waiter a villain.

5. **Add a lettering beat before the final line.** Split the balloon: “But…” then a small pause, then “what would they need a programmer for?” The pause signals invention. Keep the waiter’s expression eager and oblivious, not malicious, so his intent stays open.

So: the page has the right ingredients, but the key action — **she sees the laptop and builds the alibi from it** — is not staged clearly enough. The title and the yellow eye point toward the author’s intent, but the visuals still let the original “cat is a programmer” reading dominate. One silent glance panel would fix most of it.
