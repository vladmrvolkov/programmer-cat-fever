It's a good joke, and the structure is the reason it works.

The engine is a frame mismatch. The waiter and the reader both know exactly what's remarkable about this cat — it talks. The whole café scene is built around that reaction. Then the punchline reveals the cat has been operating in a completely different frame the entire time: to itself, it's a professional with a specialty, and the miracle of speech is just background noise. The humor isn't in the cat being witty; it's in the cat being *earnest*. It genuinely wants to know what the circus's technical needs are.

The deadpan is what sells it. A lesser version has the cat be offended ("I'm not a performing animal!"), which is the obvious move and much weaker. Here the cat is doing polite due diligence on a job offer. That's funnier and also sharper — it's a joke about professional identity swallowing everything else, which lands especially well aimed at programmers.

A few craft observations:

The satchel in panel 1 is a nice quiet plant. But the laptop only appears in the final panel, which means the reveal isn't quite *earned* visually — it's asserted. If a corner of the laptop were poking out of the bag in panel 1 or 2, the ending would reward a re-read instead of just landing.

Panel 5 is the one I'd argue about. The circus daydream is charming, and it does buy a beat of delay before the punchline, but it slightly undercuts the premise: if the cat is picturing acrobats and cotton candy, it's not fully oblivious to what a circus *is*. The joke is strongest if the cat's blind spot is total. As drawn, the daydream reads more like "the cat finds this appealing" than "the cat is calculating."

Panel 1 also does a lot of scene-setting for a six-panel gag. Comics pay for setup in real estate, and an establishing wide shot of an empty-ish café is expensive for what it delivers.

And the final line is a touch loose. "But I wonder, why do they need a programmer?" has two hedges before the payload. Something like "Though what does a circus need a programmer for?" gets to the operative word faster — and in a punchline, the last word should be the one doing the work.

Minor stuff. The core mechanism is solid, and it's the kind of joke that gets better the second you realize the cat was never confused at all — everyone else was.
