# How would you explain diptychs attached?

A diptych is a composition of two images placed side by side that work together to communicate a single idea, narrative, or visual message.

In this diptych, the central theme is consumerism, desire, and the power of advertising.

The two photographs create a visual dialogue:

\- Left image – The temptation to buy: A man looks through sunglasses featuring the word “BUY,” with an aspirational lifestyle advertisement behind them. The composition suggests how advertising shapes our perception, encouraging us to see the world through the lens of consumption.

\- Right image – The act of consumption: The man is now inside a clothing store, trying on a T-shirt featuring the same word, “BUY.” He looks at himself in the mirror, suggesting how consumer culture influences not only what we purchase but also how we see and present ourselves.

The connection: The repeated word “BUY” visually unites the photographs, while the progression from looking at merchandise to wearing it creates a subtle narrative.

The diptych also explores the relationship between looking and being looked at, desire and identity, advertising and self-expression.

### Suggested artistic statement

> “BUY” — A Diptych About Consumer Desire
>
> This photographic diptych explores the relationship between advertising, perception, and consumer identity. In the first image, the desire to consume is communicated through the act of looking; in the second, consumption becomes part of personal identity. The repeated word “BUY” connects the two moments, transforming a simple shopping experience into a playful commentary on how commercial messages shape what we desire and how we see ourselves.

What makes the diptych particularly effective is its visual irony: in the first photograph, the man looks through the message “BUY”; in the second, he becomes the carrier of that same message.

The two images are individually understandable, but together they create a stronger conceptual story: from seeing the advertisement to becoming the advertisement.

# What if I told you that another diptych in the attachment visualizes the same idea?

That would fundamentally change how I interpret the first diptych—and reveal a much more interesting conceptual connection between the two.

I would argue that both diptychs explore the same central idea: the difference between what we see, what we are encouraged to believe, and what is actually there.

The second diptych references The Emperor's New Clothes, but reimagines the story through anthropomorphic cats and Japanese woodblock-print aesthetics.

In the left panel, the emperor appears magnificently dressed, surrounded by attendants and subjects who bow before him. In the right panel, he stands naked, exposed to the gaze and ridicule of the crowd.

The contrast is not simply between being dressed and undressed. It is between collective belief and the exposure of an illusion.

And this brings us back to the first diptych.

In the photographic diptych, the man initially looks through sunglasses bearing the word "BUY." In the second photograph, he wears that same message on his body.

In the illustrated diptych, the emperor's clothing similarly operates as a visual construction of identity and status. When that construction is removed, our perception of the character changes.

### What connects the two diptychs?

I see three particularly strong connections:

1\. Perception is constructed. We do not necessarily see things as they are. Advertising, fashion, authority, and social expectations influence what we perceive.

2\. Clothing becomes a language. In both works, what someone wears communicates something beyond the garment itself: identity, status, desire, or submission to a social narrative.

3\. The observer participates in the illusion. The sunglasses in the photographic diptych and the spectacle-like imagery at the top of the illustrated panels introduce the act of looking itself. They suggest that seeing is never entirely neutral.

### A deeper interpretation

There is also an interesting reversal between the works.

The first diptych shows how an external message becomes internalized. The man goes from looking through the word "BUY" to displaying it on himself.

The second diptych shows how an internalized collective belief can be dismantled. The emperor goes from being perceived as magnificently dressed to being recognized as naked.

One depicts the construction of an illusion; the other depicts its collapse.

This suggests that the common theme is not necessarily consumerism, as I initially proposed, but something more universal:

The power of appearances to shape our understanding of reality.

Or, more provocatively:

> We see what we are conditioned to see, until something makes us question it.

I would be particularly interested in whether the two diptychs were deliberately conceived as variations on The Emperor's New Clothes. Because if so, the repeated references to eyewear, clothing, and public perception make the conceptual connection remarkably direct.
