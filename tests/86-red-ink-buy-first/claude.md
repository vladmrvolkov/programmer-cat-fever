# How would you explain diptychs attached?

This diptych makes a point about **consumer culture absorbing its own critique**. It borrows from John Carpenter's 1988 film *They Live*, in which special sunglasses let the hero see past ads and billboards to the hidden commands underneath them, such as OBEY, CONSUME, and BUY.

**The left panel is the revelation.** A man stands at a shop window showing a sunlit fantasy: the Mediterranean coast, a laughing woman, champagne, lemons. The ad sells a lifestyle rather than a product. When he holds up the sunglasses, the lens strips away the color and glamour and shows the real message in gritty black and white: BUY. He has seen through the illusion.

**The right panel is the punchline.** The same man is now inside a store. He wears the sunglasses as a fashion accessory and a T-shirt with BUY printed across it, price tags still hanging off. He is admiring himself in the mirror, clearly pleased. The insight that was supposed to free him has been turned into merchandise, and he bought it. He hasn't escaped the system. He has become a walking billboard for it and paid for the privilege.

The mirror adds a clever detail. His reflection reads backwards, so as he looks at himself he can't actually read the message he's wearing. The glasses that once exposed the truth are now just style.

The Situationists of the 1960s had a name for this: **recuperation**, the way radical ideas get defused by being packaged and sold back to people. Real life offers a neat example. Shepard Fairey's OBEY campaign was directly inspired by *They Live*. It began as guerrilla street stickers and became a mainstream clothing brand. Barbara Kruger's anti-consumerist text art, whose look was famously echoed by Supreme's logo, followed a similar path.

The short version is that seeing through consumerism doesn't stop you from consuming, because the market will happily sell you your own disillusionment.

# What if I told you that another diptych in the attachment visualizes the same idea?

It does, and it adds a few twists.

The story underneath is Hans Christian Andersen's *The Emperor's New Clothes*, drawn in the style of Edo-period woodblock prints (cat prints were a specialty of the ukiyo-e master Utagawa Kuniyoshi). The fable is itself about seeing through a collective illusion, so it pairs naturally with the *They Live* idea.

**The left panel is the illusion and its unmasking.** The cat emperor parades in magnificent robes with a long train, a parasol, spear-carrying guards, and a crowd bowing in obedient unison. Off to the side sits a small kitten in round dark glasses. This is Andersen's child, the one who says what no one else will. The inset at the top shows what the glasses reveal, in drab grey, just like the black-and-white lens in the first diptych: the emperor is naked. There's also scrawled graffiti on the wall, including a cat face with a crown, a sign of street-level mockery.

**The right panel is what happens after the truth gets out.** Everyone now knows the emperor is naked, and that knowledge has become a uniform. The crowd wears matching blue coats printed with 裸 ("naked") and points and jeers in unison. They're exactly as conformist as when they were bowing; only the script has changed. A shop stall sells the whole thing back to them: prints of the naked procession, the round glasses on a display rack, and among the masks, one that looks like the Guy Fawkes mask from *V for Vendetta*. That's a pointed real-world reference, since the mask became the global face of anti-establishment protest while its official versions are licensed merchandise. The graffiti from the left panel is gone, replaced by the souvenir shop. The inset glasses have gone from grey to cheerful full color: the truth is now the new spectacle.

Two details make this version darker than the first. The emperor is still under his parasol, still guarded, still processing calmly. Being exposed hasn't cost him anything, and the mockery has simply become part of the parade. And the kitten, unlike the smug man in the BUY shirt, is clutching its face in dismay. The first diptych shows the buyer happily wearing his disillusionment. This one shows the original truth-teller watching their insight turn into a costume for the same crowd that once bowed.

Both diptychs follow the same two beats, revelation and then recuperation, with the glasses as the shared symbol: first a tool for seeing, then an accessory for sale.
