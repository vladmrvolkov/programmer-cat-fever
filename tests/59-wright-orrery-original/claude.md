Yes, I recognize it. It's Joseph Wright of Derby's *A Philosopher Lecturing on the Orrery* (c. 1766). It wasn't painted as an allegory of one specific concept. It depicts a public science demonstration of the kind lecturers gave in 18th-century England. But it supports a few strong philosophical readings, and my top guess is the **clockwork universe**.

An orrery is a geared brass machine that reproduces the planets' motions, which is exactly the Newtonian and deist picture of the cosmos: a vast mechanism designed and set running by a divine "watchmaker," following laws that reason can grasp. The lecturer, standing above the apparatus and explaining its workings, almost plays the role of that clockmaker-god.

A close second is the **Enlightenment as the "light of reason."** The only light in the room comes from a lamp standing in for the sun, hidden behind the silhouetted boy in the foreground. Every face is lit by it, and the reactions range from the children's wonder to the adults' quiet contemplation. Knowledge literally illuminates people in the surrounding darkness.

If you were thinking of **Plato's Cave**, that reading works too, maybe more cleverly than it first seems. There's a dark room, an artificial light source, and people gazing not at the real cosmos but at a man-made model of it, lit by a lamp that only stands in for the true sun. That parallels the fire and the puppets in Plato's allegory.
