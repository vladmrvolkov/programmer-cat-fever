My read is that the comic is about a foreign professional in Japan whom nobody sees as a person. The panels added at the end of each page are where the author shows the reader what the waiter can't see.

**The badge decides it.** The photo on her ID lanyard isn't a cat. In every panel where it's visible, it shows a human woman with dark hair and brown skin, plausibly South or Southeast Asian, which is where much of Japan's imported IT workforce comes from. This is Kuniyoshi's device turned around. In his prints the face was a cat's, but the crest told the buyer which actor it was, while the censor saw only a cat. Here the badge plays the role of the crest. The waiter is the censor: he sees a cat. Read that way, "A talking cat?! Incredible!" is "日本語お上手ですね," praise that is really surprise that an outsider can do something ordinary. Her flat "Are you bringing my order or not?" is the reply of someone who hears it every day.

**The last panel of page 1 shows the order.** She asked for coffee with cream, and the coffee is black. He was so struck *that* she spoke that he didn't register *what* she said. Next to the daifuku sits a metal fork, though the Japanese customer in the first panel eats his with his hands. That's the "gaijin fork": he served her what he assumed she needed, not what she asked for. Then there's the close-up of her paw, which is how he sees her hand. A Japanese reader will likely hear 猫の手も借りたい ("so short-handed I'd borrow a cat's paw"), and "he'd hire you on any terms" is that idiom taken literally. A labor-starved economy wants the paw, but the only job it can imagine for it is in a circus.

**The circus is a pre-built slot.** Compare the folding screen across the two pages. On page 1, the acrobat hanging from the pole is a human child. On page 2, once the offer is made, the same acrobat in the same red kimono is a cat. The place was waiting for her, and it's a child's place, full of lollipops and cotton candy. That's essentially the cute picture you said the models kept producing, and here it appears inside the story as the stereotype. The bakeneko lore says the same thing in folk form: a cat may walk upright, talk, dress, and dance, but it never stops being a cat. Kuniyoshi's cats stood in for actors, courtesans, and dancers, people on the margins. So the role offered to the outsider is performer.

**At "programmer," his mood shifts.** At "talking cat" he was delighted; at "programmer" he sweats, and the hitodama appears. I think this is where the double meaning works. To him it's ghost-fire: a cat that won't be a curiosity and turns out to be a skilled professional is a bakeneko. A cute cat is a marvel; a cat with a laptop and a corporate badge is uncanny. Her long tail fits too, since long-tailed cats were the ones folk belief said would turn into monsters. To the reader it's 火の玉: she throws herself into her work and opens the laptop the moment the conversation ends. It also echoes the manga convention of a soul drifting off a worn-out office worker, which is why the circus honestly "sounds tempting." He reads the sign as a monster; we read it as devotion, or exhaustion.

**On the maneki-neko, I'd push back on your reading a little.** I don't think the charm failed. As drawn here, it raises its left paw, the one that calls people in, and a black one keeps evil out. A person walked in past it, and nothing evil came. If this LTR version mirrors an RTL original, a Japanese reader would see the right paw, the one for money. That sharpens his complaint: he was hoping she'd be a windfall through his uncle's deal. Either way, his line ("you meow, the bowl stays empty… guess you're not a cat") blames the talisman. Faced with a charm that contradicts his suspicion, he decides the charm is broken rather than his suspicion. That's how prejudice protects itself. The line is also accidentally true: she isn't a cat, as her badge shows.

The final wide panel resolves it. She works on her laptop among the other customers, and no one looks up. Being served and left alone was all she wanted.

So the lesson as I read it: a society that beckons outsiders (a maneki-neko on every counter, a labor shortage it openly admits) still can't see them as people. It marvels that they can talk, doesn't hear what they ask for, hands them the fork, and offers them the stage. When they turn out to be colleagues, it finds them uncanny and blames the charm rather than its own eyes. Without the two added panels, it's a joke about a cat. With them, it's about the waiter.
