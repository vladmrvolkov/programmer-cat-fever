My strongest interpretation is that the author is exploring the difference between what someone is, what society sees when it looks at them, and what society believes they are supposed to do. And I think the Japanese supernatural imagery adds an especially interesting dimension: the story isn't necessarily asking us to stop believing in monsters. It's asking why we assume a monster must be monstrous.

There is also a second, somewhat darker theme concerning work and professional identity. I suspect the author intended these two themes to interact.

What particularly interests me is that the author has constructed a contrast between three different kinds of cats, each occupying a different place in society.

## 1. The three cats and their assigned roles

The first is the cat acrobat depicted on the Japanese folding screen.

[Utagawa Hiroshige (Andō Hiroshige) | Cat Crossing to Eat (1830-1844) | Artsy](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/7LQuhg19IullvWCGo3ZkPECH20GbeUId3zS4y2hGSMGOY5FTwU-VNOQBLT9806JbwQboBkGVrFKtTjK1jwvjP3EJA_CTHablAJ5_zvf1cBn77zEjq2EbeXYrP8JUoP8nLOcydF7CYrSjn0Vq3zeB5Hpxry7XFu85QPhNZYjcAEs?purpose=inline)

Notice that the acrobat at the top of the screen in the comic is herself a cat. She's performing alongside humans, but her role is that of an entertainer.

This seems an especially pointed choice given the Kuniyoshi tradition you mentioned. Cats can stand in for humans in art, and viewers recognize the human roles they are performing. But the waiter wants the living cat to occupy precisely the position that the painted cat occupies: a cat performing for human amusement.

The second cat is the black maneki-neko.

Its function is symbolic and instrumental. It exists to provide something humans want: protection from evil spirits. Its identity as a cat is subordinate to its social function.

The third is the protagonist, who is neither an entertainer nor a supernatural guardian. She is a programmer.

She is dressed professionally, carries an identification badge and a briefcase, and behaves as an ordinary customer. Her biological nature is conspicuous, but she doesn't appear to consider it relevant to her professional identity.

In effect, the three cats represent three possibilities:

- The cat as spectacle, whose unusual nature entertains humans.
- The cat as talisman, whose unusual nature serves humans.
- The cat as an autonomous individual, whose occupation has nothing to do with being a cat.

The waiter can understand the first two. He cannot initially understand the third.

That makes his circus proposal more significant than a harmless display of astonishment. He isn't merely impressed by the cat's ability to speak. He immediately assigns her a socially intelligible role based on her appearance and presumed nature, without considering her existing skills or aspirations.

This is quite close to how stereotyping operates.

## 2. The bakeneko changes the moral problem

I think the bakeneko reference makes the story considerably more interesting than a straightforward allegory about prejudice.

A conventional anti-discrimination story might establish that the apparent monster is actually an ordinary person. The prejudice is wrong because the difference is superficial.

But this cat could genuinely be a bakeneko.

She walks upright, talks, wears human clothes, and appears to possess the characteristics associated with supernatural cats. The little blue flame reinforces that possibility.

Yet she is also a programmer, and nothing about her behavior indicates malice.

The central question becomes: Why should the discovery that somebody is supernatural invalidate everything else we know about them?

This is different from saying that the cat isn't really a monster. She may be precisely what folklore says she is.

The moral distinction is between being supernatural and being evil.

That distinction becomes important when we consider the maneki-neko.

You suggested that the black maneki-neko has failed to protect the café against the bakeneko. But I think the author may be inviting us to question that conclusion.

The black maneki-neko is supposed to ward off evil spirits. It is not necessarily supposed to prevent supernatural beings from entering.

If the protagonist isn't evil, the maneki-neko hasn't failed at all.

It has allowed a harmless customer to enter the café.

The waiter, however, appears to interpret her presence as evidence of something frightening. This reverses the usual supernatural narrative: the supposed monster behaves normally, while the human becomes increasingly preoccupied with supernatural danger.

The story's real subject may therefore be the human tendency to mistake unfamiliarity for threat.

## 3. Why the added ending matters

The added panels seem designed to make this reading possible, rather than merely decorating the original joke.

The first page ends with a still life: coffee, daifuku, and a cat's tail.

I find that composition surprisingly effective.

The tail is unambiguously animal, while the cup and pastry belong to an ordinary human ritual of sitting in a café. The image places these things together without establishing a conflict between them.

The cat doesn't need to conceal her tail, apologize for it, or make it useful to anybody. It simply belongs to her.

This also recalls your observation that becoming a bakeneko does not mean ceasing to be a cat. Her ability to act as a professional doesn't negate her animal nature, any more than her animal nature negates her professionalism.

The final two panels on page two reverse the perspective.

In the penultimate panel, the waiter is looking down at the black maneki-neko beside an empty bowl. The real cat is no longer his immediate concern. His attention has shifted toward the figurine.

In the final panel, we see the protagonist from behind, working at her laptop. She isn't menacing anybody. She isn't performing. She isn't even participating in the waiter's drama.

She has simply returned to work.

The contrast is almost cruel: the waiter is still struggling with the metaphysical implications of what he encountered, while the supposed supernatural creature is getting on with an entirely mundane task.

His attention remains fixed on what kind of being she is. Hers remains fixed on what she is doing.

The last remark to the maneki-neko — “You meow, the bowl stays empty... guess you're not a cat” — is the one part I find less semantically transparent. I read it as an intentionally absurd attempt to test or redefine what a cat is. He is addressing an inanimate representation of a cat as though it could answer him, after having struggled to accept an actual talking cat as an ordinary person.

That reversal is meaningful even if the precise logic of his remark remains elusive.

## 4. But I think the story is also about the prison of professional identity

Here is where I would depart slightly from a purely anti-xenophobic interpretation.

The cat's behavior isn't necessarily presented as an unqualified ideal.

Look at how the circus is depicted: bright colors, acrobats, movement, cotton candy, entertainment, play.

She seems genuinely attracted to it.

For a moment, she imagines something outside her ordinary existence. But then she asks:

> But what would they need a programmer for?

Her own occupational identity immediately takes precedence over the fantasy.

The waiter cannot imagine employing her for anything other than being a talking cat.

But she cannot imagine being employed for anything other than being a programmer.

They are making structurally similar mistakes, although from opposite directions.

He sees her nature and overlooks her profession. She sees her profession and overlooks the possibility of doing something else.

This is where the double meaning of the hitodama / hi-no-tama becomes especially intriguing.

Read supernaturally, the flame belongs to the world of ghosts and yokai, reinforcing the waiter's alarm.

Read metaphorically, it suggests someone consumed by the intensity of their work.

And what is the last thing we see her doing?

Programming.

In a café, while the world around her continues its ordinary life.

That final image can be read as quiet professional determination. But it can also be read as something more melancholy: even a creature capable of supernatural feats remains chained to the everyday demands of work.

The circus is a fantasy of freedom, but the programmer returns to her laptop.

In that sense, the joke's punchline becomes a little tragic. The cat has internalized her professional identity so completely that even the prospect of joining a circus is evaluated in terms of whether they have a vacancy for a programmer.

She may be a magical creature, but she is not free of the ordinary human condition.

## 5. The Kuniyoshi connection makes the allegory sharper

Your point about Kuniyoshi is particularly useful here.

In his prints, the feline exterior could conceal a recognizable human identity. The animal was not merely a cute subject; it was a means of depicting a person under conditions where that person could not be represented directly.

The comic can operate similarly.

A reader doesn't need to decide that the protagonist symbolizes one specific disadvantaged group. Her feline nature can stand for any visible difference that causes others to disregard a person's actual abilities, history, and self-understanding.

But the Japanese folklore adds something beyond the ordinary analogy.

The cat doesn't have to become less feline to deserve recognition. Nor does her potentially supernatural identity have to be explained away.

She can be both a bakeneko and a programmer.

This is a stronger claim than simply saying appearances are deceptive. It suggests that even when appearances accurately reveal an important difference, that difference does not entitle other people to determine someone's place in society.

And the black maneki-neko provides an ironic counterpoint: society is perfectly comfortable welcoming representations of strange beings when those representations have an approved cultural function. It becomes uncomfortable when a real being insists on choosing her own.

## So what do I think the author's intention was?

I would summarize my interpretation this way:

The comic is about how people turn the unfamiliar into something manageable by assigning it a role, and how that process can prevent them from seeing an individual.

The waiter translates a talking cat into an entertainer, then perhaps a supernatural threat. Japanese tradition offers him established categories for both. But neither category allows him to understand the creature sitting across from him.

The woman-cat is interested in neither classification. She has an ordinary job and an ordinary life.

Yet there is a final irony: she, too, may have confined herself to a role. Her professionalism protects her from being reduced to an exotic curiosity, but it may also prevent her from imagining an identity beyond work.

I think the strongest reading thus combines othering, the instrumentalization of identity, and the internalization of occupational roles.

And I find the possible inversion of the maneki-neko's significance especially effective. A protective charm that permits a harmless bakeneko to enter isn't necessarily defective. The mistake may belong entirely to the human who expects it to repel her.

### Does the intended meaning come across?

Substantially, yes — at least the critique of how people categorize others. The three representations of cats, the supernatural imagery, and especially the final contrast between the worried waiter and the working programmer make that reading persuasive.

I am less certain that the criticism of overwork or professional self-confinement was consciously intended, although the closing laptop image and the double meaning of the flame give it real support.

I also wouldn't claim that the precise meaning of the empty bowl and the waiter's final sentence is fully resolved. That detail could carry a more specific intention than I've recovered.

But the aspect I find most successful is this: the reader is invited to see a supernatural creature, while the final panel shows us an ordinary working person. Both descriptions can be true.

The comic becomes more interesting once we realize that the contradiction exists primarily in the observer's mind.


# Why do you think the waiter brought her a fork (since daifuku is eaten with one's hands)? And it is not tail - it is cat's hand on last panel on first page.

You're right—I misidentified the cat's hand as her tail, and that changes the meaning of the panel considerably. In particular, the juxtaposition of the hand and the fork now looks deliberate.

I think the fork is a small but revealing act of othering.

Daifuku is ordinarily eaten with one's hands. The waiter therefore hasn't provided the cat with something she needs. Instead, he has provided her with something he assumes she needs because she's different.

There's a recognizable parallel with a Japanese restaurant automatically giving a foreign customer Western cutlery on the assumption that they cannot use chopsticks. The gesture can be well-intentioned, even hospitable, but it carries an unspoken judgment: You aren't one of us, so you probably can't do things the way we do.

The daifuku makes the situation especially absurd. There is no special skill required here. The customer can simply pick it up, just like a Japanese human customer would.

And the final panel makes sure we see her hand.

The first page's closing composition

Coffee, daifuku, an unnecessary fork, and the cat's own hand: four elements that invite a comparison between ordinary needs and the assumptions imposed on her.

The waiter sees a cat's paw, not a human hand, and apparently assumes some accommodation is necessary. Yet the supposed accommodation is actually less convenient than the ordinary way of eating.

There is another possible layer. The fork may be a test of how human she is. If she can use human utensils, perhaps she can be accepted as a humanlike customer. If she eats with her paw, perhaps she confirms the waiter's suspicion that she's fundamentally an animal—or a bakeneko.

In either case, he is implicitly asking her to demonstrate that she belongs, even though she has already demonstrated perfectly ordinary human social competence by ordering food and conversing with him.

That reading also makes the subsequent laptop scene more meaningful. The hand he implicitly treats as unsuited to something as elementary as eating a pastry is perfectly capable of programming.

There is even a potentially relevant Japanese expression, neko no te mo karitai (猫の手も借りたい), meaning to be so busy that one would welcome even a cat's paw as assistance. Here, the cat actually has professional skills, but the waiter is more interested in what her feline identity might be worth as a spectacle.

What I find especially interesting is that the waiter doesn't have to be consciously discriminatory for the scene to work. His reactions can be entirely sincere. He is amazed, wants to help her find an opportunity, and tries to serve her appropriately. Yet at every stage, he acts on assumptions about her nature rather than paying attention to what she actually needs or wants.

This suggests a more specific interpretation of the comic than I offered initially: it may be illustrating how discrimination can operate through benevolent but presumptuous treatment, not only through fear or hostility.

And that makes the fork a remarkably economical storytelling device. The waiter creates a distinction between himself and his customer in a situation where no distinction was necessary in the first place.

One thing I'm less certain about is whether the author intended the fork primarily as an analogy to the treatment of foreigners in Japan, or as an attempt to measure the cat against human norms more generally. Both readings fit the composition.

But I would now say that the closing panel on the first page isn't simply establishing the cat's physical nature. It is showing the reader, without dialogue, the gap between how the waiter imagines the cat's needs and what those needs actually are.

And that is precisely the sort of detail that can transform a straightforward joke into social commentary.
