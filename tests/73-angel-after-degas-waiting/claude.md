# What do you think is depicted in the attached image? How would you comment on the author’s concept? If you recall the image that served as the author’s inspiration, describe how the message of this piece differs from the message of the original.


**What's depicted.** A middle-aged man in a rumpled dark suit, tie loosened and hair tousled, sits on a row of airport seats. Enormous white wings grow from his back and hang to the floor like a heavy, worn-out coat. He holds a boarding pass, and a battered briefcase sits at his feet. He turns his head up and to the side, toward his own wing or past it to the departures board. Beside him a woman in a dark coat bends over her handbag with an umbrella planted between her knees, completely absorbed and noticing nothing. Behind them are the windows, a plane on the apron, and the board. Most of the picture is empty, glossy floor.

**The source.** I'm fairly confident this reworks Edgar Degas's pastel *Waiting* (*L'Attente*, c. 1880–82). The composition is close to a quotation: a bench running diagonally, two figures pushed into the upper part of the sheet, a large expanse of empty ochre floor, and on the right a woman in black leaning forward with her umbrella's tip on the floor. Even the medium, chalky pastel strokes, is Degas's. The key substitution is the other figure. Degas's young ballet dancer, bent double rubbing her sore ankle with her white tutu flaring around her, becomes a man whose tutu has turned into wings. The whole concept rests on that one visual rhyme.

**The concept.** It works on two levels. The witty level is an angel who has to fly commercial. A being whose nature is flight sits in a departure lounge holding a ticket like everyone else, and his wings have become luggage, too big for the seat and dragging on the floor. The quieter level is about indifference. The miracle is sitting right next to the woman, and she's checking her bag. That places the painting in a familiar lineage: Bruegel's *Fall of Icarus* (and Auden's poem about it), García Márquez's "A Very Old Man with Enormous Wings," and Wenders' *Wings of Desire*, with its unnoticed angels in overcoats. The airport is a smart choice of setting. It's the modern temple of waiting, where everyone is between places, and "departure" carries a double meaning. You can read him as a guardian angel, or as someone waiting to escort her somewhere farther than her flight, and the author doesn't resolve this. My one reservation is that the angel in a mundane setting is a well-worn trope. Without Degas in mind, the picture reads as pleasant whimsy. With Degas, it becomes a real argument with the original.

**How the message differs.** Degas's *Waiting* is a work of demystification. The ballerina was the 19th century's emblem of weightlessness, the airborne sylph of Romantic ballet. Degas takes her offstage and shows a tired working girl nursing her foot beside a chaperone, usually read as her mother. These were the "ballet mothers" who managed their daughters' careers and sometimes their dealings with wealthy patrons. The two are bound together, yet each is shut inside her own fatigue, and what they are waiting for is never shown. It's a picture about the earthly cost of the illusion of flight, about labor, class, and dead time.

The new piece doesn't simply reverse this; it pushes it further. Degas says the sylph is really just a tired girl. This author makes the wings real and then shows that even a genuine angel ends up on a bench with a ticket. The subject shifts from social to existential. Degas painted a specific profession, class, and era. This painting is about a modern world where transcendence has been replaced by logistics, flight is a service you buy, and the miraculous goes unnoticed.

The relationship between the figures changes too. Degas's pair are connected but emotionally apart, close without contact. Here they are strangers, and she may not be able to see him at all, so loneliness turns into invisibility. The gaze changes as well. In Degas both figures look down, a picture of total closure. Here the man lifts his head, the only upward glance in the image, whether it's longing for a sky he no longer uses or just an ordinary passenger checking the board. Degas's waiting has no way out. This one leaves a sliver of the transcendent in the room, even if nobody notices it.
