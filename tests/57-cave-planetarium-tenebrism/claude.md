My guess is **Plato's Allegory of the Cave**, staged as a modern office meeting.

The composition borrows directly from Joseph Wright of Derby's *A Philosopher Lecturing on the Orrery* (1766). The lecturer in red stands over a mechanical model of the solar system, a note-taker stands at the left, and a silhouetted figure in the foreground blocks the lamp, as the child does in the original. Wright's painting celebrated Enlightenment science. This version seems to turn it into something more Platonic:

- **The blinds are drawn.** The group sits in a dark room, cut off from the outside.
- **The real sun is right there.** A bright shaft of actual sunlight cuts through the blinds, and nobody turns toward it.
- **Everyone stares at a substitute.** They're absorbed in a candle standing in for the sun inside a model, like the prisoners watching firelit images instead of reality.
- **Shadows loom on the wall.** The lecturer's huge shadow spreads across the back wall, echoing the shadows the prisoners mistake for the real world.
- **The audience is captive.** The lanyards, coffee cups, and rapt or anxious faces make it read like a corporate briefing, where people accept a presenter's model of reality without looking outside.

So the satire seems to be that the experts gather around a comfortable representation of the truth while ignoring the real thing streaming in through the window. A secondary reading could be the "map is not the territory" idea, but the darkened room, the shadows, and the neglected sunlight point strongly to Plato.
