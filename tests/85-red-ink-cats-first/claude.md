# How would you explain diptychs attached?

This diptych is a retelling of Hans Christian Andersen's "The Emperor's New Clothes," done as a mock Japanese woodblock print with cats. It's a modern pastiche, but it deliberately echoes Utagawa Kuniyoshi, the 19th-century artist famous for his cat prints and for using animals to poke fun at authority when direct satire was censored. The two panels work as a before-and-after: the illusion, then the moment it collapses.

**Left panel: the illusion.** The tabby emperor parades in magnificent layered robes with a huge red-and-gold train carried by attendants, a red umbrella held over him, and spear-bearing guards beside him. This is what everyone *claims* to see, and we're shown it too, so we're briefly in on the pretense. The crowd at the bottom bows with their faces to the ground, so no one actually has to look at him. The gray inset at the top, framed as a pair of spectacles, shows the truth: a naked cat being fawned over. It's drawn in muted monochrome because the truth exists but no one is saying it. The kitten in dark sunglasses sits quietly with its paws pressed together, apparently seeing everything and keeping it in. Even the graffiti on the wall, including a smirking cat face wearing a crown, hints at mockery hidden behind public respect.

**Right panel: the reveal.** Now we see what's really there. The emperor wears nothing but his little black cap and sandals, yet he keeps walking with the same composed expression, just as he does at the end of Andersen's story. The umbrella-bearer still holds the umbrella, and another attendant reaches out with empty paws, still miming the invisible train. The crowd has stood up and is pointing and laughing. Their blue coats are all printed with 裸, which means "naked," so the crowd literally wears the word nobody dared say. The kitten now has clear round glasses and covers its mouth, as if it has just blurted out the truth or is stifling a laugh. The spectacles inset at the top is now in full color: the truth has gone from private and muted to vivid and shared.

**Background jokes.** The shop on the right sells spectacles, so clear sight is literally available to anyone who wants it. It also sells prints of the procession itself, a sly nod to how ukiyo-e turned public scandals into instant popular media. One of the masks on display, a grinning white face with an upturned mustache, looks a lot like the Guy Fawkes mask modern protesters wear, which may be a deliberate wink. The title cartouche stacks grand titles on top of each other, including 帝 (emperor) and 大王 (great king), and that piled-up pomposity is part of the joke too.

**The overall message.** The diptych is about collective pretense: everyone goes along with an obvious falsehood out of fear or flattery until one honest observer says it out loud, and then the whole crowd can admit what it saw all along. The glasses motif carries the idea that seeing clearly is available to everyone; the hard part is being willing to say what you see. The emperor marching on regardless adds a final barb about power that won't admit it's been exposed.


# What if I told you that another diptych in the attachment visualizes the same idea?

You'd be right, and the second diptych arguably takes the idea one step further.

The core device is the same: a pair of glasses that strips away a glamorous surface to reveal what's underneath. This one borrows directly from John Carpenter's 1988 film *They Live*, where special sunglasses show that billboards and magazines secretly carry blunt commands like OBEY, CONSUME and BUY. In the left panel, the ad is pure seductive fantasy: a sunlit Mediterranean coast, champagne, lemons, a carefree woman in a flowing dress. Through the lens, all of it collapses into one gray, gritty word. That's the same visual grammar as the cat print, where the luxurious robes appear in full color and the spectacles inset shows the naked truth in muted monochrome. In both, color is the illusion and gray is the reality.

The right panel is where it gets sharper. The man is now *wearing* the truth-revealing glasses, and he's in a shop trying on a T-shirt that literally says BUY, price tags still hanging off it, admiring himself in the mirror with a satisfied smirk. He has effectively become the emperor: paying for clothing whose real content is nothing but the sales pitch, and preening over it. Even the mirror plays a part, since his reflection shows the word reversed. Looking at himself, he can't read the message straight.

What makes the pairing interesting is how each one treats seeing the truth. In the cat diptych, seeing leads to exposure. The crowd wears 裸, "naked," on their backs, and wearing the word is an act of naming the truth together. In this diptych, the man also wears the word, but it's the advertiser's word, not the truth-teller's. And the glasses now have nothing left to uncover, because the message no longer needs to hide. He'll wear it on his chest voluntarily, and the anti-consumerist lens itself has become a cool accessory. Awareness hasn't given him immunity; the critique has simply been sold back to him.

There's a real-world echo of exactly this. Shepard Fairey's OBEY street art drew on *They Live*, and it eventually became a successful clothing brand, so the film's warning about consumer manipulation ended up printed on shirts sold at retail. The cat print hinted at the same thing in its background, with a shop selling spectacles and prints of the emperor's humiliating parade.

So the two diptychs share one idea but tell different chapters of it. The cats say: everyone sees the truth, but no one dares to say it. The man in sunglasses says: everyone sees the truth, says it out loud, and buys it anyway. A modern emperor wouldn't even need swindlers; he'd pay for a shirt reading "NAKED" and wear it proudly.
