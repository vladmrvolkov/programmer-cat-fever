# How would you explain diptychs attached?

This two-panel artwork (diptych) is a whimsical, anthropomorphic **ukiyo-e** (Japanese woodblock print) depicting an elaborate **daimyo procession** (*daimyō gyōretsu*) in which all the humans have been replaced by cats. It belongs to a famous genre of satirical and playful cat art (*neko ukiyo-e*) that flourished in the Edo period, most closely associated with the master artist **Utagawa Kuniyoshi** (1797–1861) and his school .

### 🐾 Central Narrative: The Cat Lord’s Procession
The diptych captures the solemn yet humorous pomp of a feudal lord’s journey:
*   **The Main Figure (Left Panel):** A massive, dignified tabby cat stands at the center, dressed in a luxurious, patterned *haori* and *hakama*, holding a closed fan. Two smaller cats in dark formal wear trail behind, carefully supporting the long, ornate train of his robe, while an attendant shelters him with a large red umbrella and another guards him with a spear.
*   **The Retinue (Right Panel):** The procession continues with spear-bearers, umbrella carriers, and retainers. In the foreground, a group of cats in blue uniforms marked with the character “**裈**” (a pun on *fundoshi* or formal wear) point and gesture excitedly at the spectacle. A small cat wearing glasses watches from the ground, and in the background, a shop displays fans, masks, and prints, adding a bustling, street-level vibe.

### 🎨 Satirical & Cultural Context
*   **Animal Satire (*Mitate*):** This is a classic example of *mitate* (parody or visual substitution). By replacing rigid samurai and daimyo with cats, the artist gently mocked the strict social hierarchy and the costly, ostentatious processions of the Edo period, turning a serious political event into a charming, lighthearted parade .
*   **The “Cat Crazy” Era:** Utagawa Kuniyoshi was famously obsessed with cats, reportedly keeping many in his studio. He and his contemporaries produced hundreds of prints featuring cats in human roles—as actors, sumo wrestlers, and merchants—which were immensely popular among the townspeople of Edo .
*   **Title Cartouche:** The red cartouche on the top left reads “**御暑帝大王郎**” (*Gosho Tei Daiōrō*), which appears to be a fictional, grandiose title (roughly “His Majesty the Great King of the Summer Palace”) designed to heighten the mock-heroic comedy of the scene.

### 🖼️ Visual Details & Inset Frames
*   **The “Lens” Insets:** At the top center, circular and oval frames act like magnifying glasses or telescope views, focusing on the dignified, upright posture of the main cat and his attendants. This device adds a layer of voyeuristic humor, inviting the viewer to inspect the “royal” family closely.
*   **The Graffiti Wall (Left Panel):** On the white wall behind the procession, faint, childlike line drawings of cat faces are sketched, a delightful detail that breaks the fourth wall and adds to the playful, unserious atmosphere.
*   **The Shop Stall (Right Panel):** The background on the right shows a typical Edo-era shop selling *uchiwa* (fans), *tenugui* (towels), and *omamori* (amulets), grounding the fantastical cat parade in the familiar commercial life of the city.

This diptych is a masterful blend of technical skill and playful imagination, offering a window into the Edo-period love for cats and the era’s vibrant, satirical visual culture.

*   **The Cat Diptych:** Mocks the rigid, costly, and ostentatious *daimyo* processions of feudal Japan. By making the "lord" a literal fat cat, it exposes the pageantry of the samurai class as an empty, performative circus.

### 2. The Illusion of Choice and Identity
*   **The Cat Diptych:** The cats are dressed in human finery, pretending to be noble. They are just cats playing a role.
*   **The Modern Diptych:** The left panel shows the man looking through sunglasses at a glamorous, idealized lifestyle (the Amalfi coast, champagne, a beautiful woman). The reflection in his glasses literally spells out **"BUY"**—the lens through which he views the world has been entirely manufactured by advertisers. In the right panel, he is trying on the "identity" (the t-shirt) and looking in the mirror, convinced he looks good, completely unaware (or uncaring) that he is a walking billboard for his own subjugation. 

### 3. The "Gaze" and the Spectacle
*   **The Cat Diptych:** The small cats in the foreground point and stare at the grand procession. The "lens" insets at the top invite us to inspect the "royal" family closely, adding a layer of voyeuristic humor.
*   **The Modern Diptych:** The man is caught in a loop of gazing. He looks *at* the display window, he looks *through* the manipulated lens (glasses), and he looks *at himself* in the mirror. He is both the spectator and the spectacle, entirely absorbed in his own consumption.

### 4. The Literalization of the Metaphor (Mitate)
*   In Japanese art, **mitate** is the act of parodying classical themes with modern or absurd substitutes (like cats for samurai). 
*   The modern image does the exact same thing. The artist replaces the subtle, psychological manipulation of modern advertising with a loud, literal substitute: the word **"BUY"**. It is a *mitate* of the modern shopper.

In both cases, the artists are using humor to break the fourth wall. The ukiyo-e master Utagawa Kuniyoshi is saying, *"Look how silly the rigid social hierarchy of the samurai is,"* and this modern artist is saying, *"Look how silly and transparent the illusion of consumer choice is."*
