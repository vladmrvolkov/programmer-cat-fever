Joseph Wright of Derby’s 1768 painting, *An Experiment on a Bird in the Air Pump*, depicts a natural philosopher depriving a white cockatoo of air inside a glass bell jar—a dramatic scene that serves as a metaphor for several major philosophical concepts depending on how you look at the table:

**The Enlightenment (Empiricism vs. Sensibility)**
At its core, the painting illustrates the tension between the Age of Enlightenment’s cold scientific rationalism and human empathy. As the experiment unfolds, the witnesses react in starkly different ways: the young lovers on the left are absorbed only in each other, the man in green watches the mechanical apparatus with detached curiosity, and the horrified young girls weep for the suffocating bird while being comforted.

***Horror Vacui* ("Nature Abhors a Vacuum")**
The air pump experiment (originally conducted by Robert Boyle) directly challenged Aristotle’s long-standing philosophical postulate that a true vacuum cannot exist in nature. By pumping the air out of the glass globe, the natural philosopher proves the existence of a vacuum—and demonstrates its fatal consequences for living beings.

**Playing God, Determinism, and *Memento Mori***
With wild hair and an intense, direct gaze aimed straight at the viewer, the natural philosopher holds his fingers on the stopcock at the top of the glass jar. He acts as a secular deity holding the power of life and death in his hand: a twist of the valve will restore the air and save the bird, while inaction will kill it. Meanwhile, the older man seated on the bottom right stares deeply at a cloudy jar containing a submerged skull, embodying *memento mori*—the philosophical reflection on the inevitability of death.

**The Burkean Sublime**
Proposed by philosopher Edmund Burke just a decade before this painting was made, "the Sublime" describes the visceral mixture of awe, fascination, and terror experienced when witnessing overwhelming natural forces or mortality from a position of safety—captured in the dramatic *chiaroscuro* lighting and the wide-eyed expression of the younger girl looking up at the glass globe.

**A Precursor to *Schrödinger’s Cat* (Indeterminacy)**
To a modern eye, the bird trapped inside the sealed chamber—suspended on the knife-edge between life and death until the scientist acts—often reads like an 18th-century visual metaphor for Schrödinger’s famous thought experiment. Even the boy by the moonlit window holding the cord to the empty birdcage leaves the outcome unresolved: we cannot tell whether he is hoisting the cage away because the bird is doomed, or lowering it so the surviving bird can be returned.
