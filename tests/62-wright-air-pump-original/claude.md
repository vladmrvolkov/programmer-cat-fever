That's Joseph Wright of Derby's *An Experiment on a Bird in the Air Pump* (1768), now in the National Gallery in London. My guess is **Schrödinger's cat**.

The parallels are strong. A living creature is sealed in a vessel, and its fate is suspended between life and death. Nobody in the room, including us, knows whether the lecturer will open the valve and let air back in before it's too late. Wright keeps the outcome open on purpose. The boy by the window holds the empty cage, and we can't tell whether he's lowering it for the bird's return or putting it away. The experimenter's hand hovers over the stopcock while he stares straight out at the viewer, as if we're the ones who'll collapse the uncertainty. Of course, it's a retroactive metaphor, since Schrödinger's thought experiment came along about 170 years later.

If that's not the one you had in mind, a few other readings fit well. The painting is often treated as an emblem of the **Enlightenment** itself: a single hidden light of reason surrounded by darkness. It's also a textbook example of Burke's **sublime**, where terror and fascination are felt at once. And the audience splits neatly between cool rational detachment (the timing man, the young couple absorbed in each other) and raw empathy (the weeping girls). That split dramatizes the old tension between reason and feeling, and the question of whether an animal's suffering counts morally, Descartes's "animal-machine" versus Bentham's "can they suffer?"
