# Attached are four versions of the same painting. What do you think is depicted, and how do the four versions differ?

The painting depicts a group of museum visitors standing in a wood-paneled gallery, admiring a massive panoramic mural or natural history diorama of a golden grassland with distant blue mountains. The visual irony lies on the right side of the landscape, where a hidden maintenance door camouflaged with painted grass has been left propped open, breaking the illusion to reveal a concrete utility closet containing an electrical box, a fire extinguisher, and a yellow mop bucket. While all the adults gaze toward the painted horizon, the scene plays with who notices the peek behind the curtain.

**How the four versions differ**

* **Version 1:** The young boy in the blue hoodie stands quietly holding the man's hand with his right arm down at his side, looking up toward the man while the open utility closet remains empty.


* **Version 2:** The boy raises his left hand (creating an awkward anatomical quirk, as his left hand is also holding the man's hand) to point up toward the man or the landscape, while the closet stays empty.


* **Version 3:** The boy extends his right arm to point directly at the open maintenance door, trying to draw attention to the exposed utility room.


* **Version 4:** The boy's arms return to the resting position from the first image, looking up at the man, while a custodian in a grey uniform appears inside the utility closet holding the mop.

# Some viewers read this painting as an allegory of Plato's cave (Republic VII, 514a–517a). Which of the four versions works best as an allegory of the cave, and which works best as an ironic allegory of it? It may be the same version or two different ones. For each choice, say which detail decides it and how it corresponds to the parable: the prisoners, the images, the way out, the sun, the return. If a version is ironic, at whom or at what is the irony aimed?

It is also possible that the four work better together, as a series, than any one of them alone. If you think so, say in what order they should be seen and what the series shows that no single version does. Please don't use an internet search - I need your opinion.

---

**Version 3** works best as a direct allegory of Plato’s cave, while **Version 4** (closely rivaled by **Version 3** read subversively) works best as an ironic allegory. However, viewing them together in the sequence **1 → 3 → 2 → 4** unlocks a complete narrative arc about the failure of enlightenment that no single frame can achieve on its own.

**The Best Direct Allegory: Version 3**

The decisive detail in Version 3 is the boy’s outstretched right arm pointing directly at the open maintenance door. In Plato’s *Republic* (515c–517a), enlightenment begins with the *periagoge*—the painful turning of the neck away from the shadows toward the mechanism of the illusion—followed by the escaped philosopher's attempt to point out the truth to those still chained.

* **The Prisoners:** The adult museum-goers, bound not by iron chains but by gallery etiquette, the velvet rope, and their own absorption in the spectacle.


* **The Images (Shadows):** The sweeping painted panorama of golden grasslands and distant mountains.


* **The Way Out:** The camouflaged maintenance door swung open in the hillside, breaking the two-dimensional spell.


* **The Sun:** The bare, warm utility bulb on the concrete ceiling—the literal source of truth revealing the material structure behind the painted world.


* **The Return:** The boy himself. Having seen past the illusion, he remains tethered to the older man’s hand, trying to redirect the gaze of the "prisoners" toward the real world, only to be completely ignored.



**The Best Ironic Allegory: Version 4 (and the subversive side of Version 3)**

The decisive detail in Version 4 is the custodian in the grey uniform standing inside the concrete closet, looking down at her mop bucket while the boy looks quietly up at his father.

In Plato’s parable, ascending out of the cave leads to a blinding, transcendent realm of pure Forms, beauty, and sunlit nature, while the cave itself is dark and dreary. Here, the painting completely inverts Plato’s metaphysics:

* **The Ironic "Images":** The illusion inside the "cave" (the gallery) is actually the sunlit, sublime natural world.


* **The Ironic "Way Out" and "Sun":** Stepping through the threshold of truth doesn't lead to majestic pastures; it leads into a cramped, windowless concrete utility room lit by a cheap fixture.


* **The Ironic Philosopher-King:** Instead of contemplating divine Forms outside the cave, the only inhabitant of the "true" world is a weary janitor with a mop and a fire extinguisher.



The irony is aimed squarely at **idealism and philosophical romanticism**—the belief that piercing the veil of illusion will liberate us into a higher, nobler reality. It also pokes gentle fun at the **bourgeois museum-goers**, who seek a sublime connection with unspoiled nature inside a climate-controlled building, remaining oblivious to the invisible working-class labor (the custodian) right in front of them that keeps their aesthetic fantasy clean and functioning.

**Why They Work Best as a Series: The Order 1 → 3 → 2 → 4**

While Version 3 captures the act of revelation and Version 4 captures the socioeconomic and metaphysical irony, arranging the four paintings in the order **1 → 3 → 2 → 4** transforms a static visual gag into a miniature tragedy about how society domesticates curiosity:

1. **Version 1 (The Quiet Question):** The boy notices the discrepancy and looks up at the man, trusting that the adult—his guide to the world—either sees it too or will explain it.


2. **Version 3 (The Urgent Testimony):** Realizing the man is still staring at the fake mountains, the boy points directly at the open doorway with his free right hand. This is the peak of his rebellion against the illusion.


3. **Version 2 (The Crisis of Doubt):** Ignored by every adult in the room, the boy's gesture falters. He points tentatively toward the painted landscape/the man's line of sight. Even the visual "glitch" of his extra left arm works poetically here: he is literally split between holding onto the authority figure's hand and gesturing toward the consensus illusion, questioning his own eyes.


4. **Version 4 (Resignation and the Unseen Reality):** The boy lowers his arm and gives up trying to wake the prisoners, returning to his quiet upward gaze. Only now—when no one is pointing or looking—does the custodian step into view.



No single painting can show **the defeat of the truth-teller across time**. A single frame shows either a boy pointing (hope that the adults might turn around) or a boy standing still (ambiguity over whether he even noticed the door). Together, the sequence shows that the boy *did* see the truth, *tried* to share it, was met with total apathy, and learned to lower his hand—just as the real, laboring human being behind the curtain appears and goes completely unseen.

# A title is the one text that travels with a painting, and it can carry what a quick glance misses. Give a title to the series and to each of the four versions. Try not to name Plato, the cave or the shadows directly. For each title, say what it makes the viewer notice and whether it is ironic.

**Series Title: *The Permanent Collection***

* **What it makes the viewer notice:** It draws the eye to the institutional machinery—the parquet floors, the brass stanchions, the informational plaques, and the gallery visitors themselves, who stand so rigidly they resemble permanent fixtures of the museum. It highlights that the painted grassland and the concrete closet behind it are equally preserved parts of the same artificial enclosure.


* **Irony:** Deeply ironic. A "permanent collection" implies timeless cultural treasures, yet it encompasses both a synthetic wilderness and a janitor's mop bucket. More bitingly, it suggests that the human habit of staring at comforting illusions while ignoring the open door beside us is the most permanent exhibit of all.



**1. Version 1: *Field Trip***

* **What it makes the viewer notice:** It calls attention to the relationship between the boy and the older man holding his hand, specifically the boy's upturned face waiting for guidance, juxtaposed with the painted "field" stretching before them.


* **Irony:** Quietly ironic. The phrase plays on three meanings at once: an educational excursion to a museum, a journey into an open meadow, and a literal "trip" (stumble) in the visual field where the hillside cracks open to reveal a fire extinguisher. Nobody is actually in a field, and the adults leading the educational trip are the ones failing to observe their surroundings.



**2. Version 3 (Second in sequence): *A Breach in the Foothills***

* **What it makes the viewer notice:** It directs the viewer's focus along the diagonal line of the boy's outstretched right arm straight to the sharp vertical cut of the doorway interrupting the painted slope. It makes the viewer register the clash of textures: golden dry brush meeting grey poured concrete.


* **Irony:** Sincere from the boy's perspective, ironic in its framing. "Foothills" adopts the grand romantic vocabulary of landscape painting for a flat wall, while "breach" treats a forgotten broom-closet door like a dramatic geological event—an urgent discovery that only the smallest person in the room registers.



**3. Version 2 (Third in sequence): *Second Opinion***

* **What it makes the viewer notice:** It makes the viewer look closely at the boy's altered gesture—how he is no longer pointing at the door, but raising his left hand toward the painted horizon and the man's face—as well as the eerie anatomical doubling of his left arm (one holding the man's hand, one raised to point).


* **Irony:** Darkly ironic. Usually, seeking a "second opinion" is a rational step toward verifying the truth. Here, the boy seeks validation from the authority figure and begins to second-guess his own eyes, fracturing himself (literally, in the geometry of his arms) to align with the silent consensus of the room.



**4. Version 4 (Fourth in sequence): *Upkeep***

* **What it makes the viewer notice:** It pulls focus immediately to the custodian inside the utility room and the tools of her trade (the mop, the bucket, the electrical panel), while also making the viewer notice that the boy's arm has dropped back down to his side.


* **Irony:** Piercingly ironic. The word bridges the physical maintenance of the museum and the psychological maintenance of the illusion. The pristine, untouched "wilderness" requires constant janitorial labor to exist, and the social order of the gallery requires the boy to drop his hand and "keep up" the pretense that the custodian isn't there.
