As a comic, it has charm; as a **narrative joke**, I think it weakens the original mechanism quite substantially.

The strongest part of the original joke is a very specific misdirection. The listener naturally interprets the situation as:

**talking cat → circus director → obviously he wants the miraculous talking cat as an act.**

Then the last line suddenly reveals that the cat has been interpreting the conversation through a completely different frame:

**“Why would a circus need a programmer?”**

That is the joke. Two perfectly coherent models of the same conversation collide only in the final sentence.

In this version, unfortunately, the comic reveals the second model too early. The panel saying **“Freelance programmer, Cornish Rex”**, followed by the laptop and *“better than this programming mess”*, tells us explicitly that she is a programmer before the circus proposal has had time to create the false assumption. So instead of:

> “Of course the circus wants a talking cat … wait, WHAT?”

the reader is already thinking:

> “Ah, she is a programmer-cat; presumably there will be some misunderstanding about the circus.”

That turns the punchline into confirmation rather than revelation.

There is also a second problem: there are **too many competing jokes**. “Cat shapeshifter scam,” the crossed-out heels, *Mon Dieu / chat*, daifuku vs croissant, *I’ll shred your face*, bakeneko, the earrings, the deadline, etc. Individually they can be amusing, but they create several possible comic centers. The elegant stupidity of the original depends on almost everything except the central misunderstanding being completely ordinary.

The visual characterization itself is quite good, though. The cat looking like a slightly intimidating, self-possessed professional while the waiter is losing his mind is exactly the right asymmetry. And the café setting works well because she behaves as though nothing remarkable is happening.

I would restructure it roughly like this:

1. **Cat enters café and orders coffee/cake.** She can be dressed like a normal customer, but no laptop, programmer references, or conspicuous work accessories.
2. **Waiter realizes she talks.** “You’re a cat… a talking cat?!”
3. **She impatiently confirms it.** This establishes that *she* sees nothing interesting about being a talking cat.
4. **Waiter offers the circus job.** Make his excitement enormous: uncle owns circus, any salary, etc.
5. **Cat pauses thoughtfully.** This is important: the reader assumes she is considering becoming a circus performer.
6. **Punchline:** “A circus? … Why would a circus need a programmer?”

And **only in that last panel** would I reveal the laptop/programmer clue—perhaps her business card lying beside the cup, or a laptop bag marked with some unobtrusive programming sticker. Then the image becomes a retrospective visual joke: after reading the punchline, you look back and realize that she was simply a programmer going for coffee all along.

That would preserve what I think is the unusually good feature of this joke: **the cat's ability to speak is not the punchline. The punchline is that the cat doesn't understand why anyone else considers it professionally relevant.**

In fact, that makes it slightly more sophisticated than the usual “talking animal” joke. The waiter commits a perfectly reasonable category error: *extraordinary talking cat → entertainer*. The cat commits the opposite category error: *I am a software developer → obviously any job offer concerns software development*. Neither notices the other's ontology until the end.

So I'd keep the drawing style and the cat's personality, but ruthlessly remove about 70% of the secondary gags and **hide every programmer clue until the final panel**. That would make the narrative mechanism much sharper.
