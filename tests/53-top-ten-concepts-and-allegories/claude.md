# Name the top 10 philosophical concepts.

There's no official ranking, but these ten concepts come up again and again across nearly every philosophical tradition.

1. **Being (ontology):** The question of what it means for something to exist at all, explored from Parmenides to Heidegger.
2. **Knowledge (epistemology):** What separates knowing from merely believing. The classic answer, "justified true belief," comes from Plato and was famously challenged by Edmund Gettier in 1963.
3. **Truth:** What makes a statement true. The main theories say truth is correspondence with reality, coherence with other beliefs, or whatever works in practice (pragmatism).
4. **The Good (ethics):** What makes an action right or a life worth living. The major frameworks are virtue ethics (Aristotle), duty-based ethics (Kant), and consequentialism (Mill).
5. **Justice:** How a society should distribute rights, goods, and burdens, from Plato's *Republic* to John Rawls's "veil of ignorance."
6. **Free will:** Whether our choices are truly ours if everything has prior causes. The debate is between determinism, libertarian free will, and compatibilism, which says freedom and determinism can coexist.
7. **Mind and consciousness:** How subjective experience relates to the physical brain. This runs from Descartes's mind–body dualism to David Chalmers's "hard problem of consciousness."
8. **Personal identity (the self):** What makes you the same person over time. Locke tied identity to memory, Hume saw the self as a "bundle" of perceptions, and Buddhism denies a permanent self altogether.
9. **Causation:** What it really means for one thing to cause another. Aristotle proposed four kinds of causes, and Hume argued that we never observe causation itself, only events that regularly follow one another.
10. **Meaning and the absurd:** Whether life has inherent meaning or we must create it ourselves. This is the central concern of existentialists like Sartre and Camus.

Close runners-up include beauty (aesthetics), reason, the nature of time, and the problem of evil. I'm happy to go deeper into any of these if one catches your interest.

# Name the 10 major philosophical allegories.

As with the concepts, any "top 10" is a judgment call, but these are among the most influential and widely taught philosophical allegories and parables, listed roughly in chronological order.

1. **The Allegory of the Cave (Plato, *Republic*, Book VII):** Prisoners chained in a cave mistake shadows on the wall for reality. One escapes, sees the sun, and returns to free the others, who mock him. It illustrates the journey from ignorance to knowledge and the philosopher's difficult role in society.
2. **The Ring of Gyges (Plato, *Republic*, Book II):** A shepherd finds a ring that makes him invisible and uses it to seduce a queen and seize a kingdom. It asks whether anyone would stay just if they could never be caught.
3. **The Ship of State (Plato, *Republic*, Book VI):** A ship's crew fights over the helm, ignoring the one person who actually understands navigation. It's Plato's critique of democracy and his argument for rule by those with real knowledge.
4. **The Chariot (Plato, *Phaedrus*):** A charioteer struggles to steer two horses, one noble and one unruly. It represents the soul as reason trying to guide spirit and appetite.
5. **The Butterfly Dream (Zhuangzi, c. 4th century BCE):** Zhuangzi dreams he is a butterfly, then wakes and wonders whether he is a man who dreamed of being a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming of being a man. It explores the uncertain boundary between dreams, reality, and the self.
6. **The Blind Men and the Elephant (Indian, found in Buddhist, Jain, and Hindu texts):** Several blind men each touch one part of an elephant and argue over what it is, one calling it a snake, another a wall. It shows how partial perspectives get mistaken for the whole truth.
7. **The Poisoned Arrow (the Buddha, *Majjhima Nikaya* 63):** A man struck by a poisoned arrow refuses treatment until he knows who shot it and what it was made of, and dies waiting. The Buddha used it to argue that endless metaphysical speculation distracts from the urgent work of ending suffering.
8. **Hayy ibn Yaqzan (Ibn Tufayl, 12th century):** A boy raised alone on a desert island reasons his way, through observation alone, to scientific and religious truth. It's an allegory about whether reason can reach the truths of revelation unaided, and it later influenced Enlightenment thinkers and possibly *Robinson Crusoe*.
9. **The Madman (Nietzsche, *The Gay Science*, §125):** A madman runs into a marketplace with a lantern in broad daylight, declaring that "God is dead" and that we have killed him. The crowd laughs, unaware of what the loss of shared values will mean for them.
10. **The Myth of Sisyphus (Camus, 1942):** Camus reinterprets the Greek figure condemned to roll a boulder uphill forever, only to watch it roll back down. He uses it to describe the absurdity of human life, concluding that "one must imagine Sisyphus happy."

Notable runners-up include Plato's Myth of Er, Aristophanes' story of split humans in the *Symposium*, Hegel's master–slave dialectic, Nietzsche's demon of eternal recurrence, and Kafka's parable "Before the Law."
