# Name the top 10 philosophical concepts.

1. **Platonic Idealism and the Allegory of the Cave** *(Metaphysics & Epistemology)*
Plato argued that the physical world perceived through the senses is merely a shadowy imitation of true reality—the realm of eternal, unchanging "Forms" or ideas. In his Cave allegory, chained prisoners mistake shadows projected on a wall for absolute truth, illustrating the journey of philosophical enlightenment: moving past sensory illusions to perceive fundamental truths.
2. **Cartesian Skepticism and the *Cogito*** *(Epistemology)*
René Descartes applied radical doubt to discover an indubitable foundation for knowledge, systematically questioning everything that could be faked by a deceptive demon or a dream. He concluded that the very act of doubting requires a thinking agent, yielding the foundational axiom ***Cogito, ergo sum*** ("I think, therefore I am").
3. **Aristotelian Virtue Ethics and *Eudaimonia*** *(Ethics)*
Rather than judging morality by rules or consequences, Aristotle focused on character. He posited that the ultimate aim of human existence is *eudaimonia* (human flourishing or a life well-lived), achieved by cultivating virtues as a "golden mean" between extremes—such as courage residing between cowardice and recklessness.
4. **The Categorical Imperative** *(Deontological Ethics)*
Immanuel Kant proposed that moral duties are absolute and universal, regardless of desires or outcomes. His principle dictates that one should act only according to maxims that could become universal laws, and that moral agents must always treat humanity as an end in itself, never merely as a means to an end.
5. **Utilitarianism and the Greatest Happiness Principle** *(Consequentialist Ethics)*
Developed by Jeremy Bentham and refined by John Stuart Mill, utilitarianism defines moral rightness strictly by consequences: actions are good in proportion to how much aggregate happiness or pleasure they produce, and bad in proportion to how much pain or suffering they cause for all affected beings.
6. **The Social Contract** *(Political Philosophy)*
Theorized in varying forms by Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, and Jean-Jacques Rousseau, this concept argues that legitimate political authority arises from an implicit agreement among individuals. To escape the perilous chaos or inconveniences of the "state of nature," people surrender certain freedoms to a governing authority in exchange for security, rights, and societal order.
7. **Determinism vs. Free Will** *(Metaphysics & Philosophy of Mind)*
This central dilemma asks whether human choices are genuinely autonomous or strictly governed by antecedent causal chains, physical laws, or divine predetermination. It underpins how societies assign moral responsibility, guilt, and punishment.
8. **"Existence Precedes Essence"** *(Existentialism)*
Articulated by Jean-Paul Sartre, this doctrine asserts that human beings are not born with a predefined purpose, nature, or destiny. Instead, individuals first exist, encounter the world, and define their own identity, meaning, and values entirely through their choices and actions.
9. **The Problem of Evil** *(Philosophy of Religion & Metaphysics)*
First formalized by Epicurus, this dilemma questions how profound suffering and evil can exist in a world created by a deity defined as omnipotent (all-powerful), omniscient (all-knowing), and omnibenevolent (all-good). It remains one of the central challenges to theological metaphysics.
10. **Occam's Razor (The Principle of Parsimony)** *(Epistemology & Philosophy of Science)*
Attributed to the medieval philosopher William of Ockham, this heuristic states that *plurality should not be posited without necessity*. When competing hypotheses explain observations equally well, the one that makes the fewest assumptions is the most rational starting point.

# Name the 10 major philosophical allegories.

1. **The Allegory of the Cave** *(Plato, Republic)*
Prisoners chained underground since childhood see only the shadows of cardboard figures cast by a fire behind them. One prisoner escapes, ascends past the blinding light, and discovers the physical world and the sun itself. Returning to free the others, he is mocked and threatened with death. The story illustrates Plato's Theory of Forms: sensory perception offers only dim, distorted shadows of reality, while rational philosophy leads the soul to objective truth and the Form of the Good.

2. **The Chariot Allegory** *(Plato, Phaedrus)*
The human soul is depicted as a chariot drawn by two winged horses and directed by a charioteer. The charioteer represents **Reason** (*logos*). The white horse is upright, noble, and guided by modest ambition and duty (**Spiritedness** or *thumos*). The dark horse is rebellious, feral, and driven by base carnal impulse (**Appetite** or *epithumia*). The allegory details the internal psychological discipline required for reason to master conflicting desires and elevate the soul toward divine truth.
3. **The Ring of Gyges** *(Plato, Republic)*
A shepherd enters a chasm opened by an earthquake and finds a bronze horse containing a corpse wearing a golden ring. Discovering that turning the ring's collet makes him invisible, he promptly seduces the queen, murders the monarch, and takes the throne. Glaucon uses the tale to argue that people practice justice only out of fear of punishment and reputation; given absolute impunity, a moral man and an immoral man would act identically.
4. **The Ship of State** *(Plato, Republic)*
A ship's owner is large and strong, but partially blind, hard of hearing, and ignorant of navigation. The sailors brawl to control the helm, flattering the master and using force, despite never having studied the art of seafaring. Meanwhile, the true navigator—who tracks the winds, seasons, and stars—is ridiculed as a useless stargazer. Plato uses this to critique populist democracy, where charismatic demagogues gain power over skilled, philosophically trained leaders.
5. **Zhuangzi's Butterfly Dream** *(Zhuangzi)*
The Daoist sage dreams he is a butterfly fluttering freely without any recollection of being Zhuangzi. Upon waking, he finds himself unmistakably human, but is left uncertain whether he is Zhuangzi who dreamed he was a butterfly, or a butterfly now dreaming he is Zhuangzi. The allegory illustrates *wuhua* (the transformation of things), questioning rigid self-identity and the distinction between waking reality and illusion.
6. **The Blind Men and the Elephant** *(Ancient Indian traditions: Jainism, Buddhism, Hinduism)*
Several blind men encounter an elephant for the first time. Touching its tusk, one claims it is a spear; touching the trunk, another declares it a snake; feeling the leg, a third insists it is a tree trunk. They fall into furious disagreement. Central to the Jain doctrine of *Anekāntavāda* (non-one-sidedness), the story demonstrates epistemic humility: individual human perceptions grasp only limited, relative facets of absolute truth.
7. **Buridan’s Ass** *(Attributed to Jean Buridan)*
A hypothetical donkey stands exactly equidistant between two identical piles of hay and two identical troughs of water, experiencing equal levels of hunger and thirst. Because pure, deterministic rationality finds no reason to favor one side over the other, the animal is paralyzed by indecision and starves. The parable interrogates determinism, volition, and whether free will requires a capacity to choose without rational asymmetry.
8. **Schopenhauer's Porcupine Dilemma** *(Arthur Schopenhauer, Parerga and Paralipomena)*
On a freezing winter day, a herd of porcupines huddle close for mutual warmth. When their quills prick each other, the pain forces them apart, but the freezing cold drives them back together. After repeated cycles of freezing and stabbing, they settle on a moderate distance where they can tolerate each other's heat without injury. It captures the paradox of human relationships: the constant tension between social need and inevitable emotional friction.
9. **The Parable of the Madman** *(Friedrich Nietzsche, The Gay Science)*
A madman enters a crowded marketplace in broad daylight holding a lit lantern, shouting, "I seek God!" When met with amusement from secular onlookers, he declares: "God is dead. God remains dead. And we have killed him." He smashes his lantern on the ground, realizing he has arrived too early. The allegory warns of the existential and moral vacuum left by the secular collapse of Christian metaphysics, predicting an era of profound nihilism.
10. **The Myth of Sisyphus** *(Albert Camus)*
Condemned by the gods to push a massive boulder up a mountain only to watch it roll back to the bottom for eternity, Sisyphus embodies the Absurd—the clash between the human desire for inherent meaning and a silent, indifferent universe. Camus reclaims the Greek myth, arguing that the moment Sisyphus descends the mountain to reclaim his rock, conscious awareness transforms his punishment into defiant revolt: *"One must imagine Sisyphus happy."*
