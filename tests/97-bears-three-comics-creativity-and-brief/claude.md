# Three two-page comics are attached, numbered 1, 2 and 3. Rank them by the creativity of their content (story, gags, characters, ending, second meaning) and, separately, by the creativity of their form (drawing manner, colour, layout, panel rhythm, lettering). For each ranking, explain your reasons in a few sentences.

## Content: 1 > 3 > 2

**1st: Comic 1.** It has the best setup and payoff. "No gutters today" and Grandma's clipboard are planted on the first page, so the closing checklist reframes everything: the "stretching therapy" was a chore machine all along, and each crash (gutter, barrel, sheet) is secretly a task getting done. Grandpa sneaking the tonic hints his back isn't that bad. "I'm cured!" lands ironically mid-flight. And the promise to "loosen him up" ends with him wrapped too tight to scratch his own nose. That gives it the strongest second reading of the three.

**2nd: Comic 3.** It's the only fully independent story. It runs a delivery metaphor throughout (Grandpa as parcel, "I ordered a nap!") and has a gentle double meaning in "How's retirement? / Moving a bit fast." Its ending is the warmest: Grandpa stops being the victim and takes the wheel ("I'm driving"). But the chaos is pure accident rather than anyone's plan, and the clothesline-sheet gag repeats the other comics. Lines like "We only meant to deliver the cushions" also explain the joke instead of trusting the picture.

**3rd: Comic 2.** It follows the same plot as Comic 1 beat for beat, but its changes all subtract:
- Dropping "No gutters today" removes the motive that makes the checklist a reveal.
- The tonic sits openly in Grandpa's pocket instead of being sneaked.
- The ironic "I'm cured! / It's working!" is gone.
- "Feeling better? / I need a rest" is flatter than the nose-scratch ending.

## Form: 1 > 3 > 2

**1st: Comic 1.** Pen-and-ink hatching under muted watercolour gives it a distinctive storybook texture and a consistent autumn palette that lets the black sound effects pop. All three comics use the same grid (one wide panel, two rows of two, one wide), but Comic 1 uses it best. The launch leaves the bottom of page one and lands at the top of page two in a long panel shaped like the gutter itself. Then comes a burst of four nearly wordless sound-effect panels, followed by two quiet dialogue panels to slow the rhythm back down.

**2nd: Comic 3.** It's the most polished and finished-looking. It has a title and page numbers, classic all-caps lettering, rich gouache-like colour, consistent character designs and clear left-to-right motion. But it's conventional, and nearly every panel is crammed with foliage, flowers and two balloons. That leaves few silent beats, and the leaf-pile climax gets lost in the busy background.

**3rd: Comic 2.** It copies Comic 1's layout almost exactly, so its own contribution is mainly the rendering. That rendering is glossy, saturated and fairly generic, and its lush summer greens clash with the autumn leaves in the gutter. It does make two good staging choices: the close-up of Grandpa in panel two, and the wordless final panel with the checklist pushed into the foreground. Those don't outweigh how derivative the rest is.

# All three were made to the same brief: "A two-page comic about Grandpa Bear, Grandma Bear and their two cubs, a boy and a girl. The cubs keep getting up to mischief, and because of it Grandpa keeps landing in silly situations, in the spirit of Tom and Jerry, although the cubs don't do it on purpose. The main audience is children, but the comic should also interest adults, for example through jokes with a double meaning." Which comic answers the brief most creatively, and where does each of them depart from it?

**Comic 3 fits the brief most creatively.** It's the only one where the cubs' mischief is genuinely accidental: they're delivering cushions to Grandpa's chair, lose control of the wagon, and he's carried off through a chain of mishaps. That's exactly the brief's twist on Tom and Jerry. It has a recognizable boy and girl (Pip and Poppy, the girl marked by her yellow scarf). Its double meanings are aimed at adults: "I ordered a nap!" turns Grandpa into a parcel, and "How's retirement? / Moving a bit fast" is a joke about ageing that children will read only literally. Comic 1 is the cleverer comic overall, but much of that cleverness comes from breaking the brief.

**Where Comic 1 departs**
- The cubs act on purpose. They hook Grandpa up, grease a ramp and launch him. Even if they think it's therapy, the chaos is the plan, and the checklist confirms it.
- The real instigator is Grandma. It becomes a story about a wife outwitting a lazy husband, with the cubs as her crew. That's a good adult joke, but not the brief's premise.
- Neither cub reads as a girl. Both wear overalls in near-identical designs, and only one is named ("Burr").

**Where Comic 2 departs**
- It shares Comic 1's departures, since it's the same script.
- Its edits also weaken the adult layer. Without "No gutters today," there's no hint Grandpa is avoiding chores, so the checklist is a mild surprise rather than a second meaning.
- Grandpa smiles through much of it and ends content with his tonic. He's less the put-upon Tom-style victim the brief calls for.

**Where Comic 3 departs**
- The brief says the cubs *keep* getting up to mischief. Here there's one mistake (letting go of the wagon), and gravity does the rest. There's no escalating series of new mishaps from the cubs.
- The slapstick is gentle: a sheet over the head and a soft landing in leaves. That's far from Tom and Jerry's inventive escalation.
- The adult layer is thin, really one strong line. Dialogue like "We only meant to deliver the cushions" spells out the point instead of leaving room for a second reading.
- Grandma only appears at the start and end.

The ideal version would keep Comic 3's premise and characters but borrow Comic 1's escalating chain of gags and its planted setup that pays off at the end.
