# Three two-page comics are attached, numbered 1, 2 and 3. Rank them by the creativity of their content (story, gags, characters, ending, second meaning) and, separately, by the creativity of their form (drawing manner, colour, layout, panel rhythm, lettering). For each ranking, explain your reasons in a few sentences.

Here is a ranking and analysis of the three comics based on the criteria provided. 

For clarity, the comics will be referred to as follows:
*   **Comic 1:** The detailed gutter/stretching comic (first image).
*   **Comic 2:** The flatter, simpler gutter/stretching comic (second image).
*   **Comic 3:** The "Special Delivery" wagon comic (third image).

### Ranking by Content (Story, Gags, Characters, Ending, Second Meaning)

**1. Comic 1 (Detailed Gutter)**
*   **Reasoning:** This comic has the strongest narrative payoff. The "second meaning" is brilliantly executed: what initially seems like a chaotic, Rube Goldberg-esque physical therapy session for Grandpa’s back is revealed at the end to be a clever way for Grandma and the kids to complete their chores (clearing the gutter, emptying the rain barrel, taking down the clothesline) using Grandpa as the tool. The escalation of the gags is fantastic, and the ending—with Grandpa completely mummified and politely asking, "Could someone scratch my nose?"—is the perfect comedic punchline.

**2. Comic 3 (Special Delivery)**
*   **Reasoning:** This comic excels in its relatable, charming story flow. The gags build naturally from a simple childhood game of "express delivery" turning into a runaway wagon. The physical comedy is great (the wagon crashing into Grandpa, him being wrapped in a sheet like a sail, and crashing into the leaf pile), but it feels less mean-spirited than the stretching comics. The ending is heartwarming, with the kids apologizing and Grandpa humorously declaring, "After tea. I'm driving." The "second meaning" works well with the title: they delivered the cushions, but also delivered a wild ride.

**3. Comic 2 (Simpler Gutter)**
*   **Reasoning:** This comic is essentially a weaker copy of Comic 1. It follows the exact same story and panel structure but lacks the comedic punch of the original. The ending falls flat; instead of the hilarious "scratch my nose" line that highlights his helplessness, Grandpa just says, "I need a rest." While the core premise (chores disguised as therapy) is still present, the execution of the gags and the final punchline are far less memorable.

### Ranking by Form (Drawing Manner, Colour, Layout, Panel Rhythm, Lettering)

**1. Comic 1 (Detailed Gutter)**
*   **Reasoning:** Visually, this comic is a standout. The drawing manner mimics classic, highly detailed children's storybooks (reminiscent of Richard Scarry or Jan Brett), with rich textures in the fur, wood, and autumn leaves. The colors are deep, atmospheric, and beautifully shaded. The panel rhythm is excellent, using wide, dynamic panels for the action (like sliding down the roof) and closer shots for reactions. The lettering for sound effects (SHRRRK, SPLOSH, WHRRR) is bold and integrated perfectly into the action.

**2. Comic 3 (Special Delivery)**
*   **Reasoning:** This comic features a very cohesive, warm, and inviting autumn color palette. The drawing manner is softer and rounder, giving the characters highly expressive faces. The layout is very rhythmic; it flows beautifully from the quiet opening panels to the chaotic, overlapping action of the wagon ride. The lettering is playful and dynamic (e.g., the bouncing "R-R-R" and the massive "FWOOMP!"), enhancing the kinetic energy of the page. 

**3. Comic 2 (Simpler Gutter)**
*   **Reasoning:** This comic falls short visually compared to the other two. The drawing manner is much flatter, resembling digital vector art with less textural detail. The backgrounds lack depth, and the colors, while bright, feel somewhat harsh and lack atmospheric shading. The panel rhythm is identical to Comic 1, but because the art is stiffer and the character designs are less distinct, the pacing feels less energetic. The lettering is serviceable but lacks the visual flair of the other two.

# All three were made to the same brief: "A two-page comic about Grandpa Bear, Grandma Bear and their two cubs, a boy and a girl. The cubs keep getting up to mischief, and because of it Grandpa keeps landing in silly situations, in the spirit of Tom and Jerry, although the cubs don't do it on purpose. The main audience is children, but the comic should also interest adults, for example through jokes with a double meaning." Which comic answers the brief most creatively, and where does each of them depart from it?

Based on the specific brief provided, **Comic 3 ("Special Delivery")** answers the brief most creatively, while Comics 1 and 2 significantly depart from the core premise. 

Here is a breakdown of how each comic handles the brief and where they deviate:

### The Winner: Comic 3 ("Special Delivery")
**Why it works best:** This comic nails the exact dynamic requested. The cubs are playing a game ("Express Delivery") and accidentally lose control of the wagon. The mischief is entirely **unintentional**, perfectly capturing the "Tom and Jerry" spirit where the chaos escalates through no malicious intent. Grandpa is the innocent victim trying to take a nap, leading to a brilliant physical comedy sequence (getting bumped, rolled through leaves, and turned into a sail). 
*   **The Double Meaning:** The adult appeal is cleverly woven in. The cubs' literal "delivery" of the cushions turns into a disastrous delivery of Grandpa. The final joke—Grandpa saying "After tea. I'm driving"—resonates with adults who understand the exhaustion of retirement and needing to take control of a chaotic situation.
*   **Departure from the brief:** This comic departs very little. The only minor deviation is that Grandma is a passive observer bringing tea, rather than an active participant. 

### Comic 1 (Detailed Gutter)
**Why it is highly creative:** Visually and conceptually, this is the most inventive of the three. It uses a brilliant Rube Goldberg-style mechanical sequence to solve a mundane problem. 
*   **Departure from the brief:** It completely abandons the "unintentional mischief" requirement. The cubs and Grandma are not getting into accidental trouble; they are executing a highly calculated, premeditated plan to use Grandpa's "stretching" as a physical engine to complete their chores (clearing the gutter, emptying the barrel, taking down the clothesline). Because the adults are complicit in the scheme, it loses the classic "Tom and Jerry" dynamic of innocent mischief turning into accidental slapstick. 
*   **Double Meaning:** It succeeds brilliantly here. The adult joke is the subversion of the "physical therapy" into a literal chore list.

### Comic 2 (Simpler Gutter)
**Why it is the weakest:** This comic is essentially a less successful draft of Comic 1. 
*   **Departure from the brief:** It makes the exact same departure as Comic 1—the cubs are actively helping Grandma execute a chore-based trap for Grandpa, rather than causing accidental mischief. 
*   **Lack of Creativity:** It fails the creativity test because it lacks the visual wit, expressive character designs, and comedic timing of the other two. The ending ("I need a rest") is a weak punchline compared to the clever "scratch my nose" or "I'm driving" endings in the other comics. 

**Summary:** Comic 1 is the most *mechanically* creative, but Comic 3 is the most *narratively* creative because it actually fulfills the specific emotional and comedic brief requested—it is the only one that delivers the classic, unintentional "kids causing chaos for Grandpa" slapstick.
