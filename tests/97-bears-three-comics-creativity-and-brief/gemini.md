# Three two-page comics are attached, numbered 1, 2 and 3. Rank them by the creativity of their content (story, gags, characters, ending, second meaning) and, separately, by the creativity of their form (drawing manner, colour, layout, panel rhythm, lettering). For each ranking, explain your reasons in a few sentences.

### Ranking by Creativity of Content

**1st: Comic 1 — 2nd: Comic 2 — 3rd: Comic 3**

* **Comic 1** takes first place for its clever dual-layered plot, in which an absurd Rube Goldberg "back-stretching" contraption secretly completes every item on Grandma’s chore list. It sharpens the premise with ironic foreshadowing (*"No gutters today"*), the sneaky *Root Tonic* detail, mid-air dialogue (*"I'm cured!"*), and a great visual callback in the final panel when a stray leaf lands on Grandpa's nose.
* **Comic 2** ranks second because it shares the exact same ingenious premise and hidden-chore twist ending, even though its comedic execution is noticeably softer. It drops the opening gutter joke, replaces the mid-air exclamation with a generic sound effect, and trades the punchy nose-leaf gag for a flatter ending.
* **Comic 3** places third; while it features charming character touches (naming the cubs Pip and Poppy), witty banter (*"How's retirement?" / "Moving a bit fast."*), and a heartwarming ending, its runaway-wagon accident is a much more familiar slapstick trope without the layered second meaning of the first two comics.

### Ranking by Creativity of Form

**1st: Comic 3 — 2nd: Comic 1 — 3rd: Comic 2**

* **Comic 3** leads in formal craft with lush, painterly textures, a rich autumn palette of golds and reds, and polished comic-book presentation including a custom title header and page numbers. Its symmetrical 1–2–2–1 panel layout across both pages establishes a crisp visual rhythm that alternates smoothly between chaotic action and wide, comedic beats.
* **Comic 1** comes in second with a warm, nostalgic storybook drawing style and muted autumnal tones that give the physical comedy an inviting, classic feel. Its panel sizing effectively emphasizes key moments—such as the wide horizontal gutter slide—and its expressive hand-lettered sound effects (*SHRRRK!*, *TWANG!*, *FWUMP!*) integrate seamlessly into the action.
* **Comic 2** ranks third due to its flatter, overly saturated Saturday-morning-cartoon colors and stiffer character anatomy and facial expressions. Its visual rhythm also stumbles slightly compared to Comic 1, most noticeably by omitting a sound-effect burst during the clothesline crash, which dampens the impact of the gag.

# All three were made to the same brief: "A two-page comic about Grandpa Bear, Grandma Bear and their two cubs, a boy and a girl. The cubs keep getting up to mischief, and because of it Grandpa keeps landing in silly situations, in the spirit of Tom and Jerry, although the cubs don't do it on purpose. The main audience is children, but the comic should also interest adults, for example through jokes with a double meaning." Which comic answers the brief most creatively, and where does each of them depart from it?

**Comic 1** answers the brief most creatively. While all three nail the *Tom & Jerry* physical slapstick for children, Comic 1 elevates the prompt with the smartest dual-audience payoff: it disguises Grandma’s chore list inside a slapstick accident and slips in a cheeky *"Root Tonic"* gag for adults. **Comic 3** is a close runner-up because it follows the literal character and mischief setup most faithfully, even if its humor is gentler.

### Where Each Comic Departs from the Brief

* **Comic 1**


* **Helpful intent instead of mischief:** Rather than getting into playful mischief, the cubs are earnestly trying to cure Grandpa’s back (*"We'll loosen him up!"*), and the accident is actually an orchestrated chore-clearing scheme.


* **One chain reaction, not recurring mishaps:** Grandpa suffers a single, continuous Rube Goldberg launch rather than repeatedly landing in separate silly situations.


* **Unclear cub genders:** Both cubs wear matching overalls with no clear visual distinction between a boy and a girl.




* **Comic 2**


* **Same plot departures as Comic 1:** It also replaces cub mischief with well-intentioned "physical therapy" that turns out to be Grandma’s chore plan, and it relies on one continuous gag rather than recurring accidents.


* **Diluted adult humor:** While it keeps the *Root Tonic* bottle and checklist twist, it cuts the ironic *"No gutters today"* setup and ends on a flat *"I need a rest"* line rather than a sharp punchline.


* **Unclear cub genders:** Like Comic 1, the two cubs (one in a yellow shirt and green shorts, one in blue overalls) are not clearly differentiated as a boy and a girl.




* **Comic 3**


* **Lighter on adult double meanings:** It hits the character brief best—clearly featuring a boy cub and a girl cub (Poppy) whose innocent game accidentally sweeps Grandpa into a runaway wagon—but its adult appeal relies mostly on a single pun (*"How's retirement?" / "Moving a bit fast."*) rather than a clever hidden layer.


* **Single mishap:** Like the others, it builds a two-page sequence around one escalating runaway-wagon accident instead of multiple distinct *Tom & Jerry*-style predicaments.
