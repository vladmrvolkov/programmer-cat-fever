# What do you think is depicted in the attached image?

It's a comic built on an old joke. In the classic version, a horse (or a talking dog) walks into a bar, the bartender says the circus is in town and could use him, and the animal replies, "What would a circus need a plumber for?" Here it's retold as a talking black cat in a café who turns out to be a programmer.

**The story.** It's a rainy night in a nearly empty café, with chairs already upturned on the tables at closing time. A waiter takes an order (coffee with cream, a pastry) and is stunned that the customer is a talking cat. The cat is unimpressed and just wants its coffee. The waiter offers to get it a job at his uncle's circus. The cat closes its eyes dreamily and lists acrobats, jugglers, lollipops and cotton candy. A tense close-up of its yellow eye follows with "Sounds tempting." Then comes the deflation: "But what would they need a programmer for?"

**Why it's funny.** The waiter and the cat see completely different things. He sees a miracle he can turn into a spectacle. The cat doesn't consider being a talking cat remarkable at all. In its own mind it's simply a professional, and its identity is its work. It can't imagine being valued for anything other than its job.

**Details I liked:**
- **The yellow accents.** The only color on the page is the cat's eyes and the glowing laptop on the far table, beside a coffee cup. That's the cat's workstation, visible from the very first panel, so the punchline is set up before anyone says a word.
- **The reflection.** In the extreme close-up, a small yellow rectangle shows in the cat's pupil. It's the laptop screen. Even while daydreaming about cotton candy, the cat is looking at its work.
- **The looming shadow.** In the circus panel, the waiter's shadow on the wall is huge and hunched, which hints that his offer isn't entirely innocent. He's looking at a meal ticket, not a colleague.
- **The pacing.** The ominous eye close-up builds suspense, as if the cat is plotting something, and then the last panel calmly pulls the rug out.

There's also a gentle in-joke about programmers: a nocturnal creature working alone in a café late at night, fueled by coffee, mildly annoyed by interruptions, and indifferent to the extraordinary. Programmers and cats have a lot in common.

# You recognised an older joke behind it. What do these characters, this setting and this drawing style change in its meaning? What meanings does the page support — one or several? For the details that matter, say which meaning each one supports, and point out any details that pull in different directions.

The old joke works on obliviousness. A horse or dog walks into a bar, hears it could join the circus, and asks why a circus would need a plumber or bricklayer. Everyone else sees a miracle, and the animal sees an ordinary working guy. It's a modest, working-class joke about how unremarkable we are in our own heads. This page keeps that skeleton but changes nearly everything around it.

## What the new elements change

**The cat acknowledges what it is.** The waiter says "talking cat, incredible," and the cat answers "Yes, I'm a cat." So the punchline can't be pure obliviousness anymore. The cat knows others find it remarkable; it just doesn't count that as worth anything. That pushes the joke toward one of two things. Either work is the only thing the cat values, or the cat is deliberately deflecting.

**A programmer instead of a plumber.** A plumber is humble. A programmer is a well-paid knowledge worker who gets recruited constantly. The waiter's "whatever terms you like" sounds like a recruiter's pitch. The cat hears it the way someone headhunted every week would: any offer must be for its skills. The joke shifts from modesty to professional identity and tunnel vision.

**A cat instead of a horse or dog.** A dog would be eager to please. Cats are nocturnal, aloof and impossible to train for an act, and they're the internet's unofficial programmer mascot. A black cat with yellow eyes also brings omen and gothic mystery. Here the cat is the customer and the human serves it, which makes the cat the stronger presence in the scene.

**A café at closing time instead of a pub.** The pub joke is sociable, the kind of thing told among drinkers. This setting is solitary: rain, one lamp, chairs upturned, and a lone customer with a laptop and an empty cup. It's a picture of working alone late, and it adds a melancholy the original never had.

**The drawing style.** The page uses dense, engraving-like cross-hatching in a noir-gothic register, monochrome except for yellow in the cat's eyes and the laptop screen. A vaudeville gag drawn like a dark parable can deepen into commentary, or the gravity can be mock-serious to make the deflation land harder. It probably does both.

## The meanings the page supports

It supports several meanings, layered and not equally weighted:

1. **The original gag.** Self-image versus how others see you. This is the frame, and it's fully intact.
2. **Work has swallowed identity.** The cat can't picture being wanted for anything but code, even with cotton candy on offer. The visual details support this reading most strongly.
3. **Refusing to be a spectacle.** The cat understands the offer perfectly, and the last line is a dry way of saying "I'm a professional, not an exhibit."
4. **Programmers are basically cats.** A lighter in-joke running underneath.

Readings 2 and 3 are the real fork. Is the cat *unable* to see itself any other way, or *unwilling* to be seen any other way?

## Where details pull against each other

**The bliss panel against the cold eye.** Within two panels the cat goes from dreaming child to predator. If "Sounds tempting" is sincere, it's reading 2: a real desire, overruled by work. If it's dry, it's reading 3. The laptop reflection tips it toward sincere but overruled. The predatory framing tips it toward calculating.

**The waiter's face against his shadow.** His face is goofy and earnest, and his shadow is menacing. He could be a starstruck kid or a hustler. The page shows both and won't choose, which keeps reading 3 suggestive rather than settled.

**The mood against the cat.** The setting is lonely and sad, but the cat shows no unhappiness at all. It's calm, a little smug, apparently content. The melancholy lives in the drawing, not in the character. Reading 2 is the reader's diagnosis, and the cat would probably disagree with it.

**Yellow as warmth against yellow as takeover.** In a gray world the screen is the only light, almost a hearth. That can mean work is what the cat loves, or that work has taken over its sight. The same detail carries opposite meanings.

**The style against the gag.** The heavy noir rendering promises a dark story, and the dialogue delivers a groaner. Whether that's ironic contrast or sincere parable is left to the reader.

Overall, the page keeps the old joke's structure but tilts it toward a gently melancholy portrait of someone defined by their work, while leaving room for the cat to be in on the joke. That unresolved question of whether the cat is trapped or choosing may be the real point. From the outside, that's often how people devoted to their work look.

# a small light-yellow rectangle in the cat's pupil - yes - it's laptop's reflection. Does it change your reading of the page or of the cat's last line?

I'd already taken it as the laptop, so it doesn't overturn my reading. Treating it as certain rather than likely does sharpen a few things, though, and shifts one.

**It shows where the cat is looking at the key moment.** In both wide shots the cat faces the waiter, and the laptop sits behind it on another table. For the screen to appear in its pupil during "Sounds tempting," the cat has turned toward its work and away from the man making the offer. Optically, a bright screen could catch the eye from the side. But in comics a reflection in the eye means "this is what the character is looking at." And the artist chose the screen over the pendant lamp, which would have been the more natural highlight.

**That changes how I read the close-up.** I'd partly credited the cold close-up to the "refusing to be a spectacle" reading, as the cat sizing up the hustler. If it were sizing him up, his figure would be in that eye. Instead there's a laptop. The noir framing is a fake-out: the panel looks like scheming and contains a screen. So that reading survives only in a weaker form. The cat isn't outsmarting the waiter; it's immune to him because it barely looks at him. The looming shadow isn't defeated, just irrelevant.

**It changes the tone of "Sounds tempting."** Said while looking at the screen, the line is sincere and absent at once, like the "mm, sounds great" you say while glancing at your monitor. The temptation is real enough to consider, and what it's being weighed against is right there in the eye.

**It makes the punchline land twice.** The turn from circus to work happens first in the picture, inside the pupil, between the two lines. The last line puts into words what the image already showed. An attentive reader gets the punchline a panel early, and everyone else gets it from the dialogue.

**It makes the last line read more like a genuine question.** It's not a confused remark or a polite no. It's a professional scoping a gig: tempting, so what's the job? That's literal-minded in the original joke's way, but with real interest behind it. The cat isn't rejecting the circus; it just can't imagine a role there that isn't its own.

What the reflection doesn't settle is whether that's sad or fine. It shows where the cat's attention goes, not how the cat feels about it. A screen in its eye fits a cat that loves its work just as well as one that can't look away from it.


# The author's title for this page is "Alibi". What does the title make of the story?

An alibi is a plea of having been somewhere else when something happened, and more loosely an excuse. Either way, it's deliberate. You only need an alibi if you know you're under suspicion. So the title pushes the story away from the old joke's obliviousness and toward evasion. The cat isn't confused about what's being offered; it's producing a cover story.

**It names what the reflection shows.** In Latin, *alibi* simply means "elsewhere." At the exact moment of the offer, the cat's eye holds the laptop: it's elsewhere. The screen is the place the cat can always say it was. The title puts into a word what the close-up shows in a picture.

**It changes how I read the last line.** Last time I read it as a genuine question, a professional scoping a gig. The title pulls it back to an excuse dressed as a question. That form is itself evasive: it declines without saying no and hands the problem back to the waiter. "Sounds tempting" becomes the confession, and the programmer line becomes the cover. The order matters, since the temptation is admitted first and the excuse comes second.

**It raises the question of what the "crime" is.** The page supports two answers.
- **Wanting.** The eyes-closed bliss over cotton candy is the guilty wish, and work is the excuse for not acting on it. Read this way, the alibi is told as much to itself as to the waiter. It's self-deception.
- **Being remarkable.** Insisting on being an ordinary professional is the best protection against becoming an exhibit, like a secret identity. The looming shadow is what the cover story protects against. Read this way, it's self-protection.

Both readings bring back the "knowing cat" I'd set aside after the reflection, but in a new form. The cat isn't outwitting the waiter; it's managing itself.

**It can be a true alibi.** The laptop is real, and the cat really is a programmer. A true alibi is still an alibi: the fact can be accurate and still not be the real reason. That's what makes the page recognizable. "I can't, I have work" is usually true, which is exactly why it works so well as an excuse.

**It makes the reader a detective.** Together with the noir drawing, the title turns the page into a scene from a crime story. The reflection becomes the clue, and it corroborates the alibi, since the cat was indeed "at" its work. But the bliss panel supplies the motive. The evidence clears the cat of lying while convicting it of wanting something else.

**It shifts who the joke is on.** It's no longer a gag about an animal that doesn't know what it is. It's a small portrait of hiding behind work, and the target is anyone who has turned down something wonderful for a reason that was perfectly true.

The title settles the naive-versus-knowing question: the cat knows. What it leaves open is whom the cat is fooling, the waiter or itself.

# Here is the author's intent. The cat hears the offer as a threat: for a talking animal, a circus means a cage. She plays dreamy to buy time, finds the glowing laptop at the next table with her eyes, and uses it to make up an excuse: a programmer is someone a circus has no use for. Whether the waiter meant any harm, and whether she really is a programmer, are left open on purpose.

How well does the page carry this intent? Which details work for it, which work against it, and what would you change?

---

The page carries the frame of your intent: "programmer" is an excuse, and the waiter is ambiguous. It doesn't carry the middle, which is the fear and the performance. As drawn, the cat never looks afraid, and the dreamy panel reads as sincere. My own readings are a fair test. I got to "excuse" only through the title, came near "threat" only as "being made an exhibit," and took her bliss at face value every time.

**What works for it**

- **The title.** It does more work than anything else on the page. It's what turns the last line from a gag into a cover story.
- **The reflection.** The idea is right: her finding the laptop is shown rather than said. The yellow screen inside the yellow eye ties the excuse to her gaze.
- **The cold close-up after the dreamy panel.** The switch to alertness reads as thinking, which is what stalling looks like from the inside.
- **The waiter's grin against his shadow.** His face says eager kid, his shadow says threat, and neither wins. This is the best-executed ambiguity on the page.
- **"Whatever terms you like."** It sounds generous on the surface and like an offer you can't refuse underneath.
- **The setting.** Closing time, an empty room, no witnesses and rain outside can all read as a trap.
- **Her composure in panel 1.** It's a useful baseline, provided something later visibly changes.
- **The last panel echoing the first.** The scene snaps back to normal, so the excuse worked.

**What works against it**

- **The threat never visibly registers.** The key turn, hearing the offer as a cage, happens off the page. Between the offer and the bliss, nothing about the cat changes.
- **The dreamy panel reads as real.** In cat body language, closed eyes mean trust and safety, and her tail curls in a loose, relaxed hook. Nothing marks the moment as an act. With her eyes shut she isn't watching the threat either, which is the opposite of buying time.
- **The reflection shows attention, not discovery.** Centered in the pupil, it says "she's looking at it," not "she just found it." I read it as her attention never leaving her work, the opposite of a search. Less attentive readers may take it for a catchlight from the café's big windows.
- **The laptop is established before it's found.** It glows yellow in panel 1, on a table with a cup, while she's the only customer. That reads as setup: her laptop, her job. So the punchline lands as a true identity rather than an improvisation, and "is she a programmer?" tilts toward yes.
- **The famous punchline brings its old meaning with it.** Readers who know the joke will finish it for you: a literal-minded animal with an ordinary self-image.
- **The mood points elsewhere.** Rain, a laptop and an empty café read first as "lonely late worker," which is where I went, before they read as a trap.

**What I'd change**

Most of these fit inside the existing panels.

1. **Panel 2: flatten her ears.** The over-the-shoulder shot already puts her ears between us and the grinning waiter. Turning them back shows the threat registering, visible to the reader and invisible to him. This is a single change with the biggest payoff.
2. **Panel 3: show it's an act.** Keep the smile, but crack one eye open to a yellow slit glancing sideways, the familiar "pretending to sleep" tell. Make the tail tip twitch with a couple of motion lines, so a calm face sits over an agitated tail. Let the window-shadow grid fall across her body rather than just the wall, so there are bars without anyone saying "cage."
3. **Panel 4: make the discovery unmistakable.** Shift the pupil to the corner of the eye so she's visibly looking sideways. Give the reflection a lid and a keyboard edge so it can only be a laptop. If there's room, add a thin panel from her eye-line onto the screen.
4. **Color: switch the screen on when she finds it.** Keep the laptop gray in panels 1–3 and let it turn yellow only in panel 4. Then the yellow marks her attention rather than her ownership, which reopens the question of whether it's hers. In panel 5 it stays lit, because the alibi is now in place.
5. **Panel 5: keep the line and add one tell.** Wrap her tail tightly around her paws, or have her eyes flick to the laptop as she speaks, so her composure looks held a little too carefully. The old joke's shape actually suits her: she's disguising a refusal as a harmless joke. The panels before it just need to show that it's a disguise.

None of these settles your two open questions. The waiter might still be harmless, and she might still be a programmer. The changes just let the reader see what she sees in the offer and what she does about it.
