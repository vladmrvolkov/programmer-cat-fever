## **9. The details that pull in different directions**

What makes the page particularly interesting is that it does not consistently instruct us to sympathise with one character or adopt one perspective.

Its internal tensions include:

- **The cat's dignity versus its intellectual blindness.** Its professional self-identification can be understood as either admirable autonomy or an inability to think outside its established role.
- **The waiter's kindness versus his objectification of the cat.** He offers an opportunity but misunderstands the individual receiving it.
- **The cat's supernatural appearance versus its mundane concerns.** It looks like a creature of mystery but wants coffee, pastry and meaningful employment.
- **The melancholy atmosphere versus the comedic dialogue.** The setting suggests loneliness or contemplation; the conversation is fundamentally playful.
- **The cat's attraction to the circus versus its reluctance to work there.** It apparently finds the circus enjoyable in principle without seeing itself as part of the spectacle.
- **The visual foregrounding of exceptional eyes versus the verbal insistence on ordinary professional identity.** Even the artist invites us to marvel at precisely the exceptional creature that refuses to define itself through its exceptional nature.

That final contradiction may be the most sophisticated feature of the page. The artist, like the waiter, derives considerable expressive value from the cat's unusual appearance, while the cat insists that such an appearance is not the defining fact about itself.

## **Overall interpretation**

I would call this a **polysemic comic with a coherent semantic centre**. It supports multiple meanings, but they are not arbitrary or unrelated.

At its centre is the discrepancy between what an individual is, what that individual thinks it is, and what others perceive it to be.

The older joke primarily exploits a misunderstanding for comedic effect. This illustrated adaptation retains that mechanism while introducing a larger question:

**Who gets to decide which of our qualities defines us—and are we necessarily any better at answering that question ourselves than other people are?**

The visual evidence supports that reading without requiring us to assume that every detail was consciously intended as a symbol. We cannot determine the artist's intentions from the page alone, but we can identify the meanings that its composition, dialogue, characterisation and atmosphere make available to the reader.

Previously, I interpreted the extreme close-up primarily as a moment of contemplation. But the reflection tells us **what the cat is looking at while saying “Sounds tempting.”**

The cat momentarily entertains the possibility of another life, then looks at an object associated with the life it already leads.

The fourth panel is therefore not merely a dramatic pause. **It contains a silent part of the narrative.** The reflection supplies the connection between the apparent attraction of the circus and the professional question that follows.

## **2. Is the cat really misunderstanding the waiter?**

The traditional joke depends on the animal apparently failing to understand why a circus would want a talking animal.

But the reflection, combined with the narrowed eye and the cat's composed expression, makes another interpretation considerably more plausible: **the cat understands perfectly well what the waiter means.**

Under this interpretation, the last line is not an innocent question but a deliberately ironic response.

Its implicit meaning might be:

> I understand why you want me there. But have you considered what I actually do?

The cat does not explicitly refuse the offer, accuse the waiter of treating it as an exhibit, or explain its professional identity. Instead, it asks a question that exposes the inadequacy of the waiter's proposition.

This would make the cat not merely the *subject* of the joke, but potentially its conscious *author*.

The visual evidence for this reading includes the cat's initial impatience, its self-possession throughout the conversation, the prolonged close-up, and the apparent deliberateness of its final question.

Nevertheless, the reflection does not prove that the cat is being ironic. The literal-minded programmer interpretation remains possible.

## **3. The difference between looking at the cat and looking through its eyes**

There is also something significant about the distribution of perspectives.

Throughout most of the page, we look **at** the cat, just as the waiter does. Its appearance, behaviour and extraordinary ability are objects of our attention.

But in the fourth panel, although we are still looking at the cat from outside, the reflection allows us to discover something of what the cat itself sees.

The distinction is important:

| The waiter's attention                      | The cat's attention                              |

# The author's title for this page is "Alibi". What does the title make of the story?

The title **“Alibi”** changes the story considerably. It introduces something that neither the dialogue nor the images explicitly establish on their own: the possibility that the cat's explanation is not simply an expression of its identity, but a means of protecting itself.

The crucial question becomes not merely *Who is the cat?* but *Why does the cat need an alibi — and for what?*

## **1. The punchline becomes a possible excuse**

Without the title, the last line can be understood as an innocent misunderstanding:

“But what would they need a programmer for?”

With the title, we have reason to suspect that the misunderstanding is deliberate. The cat knows perfectly well why a circus would want a talking cat. It simply chooses to answer a different question.

It substitutes the question of its professional qualifications for the question of its extraordinary nature.

Being a programmer provides a perfectly respectable reason not to accept the offer, without having to explain why it does not want to become a circus attraction.

Significantly, the cat never says:

- I don't want to perform.
- I don't want people staring at me.
- I don't want to be valued for my ability to speak.
- I prefer my present life.

It says none of these things. Its profession does the explanatory work instead.

**The cat avoids revealing its motives by presenting a practical objection.** In that sense, programming is its alibi.

## **2. The laptop becomes evidence**

The title makes the reflection in the cat's pupil even more important.

An alibi, in its strict sense, is a claim that someone was elsewhere when an alleged act occurred. In its broader, figurative sense, it is an excuse or justification. But the word retains associations with evidence, interrogation, suspicion and concealment.

Now consider the fourth panel again.

The cat says “Sounds tempting,” while looking at the laptop. We then see it invoke its profession.

The sequence can be understood as the cat finding its answer. The laptop is not merely a reminder of its occupation; it is the material evidence that makes its explanation credible.

The author does not show us the cat programming. We see a laptop and hear the cat declare itself a programmer. We naturally connect the two, but the connection is not independently demonstrated.

The title encourages us to notice this small gap between **evidence and interpretation**.

The cat may genuinely be a programmer. An alibi need not be false to serve as a convenient justification.

## **3. An alibi for refusing — or an alibi for remaining what one is?**

There is a more psychologically complex interpretation.

The cat appears genuinely attracted to the circus. Its closed eyes, lifted head and affectionate enumeration of the circus's pleasures suggest something approaching reverie.

The acrobats, jugglers, lollipops and cotton candy contrast sharply with the deserted, rain-darkened café and the solitary laptop.

Perhaps the cat is not merely pretending to consider the offer. Perhaps it experiences a real attraction to another kind of existence.

Under this reading, the glance at the laptop is a return to its established identity. Its profession becomes a reason not to pursue something attractive but unfamiliar.

That produces two opposing interpretations of the same gesture:

| The profession as freedom                       | The profession as confinement                        |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| The cat protects its independence.              | The cat protects itself from change.                 |
| It refuses an identity imposed by another.      | It retreats into an identity it has already adopted. |
| The laptop represents meaningful work.          | The laptop represents a familiar, safe existence.    |
| The last line is a witty assertion of autonomy. | The last line is an act of self-justification.       |

The title supports both.

The alibi may be intended for the waiter, but it may equally be intended for the cat itself.

## **4. An alibi for being extraordinary**

There is an even deeper possibility: **being a programmer is the cat's alibi for being a talking cat.**

The waiter encounters something that defies the ordinary categories of reality. The cat, however, presents itself through an entirely ordinary social category: it has an occupation, orders coffee, uses a computer and presumably earns a living.

Its professional identity allows it to participate in human society without continually having to account for its extraordinary existence.

The waiter's proposal threatens that arrangement. He wants to make the cat's exceptional nature the centre of its public identity. The cat's answer restores the normality of its existence.

It is as though the cat were saying: there is no mystery here; I am simply a programmer having coffee.

Of course, the mystery remains.

The visual style reinforces precisely this contradiction. The cat looks enigmatic, supernatural and almost archetypal, while its words insist upon the mundane. Its luminous eyes suggest mystery; the reflection inside them suggests work.

The extraordinary has acquired an ordinary explanation without actually ceasing to be extraordinary.

## **5. Why the noir-like drawing style now seems particularly appropriate**

Before knowing the title, I regarded the noir atmosphere principally as a means of giving a simple joke unusual psychological and artistic depth.

Now it acquires a more specific function.

The rain, darkness, empty establishment, dramatic shadows, mysterious black figure and extreme close-up of an eye belong to a visual vocabulary commonly associated with secrecy, concealed motives and uncertain intentions.

The title activates those associations.

There is something almost detective-like about the page: the reader is presented with observable behaviour, statements, visual clues and an ultimately unverifiable explanation.

Yet there is no explicit crime, accusation or investigation.

That absence is itself interesting. The page borrows the atmosphere of a mystery without telling us exactly what the mystery is. The cat's motives become the thing we investigate.

The drawing style and the title together encourage us to distrust the apparent simplicity of the dialogue.

## **6. Is the cat's alibi also a joke about human existence?**

The animal protagonist permits the page to approach a recognisably human problem indirectly.

People frequently use their professions, responsibilities and established identities as explanations for their choices.

Sometimes those explanations express genuine commitments. Sometimes they conceal fears, desires, reluctance or reasons that are difficult to articulate. Often they do both.

The cat's statement is especially revealing because it does not say that it dislikes the circus. It asks whether the circus has a use for its existing professional identity.

It evaluates an alternative existence through the category of what it already is.

This raises a question that goes well beyond the original joke: **is our identity an expression of who we are, or sometimes an alibi for not becoming someone else?**

The page does not answer that question unequivocally.

## **7. The title is a second punchline**

What I find particularly effective is that the title consists of just one word, and that word is never spoken by either character.

There are effectively three stages of interpretation:

**The opening:** A talking cat is an extraordinary creature.

**The punchline:** The extraordinary creature considers itself an ordinary programmer.

**The title:** Perhaps that ordinary identity is itself a form of concealment or self-justification.

The punchline overturns the apparent meaning of the opening. The title then overturns the apparent certainty of the punchline.

It does not tell us whether the cat is naïve, ironic, protective, apprehensive or quietly manipulative. Instead, it makes all those possibilities relevant.

Without the title, I would regard the cat's deliberate irony as one of several plausible interpretations. With the title, I would give considerably greater weight to the possibility that the cat knows exactly what it is doing. Nevertheless, the psychological purpose of its alibi remains open.

My strongest reading now is that the page concerns **the ambiguity of identity as both a means of self-expression and a means of self-protection.**

The cat tells us what it does. It may be telling the absolute truth.

But telling the truth about oneself is not necessarily the same thing as revealing oneself.

She is not sitting in front of it. She is not using it. There is even a cup beside it, while she is still asking the waiter to bring her order.

This is fair visual evidence for the possibility that she simply appropriates an element of her surroundings to construct a plausible story.

The reader's assumption that the laptop belongs to a talking cat who later identifies herself as a programmer is itself interesting. We spontaneously assemble the same evidence into the same explanation that the cat wants the waiter to accept.

**In effect, her alibi can deceive the reader as successfully as it might deceive the waiter.**

I would definitely retain the spatial separation.

### **The reflection in the eye**

With the author's intention established, this is the pivotal panel of the entire page.

The laptop initially exists as part of the environment. Then it appears in the cat's pupil. Finally, it becomes the basis of her verbal response.

The intended visual causality, compressed into three stages.

This is economical visual storytelling. The reader is potentially witnessing the invention of a thought without a thought balloon, explanatory narration or internal monologue.

It is also why I would resist any revision that explicitly explains that she has just invented the excuse. The pleasure of discovering that connection is central to the page.

### **The waiter's apparent friendliness**

This works extremely well for the intended ambiguity.

He is excited, smiling and seemingly generous. His promise of employment on whatever terms she likes sounds reassuring. At the same time, he rapidly moves from discovering her ability to imagining how it could be commercially exploited.

These qualities are not mutually exclusive.

He may sincerely believe he is doing her a favour. He may be opportunistic. He may simply have given no thought to what a circus means from an animal's perspective.

The relevant issue is not necessarily whether he is dangerous. It is whether **she can afford to assume that he isn't**.

That asymmetry gives the encounter considerably more psychological substance than a straightforward story about a malicious human and a clever animal.

## **3. What works against the author's intention?**

### **There is no sufficiently distinct moment of alarm**

This is the principal problem.

In the opening panel, the cat is upright, confident and impatient. In the third panel, she appears relaxed, eyes closed and head raised. In the fourth, her narrowed eye suggests intelligence or concentration. In the final panel, she returns to her composed posture.

There is no unmistakable change indicating that the circus offer has affected her emotionally.

That is partly appropriate: she is concealing her reaction. But the reader needs slightly more access to her experience than the waiter has.

At present, her concealment works so well that it conceals the danger from the audience too.

The challenge is to introduce a sign of alarm without undermining her ability to disguise it.

### **Her dreamy expression is too convincing**

**Intended meaning**

She performs fascination to gain thinking time while concealing fear.

**Immediate visual meaning**

She closes her eyes and lifts her head because she is genuinely enjoying the thought of the circus.

Her expression in the third panel is almost blissful. Her raised chin, closed eyes and curled tail communicate apparent ease.

This is excellent camouflage from the character's perspective, but potentially excessive camouflage from the storyteller's perspective.

There needs to be some small contradiction between the expression she presents and the rest of her body.

Otherwise, the reader has no particular reason to distinguish performance from sincerity.

### **The cultural association between circuses and captivity is not activated strongly enough**

For a human, a circus typically evokes entertainment. For an animal, it can evoke confinement, training, coercion and exhibition.

The entire intended story depends on that difference.

However, the dialogue explicitly lists only the pleasant associations. The drawing does not quite make the negative associations equally available.

The shadows and chair backs can suggest bars, but they also have entirely ordinary explanations within the setting.

Moreover, the juxtaposition of a talking cat and a circus is so familiar in jokes and children's stories that the reader may perceive it as a conventional comic premise rather than a disturbing proposition.

The author is relying on the reader to recognise a reversal of perspective that the reader might never consciously make.

### **The original joke is almost too effective**

There is a peculiar adaptation problem here.

The traditional punchline already provides a satisfying explanation for everything:

The cat is a programmer. The waiter misunderstands the significance of her ability. The cat misunderstands the purpose of the job offer. The laptop confirms her profession. Everyone laughs.

That interpretation is complete, economical and familiar.

The intended interpretation requires the reader to reconsider the apparent evidence:

The cat may not be a programmer. She may not be naïve. The laptop may not be hers. Her enthusiasm may be performed. The circus may be frightening rather than attractive.

The deeper story must compete with a perfectly functional surface story.

That is not inherently a flaw; indeed, it is one of the comic's most interesting properties. But it means the darker interpretation needs at least one particularly well-placed visual clue that the conventional reading cannot explain quite as comfortably.

## **4. How well does the title “Alibi” work?**

Very well, although it communicates the strategy more clearly than the motive.

The title invites us to interpret the last line as a constructed explanation rather than a straightforward statement of fact.

It establishes the possibility of concealment. It directs attention towards evidence. It makes the laptop retrospectively significant. It puts the reader in the position of someone evaluating a story that might or might not be true.

But “Alibi” does not explain why the cat needs protection.

As my earlier interpretation demonstrated, the title can easily suggest professional identity as an excuse for refusing change, preserving independence or avoiding an unwanted occupation.

Those are plausible readings, but they lack the immediate existential concern of an animal avoiding captivity.

I would keep the title. Its ambiguity is productive, and a more explicit title such as “The Cage” would sacrifice much of the subtlety.

The necessary adjustment belongs in the imagery, not the title.

## **5. What I would change**

I would make relatively small changes. The basic composition, dialogue, colour scheme and five-panel structure are already well suited to the intended story.

### **Change 1 — Introduce a tiny, involuntary reaction to the circus offer**

**Ordinary alertness**

Relaxed, upright ears.

**Momentary unease**

Slightly rotated or lowered ears.

Exaggerated schematic comparison for clarity; the actual change should be considerably subtler.

The second panel already shows the back of the cat's head while the waiter makes his offer. This is an ideal place for a small change in ear position.

Other possibilities include a slight lowering of the shoulders or a momentary stiffening of her posture.

The advantage of using the ears is that the cat remains anatomically feline. Her involuntary reaction can communicate something that her sophisticated verbal performance conceals.

The waiter might overlook it. An attentive reader would not necessarily do so.

I would avoid an expression of overt terror. She is assessing a situation and thinking quickly, not surrendering to panic.

### **Change 2 — Strengthen the suggestion of a cage in the third panel**

Existing principle: looming shadow and architectural geometry.

Possible direction: shadows arranged more suggestively like bars.

I would adjust the existing window shadows so that they frame the cat in a way that momentarily evokes confinement.

Nothing supernatural needs to happen. The shadows should remain fully explicable as shadows cast by the café's architecture.

The important thing is their arrangement relative to her body.

This would create a visual counterpoint to her pleasant enumeration of the circus attractions. The words evoke entertainment; the image evokes captivity.

I would not introduce an actual cage, a flashback, a thought balloon depicting imprisoned animals, or an explicitly sinister circus owner. All of those would explain too much and make the question of the waiter's intentions less open.

### **Change 3 — Make the fourth panel feel like discovery rather than contemplation**

The eye close-up is already excellent in conception. I would preserve the yellow rectangular reflection exactly as a storytelling device.

What I would consider changing is the expression around the eye.

Currently, the narrowed eye can suggest a knowing smile, confidence, meditation or even satisfaction. A slightly more alert expression, or a clearer sense of a sideways glance, would help convey that she is searching her surroundings and has just found something useful.

The desired sequence is not simply:

*She thinks about the circus → she remembers her profession.*

It is:

*She needs an answer → she searches → she finds one.*

That distinction is crucial.

It might also help to establish the laptop's position more clearly in an earlier panel so that the direction of her glance is unmistakable when the reader looks back.

I would not make the reflection larger or add any explicit programming text to the laptop screen. Its current simplicity allows it to function as the seed of the excuse rather than proof of a profession.

### **Change 4 — Keep the dialogue almost entirely unchanged**

I would be very reluctant to rewrite the final line.

“But what would they need a programmer for?” is precisely the right formulation for the cat's strategy.

She does not directly refuse. She does not accuse. She does not reveal her fear. She does not challenge the waiter's intentions.

Instead, she invites him to reach the conclusion that employing her would be pointless.

She transforms a potentially dangerous disagreement into an apparent misunderstanding about recruitment.

The phrasing also preserves the possibility that she really is a programmer.

The only dialogue I might experiment with is the introductory part of her question about the circus, perhaps adding a barely perceptible hesitation:

> “A circus, you say?... That's the place with the acrobats, the jugglers, the lollipops, the cotton candy?”

Even that may be unnecessary. The artwork is the better place to communicate the concealed reaction.

## **6. What should remain ambiguous, and what should become clearer?**

I think this distinction is essential when revising the page.

| Productive ambiguity — preserve                      | Unintended obscurity — reduce                             |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Whether the waiter means harm                        | Whether the cat perceives possible harm                   |
| Whether she really is a programmer                   | Whether the laptop inspires her answer                    |
| Whether the excuse ultimately succeeds               | Whether she is trying to avoid the offer                  |
| Whether the danger is real or anticipated            | Why a circus could represent danger to her                |
| Whether some part of her finds the circus attractive | Whether her dreamy behaviour is at least partly strategic |

The page need not establish the existence of an actual threat. It needs to communicate **her perception of a possible threat**.

Once that is understood, the remaining uncertainties become genuinely interesting rather than obstacles to understanding the narrative.

## **7. An especially effective aspect of the intended story**

The cat uses a stereotype about programmers as camouflage.

The familiar joke encourages us to believe that a programmer might be sufficiently literal-minded to overlook the obvious reason a circus would employ a talking animal.

She can exploit precisely that expectation.

By pretending not to understand the significance of her own ability, she avoids admitting that she understands its significance very well.

Her apparent naïveté is potentially the product of considerable social intelligence.

This is what makes the adaptation more than an illustration of an existing joke: **the author turns the original joke's misunderstanding into the character's conscious survival strategy.**

The old joke invites us to laugh at the cat's lack of understanding. The new story invites us to reconsider whether that lack of understanding was ever real.

And because readers already know how such jokes work, they are particularly susceptible to her performance.

## **Overall assessment**

I would say the page, in its current form, works extremely well as a conventional joke, quite well as a story of ironic self-protection, and somewhat less well as the specific story of an animal improvising an escape from possible captivity.

The author's intention is recoverable from the existing details. It is not an interpretation arbitrarily imposed on the work afterwards. The title, looming shadow, spatial separation of the laptop, reflected screen, extended dialogue and ambiguous final statement all genuinely support it.

However, they do not yet give the intended interpretation enough weight to compete reliably with the conventional one.

If I could make only **two changes**, I would choose a subtle involuntary reaction in the cat's ears when the circus is mentioned and a slightly stronger suggestion of cage bars in the third panel's shadows.

Those two adjustments would establish her motive while preserving the uncertainty about the external situation.

Ideally, a reader could first laugh at the familiar punchline, then notice the title, reconsider the reflection, return to the shadow and finally understand what the cat has been doing throughout the conversation.

The second reading would not invalidate the first. It would reveal that the apparent simplicity of the first was precisely what the cat needed.

That would make “Alibi” a particularly apt title: the cat invents an explanation that works not only inside the story, but also on the person reading it.
