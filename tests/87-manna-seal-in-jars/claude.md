# What do you think is depicted in the attached image?

It's a cartoon mashing up the Book of Exodus with a very recognizable post-Soviet household habit.

**The biblical scene:** It's drawn in the style of a 19th-century Bible engraving and shows the manna episode from Exodus 16. Moses stands on a rock with his staff, the Israelites' tents are in the background, and people across the desert are gathering manna into baskets. The first line of the caption is Moses quoting the instruction almost verbatim from the Russian Synodal Bible: "Manna will come every morning. Let no one leave any of it until morning." In the story, people who hoarded it anyway found it full of worms and stinking by the next day. The point was to trust that tomorrow's provision would come.

**The joke:** In the foreground is an unmistakable babushka in a headscarf, sealing a glass jar of manna with a hand-held seaming key. She has more jars packed in a red-and-white checkered plastic bag, the iconic "shuttle trader" bag of the 1990s. Her reply: "And what if we *zakatat'* it?" That word means to seal preserves into jars for winter. Home canning (*zakatki*) is a deeply ingrained ritual in Russia, Ukraine, and the wider post-Soviet world, partly born of decades of scarcity. The humor is that a divine lesson about not hoarding runs straight into an instinct no commandment can defeat: if something can't be stored, you simply haven't found the right jar.

There's also a nice hidden irony. Later in the same chapter (Exodus 16:33), Aaron is actually told to put an omer of manna in a jar and keep it for future generations. So, in a sense, the babushka was technically right.

That's a clever pairing, and knowing the two sources changes how the cartoon reads.

Warhol and Yakovlev make a near-perfect mirror. Warhol's 32 identical cans hang like a supermarket shelf, showing abundance so routine and standardized that it becomes wallpaper. Yakovlev's still life is the opposite: glossy, warm, jars and tins nestled lovingly in straw, abundance as a promise. It was painted in the late 1930s, in the spirit of Stalin's 1935 slogan that "life has become better, life has become more cheerful." There's also a historical thread linking them. The Soviet canning industry was partly modeled on American food production after Anastas Mikoyan's 1936 trip to the United States, so Yakovlev's cans were already, in a sense, a translation of Campbell's.

The choice of Exodus 16 fits your "sausage emigration" reading very precisely. That's the same chapter where the newly freed Israelites complain that back in Egypt they "sat by the fleshpots" and ate bread to the full, and Numbers 11 adds a wistful list of Egypt's fish, cucumbers, melons, and garlic. The archetypal emigration story contains, right at its start, food nostalgia and grumbling about provisions. And late-Soviet Jewish emigration was routinely framed through Exodus ("Let my people go"), so the cartoon places its emigrant squarely between two canned-food mythologies.

I'd add that the joke cuts a little deeper than "they left for sausage." Manna is essentially the anti-canned food. It can't be stored, and its whole lesson is trusting that tomorrow's portion will come. Canning is the opposite: a technology of not trusting tomorrow. The babushka has crossed the Red Sea, food literally falls from heaven every morning, and she still reaches for the seaming key. She left Egypt, but the scarcity reflex came along in the checkered bag. This echoes a popular post-Soviet reading of the forty years in the wilderness: the generation shaped by slavery had to pass before the people could enter the Promised Land.

So the caricature works less as a verdict on whether emigration "made sense" and more as an observation that changing the source of food doesn't change your relationship to it. Whether it's Yakovlev's dreamed-of abundance, Warhol's real one, or bread from heaven, she'd still be rolling up jars for winter.
