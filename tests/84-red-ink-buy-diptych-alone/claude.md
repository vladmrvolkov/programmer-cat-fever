This diptych is a two-beat joke with a sharp point: **seeing through consumerism doesn't stop you from consuming.**

**Left panel: the revelation.** A man stands outside a shop window looking at a glossy, sun-drenched ad: a smiling woman, a coastal village, champagne, lemons, linen. It sells a lifestyle, not a product. When he holds a pair of sunglasses up to it, the lens strips the fantasy away, and in gritty black and white the image's real message appears: **BUY**. This directly references John Carpenter's *They Live* (1988), where special sunglasses reveal the hidden commands (OBEY, CONSUME, BUY) behind ads and media. The contrast between the warm, painterly ad and the cold, grainy lens view is the contrast between seduction and truth.

**Right panel: the punchline.** Now he's inside the store, wearing the sunglasses as a fashion accessory, with the price tags still hanging off a T-shirt that says **BUY**. He's admiring himself in the mirror, pleased. The hidden command he uncovered has become a product, and he's buying it. Several details sharpen this:

- **The sunglasses changed roles.** In the first panel they were a tool for exposing the truth. In the second they're just a stylish look.
- **He moved from outside to inside.** He began as a critical observer on the street and ended up in the store.
- **He became the advertisement.** He used to read the message. Now he displays it to everyone else. In the mirror, the word appears backwards ("YUB"), so the slogan on his chest isn't really for him; it's for the people who see him.
- **His smile.** He isn't fooled. He knows exactly what the shirt says, and he buys it anyway, maybe because the knowing feels clever.

**The bigger idea.** The image illustrates what's sometimes called *recuperation*: capitalism's habit of absorbing criticism of itself and selling it back as a product. Think anti-consumerist slogans on merch, or rebellion as an aesthetic. It also echoes a well-known reading of *They Live* by the philosopher Slavoj Žižek: ideology doesn't need us to be deceived. People can understand perfectly well that ads manipulate them and keep obeying anyway. Awareness becomes one more identity to buy into.

In one sentence: the first panel shows a man discovering the hidden message, and the second shows him wearing it proudly.
