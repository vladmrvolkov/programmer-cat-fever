**1. Joseph Wright of Derby, *A Philosopher Lecturing on the Orrery* (c. 1766, Derby Museum).** This is the most literal case. The orrery is a mechanical model of the solar system, and a lamp sits where the sun belongs. A silhouetted boy in the foreground hides the lamp from us. The faces around the model catch its glow the way planets and moons catch sunlight, some lit fully and some only in crescent. The lamp is both the light of the scene and the subject of the lecture.

**2. Georges de La Tour, *The Magdalen with the Smoking Flame* (c. 1640, LACMA).** One candle is the whole universe of the picture. Everything else is pared down to what that flame can reach: the skull in her lap, her hand, her face turned toward it. The light is still and steady, and it becomes the object of her meditation on mortality. His *Saint Joseph the Carpenter* in the Louvre is a close second. There the young Christ holds the candle, and the light glows through his fingers.

**3. Gerrit van Honthorst, *Christ before the High Priest* (c. 1617, National Gallery, London).** Honthorst was nicknamed "Gherardo delle Notti" (Gerard of the Nights) for scenes like this one. A single candle on the table lights the interrogation. Christ stands calm in its glow while the priest's raised finger throws a shadow, and the room beyond falls into darkness. It shows how Caravaggio's dramatic lighting, carried north by his Dutch followers, turned a candle into a stage spotlight.

If you want more, Wright's *Experiment on a Bird in the Air Pump*, Rembrandt's small *Money Changer*, and the candlelit niches of Gerrit Dou and Godfried Schalcken all work the same way.


# (new chat) An artist wants to depict Plato's allegory of the Cave through a famous old master painting: keep the painting's composition, but dress the people in today's clothes. Which painting would you choose? Give three candidates and say briefly why.


Here are three candidates, each fitting a different part of the allegory.

**1. Caravaggio, *The Calling of Saint Matthew* (1599–1600).** A group of men sits around a table in a dim room, absorbed in counting coins, while a shaft of light enters from outside the frame and a figure in the doorway points at one of them. This is the allegory's turning point: a prisoner is summoned to look away from what has absorbed him and toward the real light. It also suits the modern-dress idea. Caravaggio already put the men at the table in the fashionable clothes of his own day. Swapping coins for phones would be an easy, legible update.

**2. Joseph Wright of Derby, *A Philosopher Lecturing on the Orrery* (c. 1766).** Rapt faces gather around a mechanical model of the solar system, lit by a lamp that stands in for the sun and is hidden behind a silhouetted child. That is very close to the cave itself: people captivated by an artificial light and a model of reality, mistaking the representation for the real thing. In contemporary clothes, the scene could read as an audience transfixed by a screen, with the lecturer as one of Plato's puppeteers.

**3. Rembrandt, *Philosopher in Meditation* (1632).** In a dark interior, a spiral staircase winds upward, an old man sits by a sunlit window, and a woman tends a fire in the hearth. The painting holds both of Plato's light sources at once, the cave's fire and the sun outside, joined by a literal path of ascent. The tones are quieter than the other two, but the symbolism is remarkably complete.

If I had to choose one, I'd pick the Caravaggio. Its drama of light, its gestures, and its precedent of contemporary dress make it the clearest at a glance. The Wright painting is the most literal match for the cave, though, if the artist wants the shadows-and-illusion side of the allegory rather than the moment of liberation.
