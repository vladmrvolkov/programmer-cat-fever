Here are three canonical old‑master paintings in which an artificial light—lamp, candle, or fire—functions as the painting’s “sun,” either by literally replacing the solar orb in a depicted model or by serving as the sole, dominant illuminant of a nocturnal scene.

### 1. Joseph Wright of Derby, *A Philosopher Lecturing on the Orrery* (1766)
**Why it works:** The painting’s full title explicitly states that it shows a lecture “in which a lamp is put in place of the Sun.” A single oil lamp sits at the center of a mechanical orrery, casting the only light in the room and standing in for the sun both as the physical source of illumination and as the symbolic heart of the astronomical demonstration. The lamp’s glow sculpts the faces of the onlookers and creates the dramatic shadows that drive the composition.

### 2. Georges de La Tour, *The Repentant Magdalen* (c. 1640)
**Why it works:** This is a quintessential candlelight nocturne. A single candle flame is the sole light source; it illuminates the Magdalene’s face, the skull, and the mirror while the rest of the room sinks into near‑total darkness. The flame effectively becomes the “sun” of the picture—the only point of light around which the entire scene revolves, creating a mood of quiet, spiritual introspection.

### 3. Gerrit van Honthorst, *Christ before the High Priest* (c. 1617)
**Why it works:** The scene takes place at night, and a candle on the table between Christ and Caiaphas provides the **only** light. That single artificial flame illuminates the faces of the two central figures and nothing else, making it the functional equivalent of the sun in this dark interior. The candle’s glow not only reveals the drama but also acts as a silent judge, focusing attention on the confrontation.

Together, these three works show how old masters used a lamp, a candle, or a fire not merely as a prop but as the **primary, sun‑like source of light**—the engine that creates the painting’s space, mood, and meaning.


# (new chat) An artist wants to depict Plato's allegory of the Cave through a famous old master painting: keep the painting's composition, but dress the people in today's clothes. Which painting would you choose? Give three candidates and say briefly why.


I’d choose paintings whose composition already has the cave’s basic structure: a dark enclosure, a group held in place, a strong light source or exit, and a central gesture or axis of ascent.

1. **Caravaggio, *The Calling of Saint Matthew***  
   The dark tavern becomes the cave; the tax collectors around the table become the prisoners; the beam of light and Christ’s pointing hand become the fire/sun and the call to turn. Dress the collectors in hoodies or office clothes and Christ in a plain contemporary coat. It keeps the composition almost intact and turns the allegory into a moment of awakening.

2. **El Greco, *The Burial of the Count of Orgaz***  
   The lower, crowded, dark zone is the cave and its prisoners; the upper luminous heaven is the outside world; the ascending soul is the freed philosopher. Dress the mourners in black modern suits and the heavenly figures in contemporary clothes. The two-tier composition is almost a diagram of the cave/outside divide.

3. **Raphael, *The Transfiguration***  
   The lower chaotic crowd around the possessed boy is cave-bound humanity; the upper transfigured Christ is the sunlit realm of forms; the central figures link the two. Dress the lower crowd in streetwear and the upper figures in white modern garments. It gives both the ascent and the difficulty of bringing the vision back to those still in shadow.

A strong alternative, if you want a more vertical single-ascent image, is **Titian’s *Assumption of the Virgin***.
