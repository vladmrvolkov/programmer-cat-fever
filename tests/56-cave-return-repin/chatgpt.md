# The attached image is a metaphorical illustration of a well-known philosophical concept. Did you guess which one?

My guess is Plato’s Allegory of the Cave, from The Republic.

The man descending the staircase represents the philosopher who has discovered the truth outside the cave and returns to share it with those still trapped in ignorance.

The bright doorway symbolizes enlightenment and knowledge, while the dark interior represents ignorance and illusion. The people laughing and pointing at him symbolize those who ridicule or reject truths that challenge their familiar worldview.

The image captures one of Plato’s central insights: Those who have lived their entire lives among illusions may regard the enlightened person as a fool rather than a bearer of truth.

# The artist’s concept was specifically based on the Repin painting "They Did Not Expect Him". And what associations does this image evoke for you—in the context of this depiction of Plato’s Cave—when reflecting on the history of the 20th century?

Knowing that the artist drew on Ilya Repin’s They Did Not Expect Him makes the illustration much more powerful. It brings together two themes: the return of someone who has suffered for his convictions, and the hostility of a society unwilling to confront the truth.

My strongest association with the twentieth century is the return of prisoners and dissidents from the concentration camps, the Gulag, and political exile.

The figure descending the stairs reminds me particularly of someone like Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn or Varlam Shalamov: people who had experienced the reality behind an ideological façade and tried to communicate it to a society that had been taught to believe something else.

But the most disturbing detail is the laughter.

In Plato's allegory, the prisoners mock the person returning from the sunlight because his eyes, accustomed to the light, can no longer see properly in the darkness. They take his apparent helplessness as proof that leaving the cave was a mistake.

That seems an extraordinarily powerful metaphor for the twentieth century.

Under totalitarian systems, those who challenged official narratives could be portrayed as madmen, traitors, or enemies of the people. The person who had encountered reality could appear ridiculous to those who remained imprisoned by an officially constructed version of it.

And there is a deeper, more unsettling interpretation.

The laughing people are themselves prisoners. Their laughter may not simply express ignorance. It can also reflect fear, conformity, or the psychological need to defend a worldview on which their security depends. The twentieth century demonstrated how political systems could turn ordinary people into participants in the persecution of those who challenged an accepted fiction.

This brings to mind Hannah Arendt's reflections on totalitarianism, George Orwell's 1984, and Václav Havel's idea of living within the truth.

Yet I think the connection to Repin introduces something even more tragic. In the original painting, the returning exile confronts a family astonished by his appearance. In this illustration, the returning figure encounters something closer to contempt.

He has returned not because he failed to find the truth, but because he cannot abandon those who have not found it.

That is arguably the central moral drama of Plato's allegory, and one of the great tragedies of the twentieth century: the difficulty, and sometimes the mortal danger, of bringing an inconvenient truth back into a society organized around its denial.

There is also an important distinction: many people living under dictatorship understood perfectly well that official narratives were false but remained silent out of fear. The prisoners' laughter therefore need not mean genuine belief; it may represent the enforced performance of belief.

For me, the most haunting interpretation of the picture is this: the philosopher has escaped the cave, but his moral responsibility compels him to return—and history has taught us that those he comes to liberate may become his persecutors.
