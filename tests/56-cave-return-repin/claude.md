# The attached image is a metaphorical illustration of a well-known philosophical concept. Did you guess which one?

Yes, I'd say it's **Plato's Allegory of the Cave** from the *Republic* (Book VII), and specifically its often-overlooked second half: the philosopher's **return** to the cave.

Several details fit the scene:

- **The blinding doorway at the top** stands for the world outside the cave, the realm of true reality lit by the sun (Plato's image for the Form of the Good). The man has just come from there.
- **His squint and his hand on the wall.** Plato says that someone who comes back down from the sunlight would be blinded by the sudden darkness, unable to see clearly and stumbling about. The painter shows exactly that disorientation.
- **The glowing fire or cauldron in the gloom** corresponds to the firelight inside the cave, the weak source of light that casts the shadows the prisoners mistake for reality.
- **The two men laughing and pointing** are the cave-dwellers. Socrates predicts they would mock the returning philosopher, claiming his trip upward ruined his eyes and proved the ascent wasn't worth it. Socrates then adds a dark note: if he tried to lead them out, they would kill him if they could, which is widely read as an allusion to Socrates' own trial and execution.

Most illustrations of the allegory focus on the chained prisoners or the climb toward the light, so it's interesting that this one shows the descent. That captures Plato's point that the philosopher has a duty to go back down and help others, even at the cost of ridicule. It also echoes the *Republic*'s very first word, *katebēn*, "I went down," as Socrates describes going down to the Piraeus.

# Which painting by a famous artist do you think inspired the creator of the illustration?


I can't say for certain, but my best guess is **Rembrandt's *Philosopher in Meditation* (1632, Louvre)**. Several things point that way.

That painting is one of the most famous images ever tied to philosophy, which fits an illustrator working on Plato. It's also built on the same elements as your illustration. There's a stone staircase, a strong shaft of daylight from a window cutting through a dark, vaulted interior, and a glowing fire in the gloomy lower corner with a figure crouched beside it. There's even a second figure on the stairs. The illustration seems to rearrange those pieces: the staircase becomes the path between light and darkness, the window becomes the cave's exit, and the hearth becomes the cave's fire. Even the palette of browns, ochres, and fiery reds with deep shadows feels Rembrandtian.

A secondary influence might be **Caravaggio's *The Calling of Saint Matthew*** (1599–1600). That painting also has a beam of light from the upper right falling into a dim room, a group of men gathered on the left, and a pointing gesture at the center of the drama. The illustration flips the meaning: in Caravaggio, the pointing comes with the light, as a call to a higher life. Here the people in the dark point at the man who brings the light, and they're mocking him.

The overall rendering, with its slightly theatrical lighting and genre-scene tavern figures, also has a 19th-century academic feel, as if a Romantic-era painter had reinterpreted Rembrandt. If you know where the illustration comes from, I'd be curious whether the artist named a source.

# And what if we take not the entire image, but only the figure and pose of the person descending?

If we isolate the descending figure, the obvious candidate is **Marcel Duchamp's *Nude Descending a Staircase, No. 2* (1912)**, probably the most famous painting of a person going down stairs.

At first the comparison seems odd, since Duchamp's figure is a fragmented, almost mechanical Cubo-Futurist abstraction. But the parallels are real. Both figures stride down toward the viewer mid-step, with the body twisting slightly and one leg reaching for the next stair. Both use a warm palette of browns, ochres, and wood tones. And the illustrator's coat seems to echo Duchamp: its flaring, overlapping panels and hard-edged folds read almost like Duchamp's stacked, cone-like planes. They suggest movement through a sequence of positions rather than a single frozen moment. In a sense, the illustration "re-realizes" Duchamp's abstraction, much as Gerhard Richter did in *Ema (Nude on a Staircase)* (1966), his photorealistic answer to Duchamp.

If the illustrator was thinking more about meaning than about the mechanics of the pose, another strong candidate is **Ilya Repin's *They Did Not Expect Him* (1884–88)**. A gaunt man in a long, worn coat steps through a doorway mid-stride, returning from political exile to a room full of people who stare at him uncertainly. A man coming back from somewhere others haven't been and being received with incomprehension is almost exactly the situation of Plato's returning philosopher.

So my answer is Duchamp for the pose and Repin for the spirit. Is either of them the painting you had in mind?


# Yes, the artist’s concept was specifically based on the Repin painting. And what associations does this image evoke for you—in the context of this depiction of Plato’s Cave—when reflecting on the history of the 20th century?

That's a perfect choice of source. Repin's painting is about the same thing as the second half of Plato's allegory: what happens when someone comes back from a place others haven't been, and how those who stayed receive him. Set against the 20th century, it brings several associations to mind for me.

**The revolutionary as returning philosopher.** Repin's exile is a narodnik, a young idealist who "went to the people" with a truth he believed would liberate them. The 20th century turned that figure into a historical force. In April 1917 Lenin stepped off the train at the Finland Station with his April Theses, and even many fellow socialists thought he had lost his mind; Plekhanov publicly called the theses raving. That's almost Plato's script: the man returns, and the others say his eyes are ruined. But here the story turns dark. The people who claimed to have seen the sun and come back to lead the cave-dwellers out often built new caves, with far more sophisticated shadow projectors in the form of mass propaganda, cinema, and censorship. This is why Karl Popper, in *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945), read Plato's philosopher-kings as a blueprint for totalitarianism: a vanguard that alone has seen the light and therefore has the right to rule. And there's a bitter twist on Socrates' fate. Many of the old revolutionaries who, like Repin's hero, returned from tsarist exile later died in the purges of the regime they helped create.

**Return from darkness rather than light.** The century also produced a reversed version of the allegory, with witnesses coming back from the abyss rather than from the sun. Their truth was just as unbearable to hear. When Gulag prisoners began returning after 1953–56, Anna Akhmatova remarked that two Russias would now look each other in the eye: the one that imprisoned and the one that was imprisoned. That's very close to the tense scene in Repin's room. Holocaust witnesses met the same wall. When Jan Karski described what he had seen in Poland, Justice Felix Frankfurter told him he simply could not believe him. And Primo Levi describes a recurring dream from Auschwitz in which he is home, telling his story, and his listeners are indifferent and walk away. The cave's laughter becomes disbelief and averted eyes.

**"His eyes are ruined" as a diagnosis.** Plato's prisoners explain away the returnee by saying his journey damaged his vision. The Soviet state made that literal through punitive psychiatry: dissidents were diagnosed with "sluggish schizophrenia" and confined to psychiatric hospitals. Insisting on a truth the cave rejects became, officially, a symptom.

**Who is the true returnee?** This may be the deepest association. Plato's allegory doesn't tell the cave-dwellers how to distinguish someone who has truly seen the sun from a fanatic who only believes he has. The 20th century had both, often wearing the same long coat and carrying the same certainty. That's why I find Repin's painting a richer source than the laughing figures in the illustration. In Repin, the faces are unresolved: the mother rises, the wife freezes, one child lights up and the other shrinks back. It's not mockery but uncertainty, fear and hope at once. That's perhaps the most honest picture of how a society meets the person who comes back.

Do you know whether the artist saw the man as a genuine bearer of truth, or deliberately left it ambiguous?
