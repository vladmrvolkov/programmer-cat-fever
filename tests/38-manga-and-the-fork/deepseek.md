Here is how the author's notes tie together all the clever details, subtext, and historical references in the comic:

**1. The "Bakeneko" Lore (The Core Theme)**
The note on the **BAKENEKO** is the key to the protagonist's character. In Japanese folklore, a *bakeneko* is a cat that lives long enough to gain supernatural powers—it grows, walks upright, speaks, and puts on clothes. But the crucial line is: *"Nothing in the stories says it stops being a cat."* 
This perfectly explains her character design. She wears a professional suit and uses a laptop, but she still has her large feline ears, her tail, and her fluffy paw. The waiter is trying to force human or circus-performer labels onto her, but she is just a *bakeneko* living her life as a programmer.

**2. The Cultural Setting (The Kissaten)**
The notes confirm the setting is a *kissaten* (a traditional Japanese coffeehouse). The kanji "珈琲" (coffee) isn't just decorative; the author notes it was chosen in the 19th century for its phonetic sound, originally referring to hairpin ornaments. This reinforces the blend of tradition and modernity that the cat herself represents.

**3. The Waiter's Stereotypes (Why a Cat?)**
The **WHY A CAT?** section reveals the historical context: In 1842, the Edo government banned prints of actors, courtesans, and dancers. The artist Utagawa Kuniyoshi (whose style heavily influences this comic) got around the censorship by drawing these people as cats, sparrows, and goldfish. 
This is why the waiter immediately thinks of a circus (*misemono*) and assumes she must be there to perform. He is viewing her through the lens of historical stereotypes and Edo-era entertainment, completely missing that she is a modern, white-collar professional.

**4. The Visual "Silence" (シーン)**
The note on the **シーン (Shiin)** sound effect is a fantastic manga-specific detail. It represents absolute silence. In the second panel of the first page, after the waiter's internal screaming ("A TALKING CAT?!"), the protagonist delivers her deadpan response ("Are you bringing my order or not?") surrounded by *shiin*. The author notes there is no English equivalent, leaving it in the original Japanese preserves the deadpan comedic timing perfectly. 

**5. The Background Details**
