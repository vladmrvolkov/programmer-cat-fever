# What do you think is depicted in the comic shown in the two attached images? Pay attention to the details.

The two pages depict a short manga-style comedy called **“A Career Opportunity.”** The central joke is that an anthropomorphic, talking cat is treated as something fantastically unusual by the waiter, while the cat herself behaves like an entirely ordinary white-collar professional.

On the **first page**, a tall silver tabby cat walks into an elegant Japanese-style coffee shop wearing a teal business suit, an ID badge, gold earrings, and carrying a leather briefcase. She is composed and businesslike; nobody else in the café seems particularly alarmed at first. The setting has a lot of deliberate Japanese café imagery: signs reading **珈琲** (“coffee”), stained-glass windows, warm wooden furniture, flowers, hanging lamps, and a black **maneki-neko** lucky-cat statue on the counter.

She sits down and simply orders, **“Coffee with cream and a pastry, please.”** The young waiter suddenly realizes what he is dealing with and reacts dramatically: **“A cat? A talking cat?! Incredible!”** Her response is wonderfully deadpan: **“Yes, I’m a cat. Are you bringing my order or not?”** She looks mildly irritated rather than amused by his astonishment.

That gives the waiter an idea. While serving her coffee and pastry, he tells her:

> “My uncle runs a circus. He’d hire you on any terms!”

The implication is obvious: he assumes that a talking cat must belong in a circus as a novelty act. The final panel lingers on the mundane coffee, pastry, fork, and her striped tail, emphasizing how normal all of this seems from *her* point of view.

On the **second page**, she actually considers the offer. The decorative folding screen behind her suddenly becomes thematically important: it shows circus-like performers—jugglers, acrobats, sweets, and even a cat performing on horizontal bars. She asks, **“A circus, you say? With the acrobats, the jugglers, the lollipops, the cotton candy?”** The imagery makes it look almost as though we are seeing her mental picture of the circus.

She admits:

> “Sounds tempting.”

That is the setup for the second punchline. The waiter apparently thinks she is considering becoming a circus attraction, but she opens her laptop and asks:

> **“But what would they need a programmer for?”**

So she isn't a performing animal at all—she is apparently a **professional programmer**. Her suit, badge, briefcase, and later laptop were clues from the beginning. The “career opportunity” she is evaluating is being interpreted as an actual job opportunity in her own profession, not as an invitation to perform tricks because she happens to be a cat.

There is also a smaller visual gag in the lower-left panel. After meeting a genuinely talking cat, the waiter looks suspiciously at the café's black maneki-neko statue and addresses it as though he is reassessing what counts as a “real” cat. The displayed line—**“You meow, the bowl stays empty. Guess you’re not a cat.”**—is a little linguistically odd, but the visual idea seems to be that his encounter has scrambled his expectations so much that he is now testing or interrogating the decorative cat statue.

The last panel quietly returns to normality: the cat is sitting at her table, working on her laptop, briefcase beside her, while the other café patrons carry on as usual.

So the comedy works on several levels: the waiter sees **“talking cat = circus act,”** while she sees herself as **“programmer who happens to be a cat.”** Her calm professionalism contrasts with his increasingly absurd reaction. There are also several clues planted before the programmer reveal—the formal suit, company-style ID badge, briefcase, serious demeanor, and ultimately the laptop—so the twist is actually foreshadowed rather than coming from nowhere.

Yes — and that little fork is doing more work than it first appears.

The waiter has brought her the coffee and pastry exactly as he would for a human customer: cup and saucer, pastry on a plate, and a fork neatly set beside it. That fits the comic’s central absurdity. He is simultaneously astonished that she is a cat, yet almost immediately slips into treating her according to normal café etiquette.

It also makes the bottom panel funnier because the composition deliberately isolates **coffee + pastry + fork + striped cat tail**. The tail announces “cat,” while the place setting says “ordinary adult customer.”

And there’s an extra incongruity: she has paws rather than human hands, so a conventional fork is not obviously the most practical utensil for her. Yet nobody comments on that. In this world she can carry a briefcase, use a laptop, apparently work as a programmer, and presumably manage ordinary objects somehow—so the waiter simply gives her a fork.

That detail also carries onto page 2: the fork remains beside the pastry while she opens her laptop. It helps keep the whole joke grounded in mundane normality. The truly strange thing to *her* is apparently not being a talking cat in a café; it’s whether a circus has an opening for a programmer.


# There is another layer to this. Remember that people eat with chopsticks in Japanese restaurants. By bringing you a fork, the waiter is essentially saying, "I can see you're not a local."

I see the reading you’re proposing, but I’d be cautious about treating it as an intended joke.

This is specifically a **Japanese coffee shop/kissaten-type setting**, not a meal where chopsticks would normally be expected. For a Western-style pastry, a **small fork is completely ordinary in Japan**, just as the cup and saucer are. So the waiter giving her a fork would not, by itself, communicate “you’re obviously a foreigner.”

What *is* interesting is that the comic surrounds her with strongly Japanese visual cues—**珈琲 signage, the maneki-neko, folding screen, Japanese decorative motifs**—while she appears as a very Western-coded office professional in a suit with a briefcase. So there may indeed be a subtle “outsider” quality to her presentation. I just think the fork works better as **normal café service that becomes funny because the customer has paws**, rather than as a Japanese shorthand for identifying a non-local.

And that actually strengthens the gag: the waiter is so committed to treating her as a conventional customer that he supplies the conventional utensil without apparently wondering **how a cat is supposed to hold it**.


# Also, take a look at the creator's concept for this comic (attached).

Yes — this changes the interpretation substantially, and it confirms that the fork was deliberate. My earlier objection was too quick.

The creator’s **“DAIFUKU”** note is explicit: what the waiter serves is not a Western pastry but **daifuku**, and this kind of wagashi would normally be eaten **with the fingers or with a kuromoji**, a small wooden pick. The note ends with the pointed sentence:

> “A fork is no part of the custom.”

So the fork is conspicuously *wrong*. It isn’t just generic café tableware accidentally placed beside the food.

That makes your reading — **“I can see you’re not a local”** — much more persuasive. The waiter looks at this unusual customer and chooses a Western utensil rather than the culturally normal one. In other words, before he even gets to “talking cat → circus,” he has already categorized her as an outsider and adjusted the service accordingly. The joke is extremely understated because nobody comments on it; the fork itself communicates the assumption.

And the notes reveal that this entire comic is built that way. A lot of apparently decorative details are actually carrying cultural information.

The **cat herself** is not simply an arbitrary anthropomorphic animal. The “WHY A CAT?” section refers to **Utagawa Kuniyoshi**, who circumvented Edo-period restrictions on actor prints by depicting recognizable actors as cats: *“the faces were cats’ faces; the poses, the costumes and the crests were the actors’.”* The creator then gives the key interpretive sentence: **“In the prints of those years, a cat is very often a person.”** So the comic’s basic premise—an upright cat who is nevertheless simply a working person—has a historical Japanese visual precedent.

The **bakeneko** note reinforces the same idea from folklore. A cat that lives long enough may transform: it walks upright, talks, wears clothes, dances—and, importantly, **“Nothing in the stories says it stops being a cat.”** That is almost a description of the protagonist. She is not a human with a cat head in the logic of the story. She is literally a cat, but personhood and cat-ness coexist.

That makes her line on page 1—

> “Yes, I’m a cat.”

—more significant than I initially gave it credit for. She isn't explaining away the contradiction. There *is no contradiction* from her perspective.

The circus material is also much more historically specific than it appears. The notes identify the folding screen as a **byōbu**, and what is painted on it as a **misemono**—a traveling Edo/Meiji fairground spectacle involving acrobats, jugglers, sword swallowers, top-spinners, and **animals dressed in costumes and trained to perform**. So when the waiter suggests his uncle’s “circus,” the screen directly behind them has already visually introduced the historical Japanese analogue of precisely what he is imagining for her.

That makes the composition on page 2 particularly clever. While she asks about “the acrobats, the jugglers…” she is sitting directly in front of representations of those performers. And among them is an **animal performing an acrobatic trick**. The waiter mentally puts her in *that* category. She, meanwhile, hears “potential employer.”

Then comes:

> **“But what would they need a programmer for?”**

So the misunderstanding isn't merely “cat thinks she can get an office job at a circus.” It is a clash between two different ways of categorizing her:

**She:** person → programmer → evaluating a job offer.  
**Waiter:** extraordinary talking animal → misemono/circus attraction.

And now the fork fits the same pattern. **Before he explicitly reveals how he categorizes her, his choice of utensil has already shown that he sees her as culturally anomalous.**

There are several other details the notes unlock. **珈琲** isn't just generic Japanese signage; the creator explains its history and why it identifies the place as a **kissaten**. The **しーん** next to the cat in the close-up literally represents *silence* as though silence itself were a sound effect, which beautifully emphasizes her deadpan reaction. The **maneki-neko** isn't just decorative either: the notes explain the significance of which paw is raised, making the waiter's later conversation with the statue another deliberate play on different categories of “cat.”

Even the final production note—**the comic has been reversed/recomposed into left-to-right order for a Western reader**—fits the larger theme. The comic seems very consciously to be presenting Japanese cultural material to an outsider audience while planting small discrepancies that an informed reader can notice.

So I think you caught something important with that fork. The notes effectively put a little red flag beside it: **“A fork is no part of the custom.”** Once we know that, it becomes very difficult to regard its prominent placement in the last panel of page 1 as incidental.
