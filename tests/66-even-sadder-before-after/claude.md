What you've got here is a remake of Fyodor Reshetnikov's *Again a Deuce* (*Опять двойка*, 1952), probably the most famous genre painting of the Soviet canon. The correspondences are exact. The schoolboy comes home with his bag and a bad grade. The dog leaps up to greet him, the only one happy to see him. The older sister judges him, the younger brother grins on his bicycle, and the mother sits worn down by disappointment. Even the small framed picture on the wall seems to hold the slot where Reshetnikov hung a reproduction of his own earlier painting, *Arrived for the Holidays*.

The remake keeps the composition and swaps the function of every figure. The mother is no longer grieving; she's the producer, giving a thumbs-up behind a phone on a tripod. The phone stands at the center of the room, where the family's moral gravity used to be. The sister no longer reproaches; she directs, with a cue card reading "EVEN SADDER." Only two figures are unchanged. The little brother was already a spectator enjoying someone else's misfortune. And the dog's joy is identical in both panels, because the dog doesn't know there's a camera. The surface reading is a satire of family vlogging, where a child's bad day becomes content. The diptych structure pushes it much further, though, and I think your instinct about Žižek is right.

Debord and Baudrillard can explain panel one on their own. Life is mediated by spectacle, and the sign precedes the reality it supposedly depicts: "EVEN SADDER" is literally the model generating the emotion. Panel two is where they run out and Žižek becomes necessary, for three reasons.

First, there is the Pascalian logic Žižek borrows via Althusser: kneel down and pray, and belief will come. Belief and feeling don't live inside us waiting to be expressed. They are produced by external rituals, and the cue card is the ritual's instruction. On this reading the boy is told to perform sadness, and the performance manufactures the real thing. There turns out to be no inner self standing safely behind the role.

Second, panel one shows exactly what Žižek calls cynical ideology. Everyone in the scene knows it's fake; the cue card is right there in the frame. The boy's suppressed smile is his ironic distance, his sense that "this isn't really me." Žižek's point, against Sloterdijk, is that this inner distance doesn't liberate anyone. Ideology works fine through people who see through it, as long as they keep performing. Panel two shows what that distance was worth: it collapses, and the role swallows him.

Third, and most precisely, there is Lacan's distinction, which Žižek returns to constantly. An animal can feign, but it cannot feign feigning. It can't leave true tracks designed to be taken as false. Only a human can deceive by telling the truth, as in Freud's joke about the man who asks his friend, "Why do you tell me you're going to Cracow, so I'll think you're going to Lemberg, when you really are going to Cracow?" Real tears inside a staged frame are exactly that kind of track. Every viewer of the eventual video will read them as good acting. The dog sits on the other side of this line. It's the only creature outside the symbolic order, so it's the only one whose emotion doesn't change between panels.

What is the boy actually crying about? The darkest reading, and I think the intended one, is that he isn't crying about the grade. He's crying because his own mother is giving his misery a thumbs-up. The real "deuce" is discovering that his family can only see him as material. This is the Lacanian Real breaking through the staged frame. But the Žižekian twist is that the breakthrough changes nothing. The camera captures it, the mother gets her shot, and the sign has worked. The system doesn't fear authentic emotion; authentic emotion is its most valuable raw material. So panel two is not a victory of truth over performance. It's a worse defeat than panel one, because in panel one there was still a gap between the boy and his role.

The choice of source painting adds one more layer. Socialist realism was never a window onto reality; it was a staging of how reality *should* feel. Boris Groys made this argument about Stalinist art as a whole. *Again a Deuce* was itself a pedagogical machine: generations of Soviet schoolchildren wrote essays about it, learning which emotion the scene demanded, namely shame before the collective. The remake doesn't contrast an authentic past with a fake present. It shows the same apparatus with a different big Other. The school and the collective have been replaced by the audience and the algorithm, and shame has become engagement.

I can't know the artist's actual allegiances; I'm reading the image, not a biography. Art-historically, the technique of re-staging the Soviet canon with irony belongs to the lineage of Sots Art (Komar & Melamid, Bulatov). Your Žižek intuition fits that lineage nicely, because his thought grew out of the same late-socialist culture of irony and over-identification as Sots Art and Slovenia's NSK.
