The attached diptych is a contemporary, postmodern reimagining of Fyodor Reshetnikov’s famous 1952 Socialist Realist painting, ***Low Marks Again*** (*Опять двойка*).

---

### 1. Visual Deconstruction: From 1952 to the TikTok Era

In Reshetnikov’s original canvas, a Soviet schoolboy returns home with a failing grade, met with his mother’s sorrowful disappointment, his older sister’s stern judgment, and his younger brother’s gleeful mockery—while only the family dog offers unconditional love.

In this modern adaptation, the entire domestic drama has been transformed into a **staged content-creation studio**:

* **The Smartphone Apparatus:** At the center stands a tripod-mounted smartphone recording the boy and the dog, cropping out the rest of the room to manufacture an "authentic" viral clip.


* **The Family as Film Crew:** The mother is no longer grieving over a bad grade; she looks at the phone screen and gives an encouraging thumbs-up. The older sister acts as an aggressive stage director, frowning and pointing while holding a cue card demanding **"EVEN SADDER"**. The younger brother remains on his bike, watching the production unfold.


* **State 1 vs. State 2:** In the left panel, the boy breaks character—his genuine human reaction (amusement at the dog licking his face or the absurdity of the setup) leaks through as a suppressed smile. In the right panel, he submits to the cue card and produces genuine tears, while every other element in the room remains frozen in identical positions.

### 2. Why Slavoj Žižek Fits So Well (Lacanian Psychoanalysis & Ideology)

Your instinct to invoke **Slavoj Žižek** is spot-on. Through a Žižekian and Lacanian lens, this diptych illustrates three core concepts:

* **The Shift from the "Big Other" of the State to the Algorithm:** In Lacanian psychoanalysis, the *Big Other* is the invisible symbolic authority for whom we perform our lives. In 1952, the Big Other was the Soviet state and collective morality (embodied by the reproaching sister and mother). Today, the Big Other is the **social media gaze**—the anonymous audience and the algorithm encapsulated in the smartphone lens.


* **The Superego Injunction to Feel:** Žižek frequently argues that postmodern authority doesn't forbid; it *commands us to enjoy* (or, in the case of sadness-porn and clout-chasing, commands us to *feel* intensely on cue). The sister holding the **"EVEN SADDER"** sign is the literal embodiment of the cruel Lacanian superego—demanding emotional labor and authentic trauma for consumption.


* **"Faking It Until It becomes Real" (State 1 $\rightarrow$ State 2):** Žižek often stresses that ideology is not an illusion masking reality; rather, even when we keep an ironic distance (State 1: the boy smiling because he knows it's just a fake video for views), **the performance itself captures our real emotions** (State 2: the boy actually crying). As Lacan put it, even if a jealous husband’s claims about his wife are pathological fabrications, she might still really be cheating. Here, even though the boy is crying *for a staged TikTok*, the tears in State 2 are real. The symbolic performance colonizes his actual emotional state, or the sheer pressure of being commodified by his own family makes him genuinely miserable.

### 3. The Broader Philosophical Systems at Play

Alongside Žižek’s Hegelian-Lacanian critique, the artist belongs squarely within **Critical Theory, Post-Marxism, and Postmodern Media Philosophy**:

* **Guy Debord and *The Society of the Spectacle*:** Debord famously wrote that *"All that once was directly lived has become mere representation."* In 1952, shame and family tension were directly lived experiences. In the diptych, human emotion is extracted as raw material to be packaged into digital spectacle.


* **Jean Baudrillard’s *Simulacra and Simulation*:** The transition from State 1 to State 2 illustrates the birth of the **hyperreal**. The image inside the smartphone screen (a heartbreaking clip of a sad boy comforted by his dog) is a simulation that replaces domestic reality. By the second panel, the simulation has dictated reality itself: the boy cries real tears to satisfy a fake narrative.


* **Byung-Chul Han’s *Psychopolitics* and the Transparency Society:** Contemporary philosopher Byung-Chul Han argues that late capitalism no longer exploits solely our physical labor (the industrial/Soviet paradigm), but our **emotions, intimacy, and psyche**. We voluntarily turn our private homes into panopticons and exhibition spaces, monetizing vulnerability for digital validation.



---

### 4. How to Explain the Image as a Whole

The diptych is a biting satire of **emotional commodification in the attention economy**.

By hijacking a classic Soviet painting about moral accountability, the artist contrasts two eras of alienation. In the past, the individual was judged by rigid institutional standards (school, state, family honor). Today, those institutions have dissolved into a cooperative content-farm where the family unit exploits its own child's vulnerability for likes.

The tragic irony lies in the transition between the two panels: the dog is the only creature in the room offering unmediated, authentic affection, yet even that pure bond is hijacked by the tripod. By the right panel, whether the boy is crying because he is a brilliant method actor or because he realizes his family only values his pain as "content," the result for the camera is the same—a perfect take.

Do you read the boy's tears in the right panel as successful acting on command, or as genuine breakdown caused by the pressure of the room?
