This looks like a three-panel comic in the style of Ukrainian folk painting, with Petrykivka-style floral borders and naive figures that recall the "Cossack Mamai" folk portraits. It reads as a humorous story about a village tavern and paying the bill.

**Top panel: the tavern.** A whitewashed, thatched khata stands where several roads meet. Its sign reads "У сестер" ("At the Sisters'"), so it's a korchma run by sisters. Outside are the customers' mounts: a saddled horse, a donkey, and a goat loaded with baskets.

**Middle panel: running up the tab.** Four mustachioed men with Cossack-style hair sit at a table with an embroidered cloth, mugs, shot glasses, bread, and a bowl of dumplings. The hostess, in a vyshyvanka, red coral beads, and a headscarf, holds what looks like a tally stick (бирка). Tavern keepers traditionally notched debts onto these. Each man's tab is written above him:

- Panteley: 3 shots of horilka, owes 6 groshi
- Second man: 2 mugs of beer, owes 4 groshi
- Third man: 1 mug of kvass, owes 1 grosh
- Fourth man: 2 shots of vyshnivka (cherry liqueur), owes 8 groshi

The prices work out consistently. Horilka and beer cost 2 groshi each, kvass costs 1, and vyshnivka is the luxury drink at 4 a shot. The whole table owes 19 groshi.

**Bottom panel: settling up.** Panteley, in the same yellow coat, holds out his money pouch. The hostess, now named as Motria Halushko, has a bowl of coins and is biting something, echoing the old habit of biting a coin to check it's genuine. What she's biting looks oversized and tusk-like, which may be part of the joke. Her surname comes from halushky, the dumplings, which fits the bowl on the table. In the window, a three-headed Zmiy, the dragon of Ukrainian folklore, peers in under the caption "Змій чекає на решту" ("The dragon is waiting for his change").

The punchline seems to be that Motria is so tight-fisted, or so slow and suspicious with money, that even a fearsome three-headed dragon has to wait patiently for his change. "Решта" can also mean "the rest" or "what's left," so it might be a double joke: the dragon is waiting for leftovers, or for whatever's left of the customers once Motria is done with them.
