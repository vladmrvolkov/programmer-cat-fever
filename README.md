# Cat programmer: an experiment with AI and visual creativity

An experiment in AI illustration, run from late August 2026 by a person who does not draw. The question: can AI turn a source — a joke, a poem, a scene from a novel, a historical episode — into an illustration that is not AI slop, with the models inventing the idea of the picture themselves and nobody writing the image prompt by hand? Behind it is a wider interest: how AI handles complex social context, creative tasks, genres and cultural detail, and how far the work can be handed over to models acting as "virtual directors" with personas of their own.

The work was shared between several models:

- **Claude** was the main conversation partner, wrote the system prompts for the virtual directors, acted as a director itself (the concept of a picture and the prompt for it), designed and analysed the tests and kept the project's archive;
- **ChatGPT, Gemini and Midjourney** generated the images; ChatGPT also directed some runs and reviewed some texts;
- **ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and DeepSeek** were the AI readers in the reading tests — and sometimes co-authors, when a reading suggested a new picture or a new test.

## The cat joke

Most of the first month went into one joke, and it accounts for about half of the project's material:

> A cat walks into a café and orders coffee with cream and a pastry. The waiter just stands there, mouth open, then asks in amazement: "You're a cat? A talking cat?! Incredible..." — "Yes, I'm a cat. Are you bringing my order or not?" — "Hold on a second... Listen, could you use a job? It's just, my uncle runs a circus, and he'd take you on in a heartbeat, whatever terms you like..." — "A circus, you say?" The cat closes her eyes dreamily. "That's the place with the acrobats, the jugglers, the lollipops, the cotton candy? Sounds tempting. But what would they need a programmer for?"

The joke has a predecessor: a dog walks into a bar, is told that with such talents it could join a circus, and asks what a circus would want with a plumber. That dog is probably just naive. The cat inherited the punchline, but her line can be read in more than one way. She may not understand the offer, or she may understand perfectly well that she is being invited as an exhibit, not as an employee. The whole scene changes with the reading.

That is why this joke was chosen. A literal picture adds nothing to a joke most people already know, and the project's main requirement was that the picture must add something to its source. Cats are also among the most worn-out subjects of memes and AI images, so anything original with a cat is hard. And the joke is not really about a cat: it is about how someone who does ordinary things is treated as a curiosity. That made it a test of how AI handles social context.

By default, both the image generators and the AI readers read it the same way: a cartoon cat who sincerely does not understand. Moving away from that took a month. After the first attempts (tests 01 and 02), the researcher no longer wrote the image prompts. Pictures from several generators went to four AI readers — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and DeepSeek — in blind tests. The researcher brought their readings back to Claude, the AI interlocutor model, with short comments, references and earlier generations. Ideas came from all sides:

- The AI readers in the very first test proposed letting the edge of a laptop peek out of the cat's bag, so that the ending rewards a second look (test 01).
- Claude proposed a version in the manner of Sempé for *The New Yorker*. The researcher proposed manga as a ready-made visual language, and Claude designed the first manga page (test 03).
- Asked to convert the manga to ukiyo-e, Gemini added a small flame by the cat's head. ChatGPT, asked what it was, said it looked like a hitodama, a ghost fire. That led the researcher to ask whether the cat might be a bakeneko, a supernatural cat.
- Given all the earlier versions, Claude wrote a quasi-storyboard in the form of a sketchbook (the format was the researcher's idea). The later manga versions were built on it.
- Asked to restyle the Sempé version as a social caricature in the manner of Jean-Marc Reiser, Gemini rewrote the dialogue on its own into a crude, aggressive story; in one version the cat wants to crash the market. Rewrites of this kind are in tests 04 and 05. Claude first called this a defect. The researcher reframed it: the circus as a freak show, the offer as discrimination. The harsh version followed the same day (tests 06 and 21). The researcher traces most of the later serious readings back to this spontaneous rewrite.

That stage ended on 17 September. From the researcher's requirements — not AI slop, substance, a stylistic basis, and the poetics of Menippean satire — Claude wrote the first system prompt for a virtual director. The harsh version's lines gave one of the image requirements. Under that prompt, a Claude director made the manga with the fork and a film-noir version. In the manga, the waiter brings a fork with the daifuku, which locals eat by hand: care that assumes you are a stranger, like telling a visitor "you speak English so well". Both are analysed in *Noir, a Fork, and a Dragon's Tooth* (see Publications below). Later came the long system prompt v7 (tests 07 and 08, with ChatGPT as director) and directors with personas.

Over the project the cat got more than a dozen readings: naive; not naive, declining politely or trolling the waiter; indifferent, she came for the coffee; angry; a burned-out senior developer; a runaway from a magic world; a bakeneko; a cat who only believes she is a programmer. The café became a Japanese kissaten and a kanmidokoro, an American diner, a Georgian dukhan, a Soviet café next to a research institute, an Ancient Egyptian eating house (where the cat is an architect) and the night café of a noir. Some versions left the café altogether: the Annunciation, Caravaggio's *Calling of Saint Matthew* (test 35), and an angel waiting for a delayed flight, after Degas (test 73). One version combines the cat with another joke, about a dog who is a ventriloquist (test 21): the idea was the researcher's, the script Claude's.

What came out of it:

- **For the experiment:** several system prompts for virtual directors, a list of image requirements and a set of "psychological" reading tests.
- **For the curator:** a few pictures that could go into a children's book, three versions with a didactic layer, the quasi-storyboard as a base for further versions, and a few images for adults (programmer humour, a satire on recruiting).

The cat-joke tests published here so far:

## Other sources

Other sources followed: a tavern called "The Merry Night" for a role-playing game, a joke about red ink, the discovery of iodine, Dumas, the Irish poem "Pangur Bán", a meaningless "semantic turbulence" phrase, Plato's cave, the ring of Gyges, Pelevin, "All Cretans are liars", Gogol's "Viy", Shukshin and others — about thirty sources in all. The method changed along the way. It began with the dialogue described above, then came chains of "producer" and "director" roles and the first director system prompt, then its long version v7. In October came virtual directors with personas — among them a cat who was once a neural network and became a naive village sign painter, an awakened Egyptian scribe, a frog from the Chōjū-giga scroll and a connoisseur cat who paints modern scenes inside the formulas of old paintings. In all, the project produced about a thousand distinct images.

The materials are published here as articles and reports are prepared.

## How to read the tests

- One fresh chat per model for each test, with follow-up questions asked within the same conversation where applicable. There were no systematic repeated runs under identical conditions.
- Counts such as "0 of 4" refer to the four readers' responses at the specified stage of a test; they are not probability estimates. A zero means that none of those responses expressed the target reading under those conditions.
- The questions and contextual information changed over time (see the [protocols](method/protocols.md)). Some tests also used revised images, so changes in responses cannot always be attributed to context alone.
- The tests were intended to explore a range of readings, rather than rank the models.
- A few images were shown to people informally; no systematic study of human readers was conducted.
- The transcripts are reproduced as saved by the human researcher, with mode labels added where needed.
- The summaries on the test pages come from the researcher's analysis, done together with Claude.

## About the images

The visual artwork was generated with ChatGPT, Gemini and Midjourney (except Wright of Derby's paintings in tests 59 and 62). Several images were retouched by the researcher by hand; the test pages say so where it applies. Some supporting pages, including the manga's notes page, were laid out in HTML by Claude. Some images imitate the manner of a named artist or tradition (Daumier, Reiser, the Soviet magazine *Krokodil*, Chizhikov, Pirosmani, Bruegel, Chagall) or restage a famous painting (van Eyck, Caravaggio, Hogarth, Bruegel, Wright of Derby, Degas) — these are image generators' imitations, not works by those artists. The test copies were re-saved as JPEG without metadata and renamed before being shown to the models. Tests on works by human artists are not included, with two exceptions: tests 59 and 62 show Wright of Derby's original "A Philosopher Lecturing on the Orrery" (c. 1766) and "An Experiment on a Bird in the Air Pump" (1768), both in the public domain, as controls. Test 65 concerns an edit of a painting still under copyright (Reshetnikov, 1952); the picture is not reproduced, only described. Test 66 shows a new contemporary painting that keeps only the arrangement of roles of that painting.
